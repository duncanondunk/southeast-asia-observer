# 衣冠为媒：汉服与娘惹可峇雅在马六甲同台，民间文旅再升温

2026年9月19日，一场名为"2026马来西亚礼乐大会暨中马传统服饰交流活动"的展演，在世界文化遗产地马六甲鸡场街拉开帷幕。活动由马六甲鸡场街工委会、马中民俗文旅总商会与福建汉服天下联合主办，吸引来自中国、加拿大、法国、希腊等海内外三十余家文化团体参与，海丝文化会馆也同期落地启用。现场，华夏汉服、娘惹可峇雅（Nyonya kebaya）与马来西亚各族传统服饰同台展演，开幕式服饰秀《华裳启序》将华夏礼乐意象与南洋衣冠美学熔于一炉。这看似一场轻柔的文旅秀，却恰是观察中马关系"软地基"的一扇窗口。

## 以服为媒：一场文明互鉴的展演

据主办方介绍，活动聚焦中马传统服饰的文化对话。开幕式上，汉服展演、娘惹舞蹈、传统器乐与中式茶礼轮番登场；在百年会馆里，昏礼、射礼、拜妈祖等沉浸式礼乐展示依次铺开，参与者身着传统服饰在鸡场街古巷旅拍、泛舟马六甲运河，一条"流动的衣冠风景线"由此生成。马六甲历史城区（鸡场街）工委会主席颜天禄说得直白："服饰是穿在身上的文明。"当汉服、峇峇娘惹可峇雅与马来传统服饰在同一座世界文化遗产古城同台绽放，本身就是一幅文明互鉴的画卷。

这话不算夸张。服饰之所以动人，正因为它把抽象的"文化"缝进了可触可感的日常。比起宏大的联合声明，一套衣冠、一段礼乐，更能让普通人对"另一种文明"生出亲切。这也正是民间文旅最朴素的逻辑——以美为桥，先相知，再相交。

## 娘惹可峇雅：华人离散的"混血"衣冠

本文更想点出的是：这场展演里最耐人寻味的，恰恰是"娘惹可峇雅"这一件衣。所谓峇峇娘惹（Baba-Nyonya），是明清以降南下南洋的华人与马来、印尼原住民族通婚形成的土生社群，其女性传统上衣可峇雅，正是中西布料、南洋剪裁与中华刺绣的混血产物。换句话说，这件衣本身就是华人离散（diaspora）在地的活标本——它既非纯粹的中国，也非纯粹的马来，而是两种文明在漫长岁月里彼此借光的结果。

这恰是"华侨华人"议题最深的一层：离散群体的文化从不是母国的简单复制，而是在异乡长出的新枝。今天我们在马六甲看到的，不是"汉服走出去"的单向输出，而是汉服与娘惹衣冠在同一舞台上的彼此映照。若只把它读成"中华文化出海"的又一个注脚，反倒窄化了它的意义——它真正讲述的，是华人如何在他乡把异质文明织进自己的衣襟。

## 马六甲：海上丝路早已织就的十字路口

为何是马六甲？这座扼守马六甲海峡的古城，自十五世纪起便是海上丝绸之路的枢纽，郑和船队曾数度驻节于此；后来葡、荷、英殖民者相继到来，华人、马来人、印度人、波斯人于此交汇。今天的鸡场街，红瓦骑楼与宗乡会馆比邻，本身就是一部活着的多文明共存史。

因此，把"中马传统服饰对话"放在马六甲，几乎是一种自然的选择——这里从来不是两种文明初次相遇的边境，而是多种文明早已彼此渗透的腹地。主办方说这是"文明互鉴"，但更准确地说，是让一种本就存在的融合被重新看见、被郑重对待。比起在全新空间里"制造"对话，马六甲的优势在于：对话的底稿，早已写好在它的街巷与衣冠之中。

## 不只有情怀：文旅IP与经济账

若是以为这只是一场情怀秀，便小看了主办方的算盘。同期举行的"中马文化产业对接交流会"，议题直指非遗活化、文旅开发与服饰IP运营。换句话说，从汉服巡游到娘惹可峇雅，要被沉淀为可消费的文旅资产与可运营的知识产权。据媒体报道，疫后中国与马来西亚人员往来快速回暖，马来西亚亦借对华免签等政策大力招揽中国游客。把"传统服饰"做成城市名片，本质是给马六甲——这座早已被中国游客熟知的古城——再添一道记忆点。

这一层逻辑并不庸俗。文化若要"走出去"且走得远，终究要落到产业与就业上。当一套衣冠能带动一条街的旅拍、一家店的文创、一场城的节庆，文明的互鉴才算接了地气。只是要提醒一句：知识产权运营最忌竭泽而渔，若把文化符号过度商品化，反倒会稀释其本真。马六甲的经验，值得后来者细看。

## 民间相知，撑得起大外交吗？

最后要问一个清醒的问题：这样一场民间文旅秀，究竟能在中马关系里撑起多大分量？答案是——它是"软地基"，却不是"承重墙"。

大国博弈的叙事往往占据头条：美国"印太"框架下的防务合作、马来西亚在美中之间的"中间道路"、安华政府的经济平衡术。相较之下，鸡场街上的衣冠风景线显得轻盈。但它自有其不可替代的价值。中马关系的韧性，很大一部分正建筑在这样的民间相知之上——华人社群作为两国之间的天然人桥，使文化往来比政治议程更不易被风向左右。一个在马六甲穿过汉服、尝过娘惹菜的年轻人，日后看待两国关系时，大概率会比只读头条的人多一分温度。

当然，我们也要避免浪漫化。民间交流再热，也替代不了贸易结构、南海议程与战略互信等硬议题。它的作用更像"储备金"：平日不显山露水，危机来临时却能兑出一份善意。马六甲这场衣冠之约提醒我们：大国外交的高低，不只写在峰会公报里，也缝在普通人衣襟的针脚中。

## 延伸阅读

- **峇峇娘惹（Baba-Nyonya / Peranakan）**：明清以来南下南洋的华人与马来、印尼原住民族通婚形成的土生华人社群，其衣冠、饮食与语言均为中西文化交融的产物，是华人离散在地化的典型样本。
- **海上丝绸之路（Maritime Silk Road）**：古代中国与东南亚、南亚、阿拉伯世界海上贸易与文明交往的通道，马六甲因其扼守海峡而成为关键枢纽。
- **公共外交（Public Diplomacy）**：国家以文化与信息手段塑造外国公众认知的实践，区别于传统的国家间外交，本文所述民间文旅即其一端。

# Costumes as Common Ground: Hanfu and the Nyonya Kebaya Meet in Malacca

On 19 September 2026, a pageant billed as the "2026 Malaysia Ritual Music Congress and China–Malaysia Traditional Costume Exchange" opened on Jonker Street in Malacca, a UNESCO World Heritage site. Co-hosted by the Malacca Jonker Street Committee, the Malaysia–China Folklore, Culture and Tourism Chamber of Commerce, and Fujian Hanfu Tianxia, the event drew more than thirty cultural troupes from China, Canada, France, Greece and beyond; a "Maritime Silk Road Culture Hall" was inaugurated alongside it. On stage, Hanfu, the Nyonya kebaya and the traditional dress of Malaysia's diverse communities shared the same runway; the opening show, "Hua Shang Qi Xu" (The Awakening of Splendid Attire), fused the imagery of Chinese ritual music with the sartorial aesthetics of the Nanyang, or "Southern Ocean." What looks at first like a gentle cultural-tourism spectacle is, on closer reading, a small but telling window onto the soft underlay of China–Malaysia relations.

## Garments as a medium: a pageant of mutual learning

According to the organisers, the event centred on a cultural dialogue between Chinese and Malaysian traditional dress. The opening ceremony staged Hanfu displays, Nyonya dance, traditional instrumental music and a Chinese tea ritual; inside a century-old guild hall, immersive rites — a wedding rite, an archery rite, and the worship of the sea goddess Mazu — were performed in sequence. Participants, dressed in traditional attire, posed for travel photographs in the alleys of Jonker Street and boated along Malacca's canals, producing what organisers called "a flowing landscape of attire." Gan Tianlu, chairman of the Malacca Historical City (Jonker Street) Committee, put it plainly: "Costume is civilisation worn on the body." When Hanfu, the Baba-Nyonya kebaya and Malay dress bloom together in the same heritage city, he argued, the result is itself a moving tableau of mutual learning among civilisations.

The claim is not mere rhetoric. The power of dress lies precisely in its ability to stitch abstraction — "culture" — into something tactile and daily. Compared with a grand joint communiqué, a single garment or a passage of ritual music is far more likely to make an ordinary person feel warmth toward "another civilisation." This is the most elemental logic of people-to-people cultural tourism: let beauty be the bridge — first knowing, then bonding.

## The Nyonya kebaya: a creole garment of the Chinese diaspora

What this essay wishes to underline is that the most intriguing exhibit on that runway was, in fact, the Nyonya kebaya itself. The Baba-Nyonya (or Peranakan) are the creole communities formed by Chinese migrants who, from the Ming and Qing dynasties onward, settled in the Nanyang and intermarried with Malay and Indonesian populations. The kebaya worn by Peranakan women is itself a "mixed-blood" garment — Western and Chinese fabrics, Nanyang tailoring, and Chinese embroidery braided into one. In other words, this piece of clothing is a living specimen of the Chinese diaspora's local adaptation: neither purely Chinese nor purely Malay, but the fruit of two civilisations borrowing light from each other across centuries.

Here lies the deepest layer of the "overseas Chinese" question. The culture of diaspora communities was never a simple copy of the motherland; it is a new branch grown on foreign soil. What we see in Malacca today is not a one-way "export of Hanfu," but Hanfu and the Nyonya garment reflecting one another on the same stage. To read it merely as another footnote to "Chinese culture going global" would actually narrow its meaning. What it truly tells is how the Chinese, far from home, wove an alien civilisation into the very lapels of their own dress.

## Malacca: a maritime-Silk-Road crossroads already woven

Why Malacca? This ancient city, guarding the strait of the same name, has been a hub of the maritime Silk Road since the fifteenth century; Zheng He's fleets anchored here on several of their voyages. Later came Portuguese, Dutch and British colonists, and with them Chinese, Malay, Indian and Persian communities intermingled. Today's Jonker Street, with its red-tiled shophouses and clan associations side by side, is itself a living history of multi-civilisational coexistence.

To stage a "China–Malaysia traditional costume dialogue" in Malacca is therefore almost a natural choice — this was never a frontier where two civilisations first met, but a heartland where several had long since permeated one another. The organisers call it "mutual learning among civilisations"; more precisely, it lets an already-existing fusion be seen again, and taken seriously. Compared with "manufacturing" dialogue in a brand-new space, Malacca's advantage is that the draft of that dialogue was already written into its streets and its attire.

## More than sentiment: cultural-tourism IP and the hard numbers

To mistake this for a mere sentimental show would be to underestimate the organisers' calculus. A parallel "China–Malaysia Cultural Industry Matchmaking Session" addressed, with unusual directness, the revitalisation of intangible heritage, cultural-tourism development, and the operation of costume IP. In other words, everything from the Hanfu parade to the Nyonya kebaya is to be distilled into consumable tourism assets and operable intellectual property. Media reports note that people-to-people exchanges between China and Malaysia have rebounded quickly since the pandemic, and Malaysia has courted Chinese visitors through measures including visa-free entry. Turning "traditional costume" into a city brand is, at bottom, a way to add one more memory point to Malacca — a heritage town already familiar to Chinese travellers.

This logic is not vulgar. For culture to "go out" and travel far, it must ultimately land on industry and employment. When a single garment can animate a street's travel photography, a shop's cultural products, and a city's festival, the mutual learning of civilisations finally touches the ground. One caveat, though: IP operation at its worst drains the pond to catch the fish; over-commercialising cultural symbols dilutes their authenticity. Malacca's experience is worth studying closely by those who follow.

## Can people-to-people ties carry the weight of statecraft?

A sober question remains: how much weight can such a people-to-people spectacle actually bear within the China–Malaysia relationship? The answer is that it is the "soft foundation," not the "load-bearing wall."

The headlines are monopolised by great-power narratives — U.S. defence cooperation under the "Indo-Pacific" framework, Malaysia's "middle path" between Washington and Beijing, the economic balancing act of Anwar Ibrahim's government. Against that backdrop, the sartorial scenery on Jonker Street looks weightless. Yet it has an irreplaceable value of its own. Much of the resilience of China–Malaysia relations is built precisely on this kind of grassroots acquaintance — the ethnic-Chinese community serves as a natural human bridge between the two countries, making cultural exchange harder to sway with the political wind than formal agendas. A young Malaysian who has tried on Hanfu and tasted Nyonya cuisine in Malacca will, when judging the bilateral relationship in later years, likely carry one degree more warmth than someone who reads only the headlines.

Of course, we must resist romanticising. No amount of popular exchange can substitute for the hard issues — trade structure, the South China Sea agenda, strategic mutual trust. Its function is more like a "reserve fund": invisible in calm weather, yet cashing out as goodwill when a crisis arrives. The sartorial covenant in Malacca reminds us that the highs and lows of great-power diplomacy are written not only in summit communiqués, but also in the stitching on an ordinary person's collar.

## Further reading

- **Baba-Nyonya / Peranakan**: Creole communities descended from Chinese migrants who settled in the Nanyang from the Ming–Qing era and intermarried with Malay and Indonesian populations; their dress, cuisine and language are products of Chinese–local fusion, a classic specimen of diaspora localisation.
- **Maritime Silk Road**: The ancient sea corridor linking China with Southeast Asia, South Asia and the Arab world for trade and civilisational exchange; Malacca became a key hub by virtue of its command of the strait.
- **Public diplomacy**: A state's use of cultural and informational means to shape foreign publics' perceptions, distinct from traditional state-to-state diplomacy; the people-to-people cultural tourism described here is one expression of it.
