在曼谷的晚霞与工厂的烟囱之间，泰国正试图重写自己的能源剧本。一份把清洁能源发电占比拉高到接近九成的中期蓝图，把这个长期依赖天然气发电的"气电王国"，推到了转型的十字路口。

## 一份被重新校准的蓝图

泰国是全球少有的高度依赖天然气发电的国家，天然气在其发电结构中长期占据六成以上。据日经亚洲报道，泰国在新版电力发展规划中大幅调高清洁能源目标，提出到二〇五〇年清洁能源发电占比接近九成（约89%），并以此重塑电源结构。这份蓝图不再把天然气与煤炭当作理所当然的"压舱石"，而是把太阳能、风电与储能推到前台。

## 为什么是"接近九成"

泰国并非凭空立靶。一方面，气候承诺与净零压力逼近，东盟国家纷纷上调可再生能源野心；另一方面，过度依赖进口天然气让泰国能源安全脆弱——国际气价波动直接传导至电价。把清洁能源占比抬到接近九成，既是减碳题中之义，也是对冲进口依赖的算术题。对正奋力打造电动汽车与电池产业链的泰国而言，绿色电力还关乎制造业的"含碳量"与国际竞争力。

## 野心之下的现实裂隙

蓝图很美，裂缝也在。泰国目前的清洁电力基础薄弱，距九成目标隔着巨大的装机与电网鸿沟；天然气电厂已深度绑定既得利益与基荷供电，退场绝非一日之功。更要紧的是钱：能源转型预计需逾三万亿泰铢（约六千亿元人民币）投资，电网扩容、储能与调度能力都得同步跟上。在财政与电价承受力之间，泰国政府要在"绿色"与"可负担"间走钢丝。

## 东盟能源转型的样板与困局

泰国的纠结，是东盟的缩影。区域内国家一边签下气候承诺，一边仍离不开煤电与气电保供；一边招商引资绿色产业，一边苦于电网老旧、融资昂贵。泰国若真能把蓝图落进现实，将给越南、印尼、菲律宾提供可参照的"渐进脱碳"样本；若停留在纸面，则再次印证东盟转型"承诺易、落地难"的老题。

## 一场关于"公平转型"的镜鉴

泰国的尝试提醒我们：能源转型从不是简单的"换电源"，而是一场牵涉安全、成本与公平的系统重排。当"接近九成"从口号走向工地，真正考验的不是目标够不够高，而是电网够不够韧、资金够不够稳、普通人的电价够不够暖。清洁能源的尽头，终究是人的生计。

## 延伸阅读

**电力发展规划（Power Development Plan）**：泰国政府制定的中长期电源与电网建设纲领，决定各类能源的发电占比与投资优先级。

**净零（Net Zero）**：温室气体排放与吸收相互抵消的中长期气候目标，多国承诺于本世纪中叶前后实现。

**基荷供电（Baseload power）**：电网中持续、稳定输出以满足基本需求的电力，传统上由煤电、气电等可调度电源承担。

In Bangkok's dusk between temple spires and factory chimneys, Thailand is rewriting its energy script. A mid-century blueprint lifting clean-power share toward nearly 90% pushes this "kingdom of gas" to a crossroads of transition.

## A Blueprint Recalculated

Thailand is unusually reliant on natural gas for power, which has long supplied over 60% of its generation. According to Nikkei Asia, Thailand's revised Power Development Plan sharply raises its clean-energy goal, targeting nearly 90% (about 89%) clean power by 2050 and reshaping its generation mix. The plan no longer treats gas and coal as default ballast, pushing solar, wind and storage to the front.

## Why "Nearly 90%"

Thailand is not setting the target in a vacuum. Climate pledges and net-zero pressure are closing in, and ASEAN peers are lifting renewable ambitions. More concretely, heavy reliance on imported gas leaves Thailand's energy security fragile—global price swings hit household bills directly. Lifting clean power toward 90% is both a decarbonisation imperative and an arithmetic hedge against import dependence. For a country betting on EV and battery supply chains, green electricity also decides the "carbon content" and competitiveness of its manufacturing.

## The Cracks Beneath the Ambition

The blueprint is beautiful; the cracks are real. Thailand's clean-power base is thin, separated from 90% by a vast gap in capacity and grid. Gas plants are deeply tied to vested interests and baseload supply; their exit will not happen overnight. Money is the bigger knot: the transition is expected to need over 3 trillion baht (about US$85 billion), with grid expansion, storage and dispatch all needing to keep pace. Between fiscal room and tariff affordability, the government walks a tightrope of "green vs. affordable."

## A Template and a Trap for ASEAN

Thailand's dilemma mirrors ASEAN's. The region signs climate pledges yet leans on coal and gas for security; it courts green industry yet struggles with ageing grids and costly finance. If Thailand turns blueprint into reality, it offers Vietnam, Indonesia and the Philippines a reference for "gradual decarbonisation"; if it stays on paper, it reconfirms the old ASEAN story—easy to promise, hard to deliver.

## A Lesson on "Just Transition"

Thailand's attempt reminds us: energy transition is never a simple swap of sources, but a systemic reordering of security, cost and fairness. As "nearly 90%" moves from slogan to construction site, the real test is not how high the target sits, but how resilient the grid is, how stable the funding, how warm the tariff for ordinary people. The end of clean energy is, ultimately, people's livelihoods.

**Power Development Plan**: Thailand's mid-to-long-term blueprint for generation and grid, deciding each energy's share and investment priority.

**Net Zero**: A climate goal where greenhouse-gas emissions and removal cancel out, targeted by many around mid-century.

**Baseload power**: Steady, continuous electricity meeting basic demand, traditionally supplied by dispatchable coal or gas.
