# 曼谷又淹了：一场"洪灾假日"照出的，是中等收入国家的治水无力

## 曼谷又淹了

泰国首都又沉到水里了。2026年9月下旬的一个周末，曼谷在三日之内落下约320毫米降水，几乎等于这座城市整个9月的平均雨量。排水网络当场"缴械"：从邦甲比到东部郊区，街区积水齐腰，小贩把摊位架在塑料箱和油桶上漂浮售卖，政府则做了一件现代经济体本不该做的事——宣布为期两天的"洪灾假日"，让曼谷及周边三府的政府机关与学校停摆。超过5900人住进临时安置中心。一座常住人口逾千万的超级都市，被水按下了暂停键。这画面不是韧性，是认输。

## 数字：三天下完一个月的雨

账本触目惊心。自9月16日以来，洪灾波及41个府，至少8人遇难，180余万人受灾，受损房屋超过58万间。仅曼谷一地，约32.9万户、70多万人受灾。泰国商会大学测算，全国经济损失超过110亿泰铢（约23亿元人民币），其中首都约占70亿泰铢。兰实大学经济学者披桑瓦尼（Aat Pisanwanich）警告，洪水可能把全年经济增速拉低约0.3个百分点、降至2.1%，并把第三季度产出砍掉约0.5个百分点。孤立地看，这些数字算不上灾难；放在语境里，却是灾难——它们压上的是一个增速仅勉强两成的经济体。

## 为什么曼谷总在溺水

曼谷本就是一座为洪水而建的城市。它坐落在软黏土冲积三角洲上，平均海拔仅1.5米，且因抽取地下水、混凝土重压，每年还在以数毫米的速度下沉。当一场被强厄尔尼诺放大的季风在七十二小时内倒下一整月的雨，再多的抽水机也保不住这座碗状下沉的都会。2011年的那场大洪水夺走八百余人生命、造成数百亿美元损失，本应是一记警钟。十五年过去，首都依赖的仍是同一套临时拼凑：沙袋、船只、流动厨房。气候变化没有被规划应对，只是被一年又一年硬挺过去。

## 经济时点：复苏刚露头，洪水先出手

泰国是以"掉队者"身份进入这个汛期的。第二季度增速仅1.9%，低于首季度的2.8%，明显落后于东南亚同伴。总理阿努廷把复苏押在中国资本与投资促进委员会（泰国投资委员会）的快速通道上；世界银行也才刚为该国画出2037年跻身高收入国家的路线图。然后，水来了。把全国最大经济中心关停两天——而这里的贸易与服务恰恰靠人到场——本身就是一种增长税。洪水没有造成泰国的放缓，它照出的，是这个国家扛住一次冲击的余地有多薄。

## 日本企业的弦绷得最紧

走到被淹的东部工业带，就能明白东京为何把泰国的洪灾报道当作资产负债表来读。本田（Honda）与泰国国际航空均报告运营受扰；罗勇府——东部经济走廊（泰国东部经济走廊）的核心、日系汽车与电子工厂的聚集地——在两座水库超容量后下令撤离。日本是泰国最大的外来投资者，其汽车、零部件、硬盘等供应链，正从这些被淹的府穿行而过。对东京而言，曼谷的洪水从来不只是天气，而是一行损益。正因如此，日本银行与制造商关注泰国降雨的强度，堪比别国盯住利率决议。

## 穷人的水，富人的假

在邦甲比，小贩站在齐膝的水里、挪到市场边缘摆摊，因为顾客不肯蹚水进来。政府的纾困承诺——为撤离者一日三餐、覆盖整个汛期再加退水后的七天——值得肯定，但它惠及的是登记在册者，而非非正规者。日薪劳工与小经营者，水一涨就断了收入，偏偏这两天的"假日"不补偿他们。一场让正规经济暂停、却让非正规经济更困顿的灾害，不是一视同仁的冲击，而是一面放大镜，照出泰国尚未织就的社会保障网。

## 区域气候压力测试

泰国的洪水不是孤立发生的。同一周，老挝在出口大半电力之际仍频繁断电，柬埔寨正办起反电诈大会，越南则逼近与美国达成贸易协定。东南亚那份"发展红利"——稳步脱贫、跻身中等收入——正在被气候冲击与治理缺口从边际上蚕食，而这是任何一个部委都单凭自己修不好的。区域领导人把"韧性"说得流利，季风却对修辞无动于衷，照旧送来账单。

## 结语：水泵救不了下沉的城市

曼谷会退水，像往常一样，一两周内。部长们会巡视干街、宣布再多买几台泵、把教训归档。但一座在海水与天空同时上涨中下沉的城市，单靠硬件工程回不到安全线。除非泰国把地面沉降、排水与加剧的季风当作长期常量而非年度意外来对待，否则每个雨季都是一次掷骰——而庄家，越来越常是水。

## 延伸阅读

**东部经济走廊（Eastern Economic Corridor）**：泰国在东部沿海打造的先进制造与基建走廊，日系汽车与电子产业聚集，也是本次洪灾中供应链风险最高的区域。

**强厄尔尼诺（El Niño）**：太平洋海温异常偏暖现象，会放大东南亚季风的降水与干旱，是近年区域极端天气频发的重要气候背景。

**地面沉降（Land Subsidence）**：因超采地下水与城市荷载导致的地表下沉，曼谷每年下沉数毫米，是其"易涝体质"的结构性根源。

# Bangkok Underwater Again: A 'Flood Holiday' That Exposes a Middle-Income Nation's Powerlessness Over Water

## Bangkok is underwater again

Once more, Thailand's capital has gone under. Over a single weekend in late September 2026, roughly 320 millimetres of rain fell on Bangkok — almost the city's entire average September rainfall in three days. The drainage network simply gave up. Neighbourhoods from Bang Kapi to the eastern suburbs stood waist-deep in water, vendors floated their stalls on plastic boxes, and the government did what a modern economy is not supposed to do: it declared a two-day "flood holiday," shutting government offices and schools across Bangkok and three surrounding provinces. More than 5,900 people moved into evacuation centres. The spectacle of a megacity of over ten million people being paused by water is not resilience. It is surrender.

## The numbers: a month's rain in three days

The tally is stark. Since 16 September, floods have hit 41 provinces, killed at least eight people, and affected more than 1.8 million residents; over 580,000 homes have been damaged. In Bangkok alone, some 329,000 households — more than 700,000 people — were hit. Economists at the University of the Thai Chamber of Commerce put nationwide damage at over 11 billion baht (about US$327 million), with the capital accounting for roughly 7 billion. Rangsit University's Aat Pisanwanich warned the floods could shave about 0.3 percentage points off full-year growth, dragging it to 2.1%, and cut third-quarter output by around 0.5 points. These are not catastrophic numbers in isolation. They are catastrophic in context: they land on an economy already growing at barely 2 per cent.

## Why Bangkok keeps drowning

Bangkok is a city built to flood. It sits on a soft-clay river delta barely 1.5 metres above sea level and is sinking several millimetres a year as groundwater is extracted and the weight of concrete bears down. When a super-El Niño-intensified monsoon dumps a month of rain in seventy-two hours, no quantity of pumps can keep a bowl-shaped, subsiding metropolis dry. The 2011 great flood — which killed more than 800 people and caused tens of billions of dollars in damage — was supposed to be the wake-up call. Fifteen years on, the capital still relies on the same improvisation: sandbags, boats, and mobile kitchens. Climate change has not been planned for; it has merely been survived, season after season.

## Timing: a fragile recovery meets the flood

Thailand entered this flood season as the region's laggard. Second-quarter growth came in at just 1.9 per cent, down from 2.8 per cent in the first quarter, well behind its Southeast Asian peers. Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul has been betting the recovery on Chinese capital and a fast-track investment board; the World Bank only recently sketched a 2037 high-income roadmap for the country. Then the water arrived. A two-day shutdown of the country's largest economic centre — where trade and services depend on people showing up — is itself a growth tax. The flood did not cause Thailand's slowdown. It exposed how little margin the country had to absorb one.

## Japan Inc. is watching the closest

Walk the flooded eastern belt and you find why Tokyo reads Thai flood reports like a balance sheet. Honda and Thai Airways both reported disruption; Rayong province, heart of the Eastern Economic Corridor and home to Japanese auto and electronics plants, ordered evacuations as two reservoirs overtopped. Japan is Thailand's largest foreign investor, and its supply chains — cars, parts, hard disks — run straight through the provinces now underwater. For Tokyo, a Bangkok flood is never just weather. It is a line item. That is precisely why Japanese banks and manufacturers track Thai rainfall with the intensity others reserve for interest-rate decisions.

## The poor stand in the water; the rich get a holiday

In Bang Kapi, vendors stood knee-deep to sell essentials from the market's edge, because customers would not wade in. The government's relief pledge — three meals a day for evacuees, for the flooding period plus seven days after — is welcome, but it reaches the registered, not the irregular. Daily-wage workers and small traders, who lose income the moment the water rises, are precisely the people the two-day holiday does not compensate. A disaster that pauses the formal economy while it immiserates the informal one is not an equal-opportunity shock. It is a magnifying glass held over Thailand's unfinished social protection.

## A regional climate stress test

Thailand's flood did not happen in a vacuum. The same week, Laos was blacking out despite exporting most of its power, Cambodia was staging its anti-scam conference, and Vietnam was closing in on a U.S. trade deal. Southeast Asia's development dividend — the steady climb out of poverty and into middle-income status — is being eaten at the margins by climate shocks and governance gaps that no single ministry can fix. The region's leaders speak fluently of "resilience." The monsoon, indifferent to rhetoric, keeps sending the invoice.

## Closing: pumps cannot save a sinking city

Bangkok will drain, as it always does, in a week or two. Ministers will tour the dry streets, announce a few more pumps, and file the lesson. But a city that sinks while the seas and the skies both rise cannot be engineered back to safety with hardware alone. Until Thailand reckons with land subsidence, drainage, and the intensifying monsoon as a permanent condition rather than an annual surprise, every rainy season is a roll of the dice — and the house, increasingly, is underwater.

## Further reading

**Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC):** Thailand's advanced-manufacturing and infrastructure corridor along the eastern coast, where Japanese auto and electronics supply chains cluster — and where flood supply-chain risk is now highest.

**El Niño:** The Pacific warming anomaly that amplifies Southeast Asia's monsoon rainfall and droughts, the climate backdrop behind the region's intensifying extreme weather.

**Land subsidence:** Sinking of the ground surface from groundwater over-extraction and urban load; Bangkok drops several millimetres a year, the structural root of its flood-prone physique.
