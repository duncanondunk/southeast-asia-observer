# 「我很害怕」：人工智能如何冲击东南亚应届生就业

从曼谷到新加坡，一种安静的恐惧正在这个地区的年轻求职者中间弥漫。据《南华早报》2026年10月31日报道，生成式人工智能（Generative AI）已经开始收割东南亚各地的入门级岗位，新加坡、泰国、马来西亚的应届生失业率都在抬头。报道中那句「我很害怕」，并不是无病呻吟，而是一代人突然意识到：自己还没踏上职场，梯子最底下的几级就已经被抽走了。这当然不只是又一个科技故事，它更像是一次压力测试——测试这个地区的经济究竟还能不能接住它的年轻人。

## 一句「我很害怕」，戳破体面幻觉

东南亚长期以来把自己——也被外人——包装成一个人口红利的甜点区：年轻、廉价、肯干。二十年来，主流叙事一直是外资与工厂线每年会吸纳数以百万计的新劳动力。而《南华早报》这篇报道，恰恰戳破了这个安慰。它所记录的不安，并不发生在流水线工人身上，而发生在西装革履的大学毕业生中间——恰恰是那些被地区吹鼓手拿来证明「亚洲世纪到来」的人群。当最有学历的年轻人都说自己害怕，那个「就业无限吸纳」的乐观叙事就开始显得单薄了。连新加坡总理黄循财都不得不承认，人工智能已经「打破了职场的底层一级」——一个建立在精英向上流动之上的政府，说出这句话的分量，可想而知。

## 被瞄准的，是「最底层一级」

这一轮冲击之所以特别，在于落点的精准。自动化并没有从顶端开始，而是先啃食最底层那一级——分析岗实习生、初级文案、第一年研究员、后台处理员，这些岗位的任务最容易被编码、最容易交给模型。这些工作的存在，本是为了训练人，而不是为了产出最大化。把它们抽走，失去的不只是岗位，更是入场坡道。一段被抽掉头三级的梯子，根本算不上梯子。「我很害怕」这句话，就是人们眼睁睁看着入场坡道在自己抵达之前被拆除时的声音。在马来西亚，市场营销专业的毕业生发现，过去雇他们来写的文案和做的图，如今人工智能几下就生成了；泰国开泰研究中心（Kasikorn Research Centre）也指出，企业不再愿意为新鲜毕业生培训那些「第一步」的工作，因为机器做得更快更便宜。

## 最需要就业，却最经不起冲击

这里的地区性反讽再鲜明不过。东南亚恰恰是全世界最需要一个大规模入门就业的地方——人口年轻、保障网单薄、社会契约很大程度押在「读书就能找到工作」的承诺上。然而，它偏偏又是应对这类岗位冲击能力最弱的地区。与那些可以倚仗失业保险、再培训预算或密集服务业的富裕经济体不同，许多东南亚国家只能押注「数量」：足够多的新岗位、足够快地出现，才能维持住这份契约。如果人工智能把最底层的岗位变薄，这道算术题很快就会变得残酷。数据已经在低声预警：新加坡求职毕业生在半年内就业的比例，从2024年的87.1%滑落到2025年的83.4%；泰国失业者中约44%是20至24岁的毕业生；印尼的青年失业率则在13%上下徘徊。

## 文凭不再保值：教育的回报被重估

更深的不安，是关于「价格」。对于一个把一代人积蓄砸进一张文凭的菲律宾或印尼家庭来说，这张证书一直像一种保值资产——赌的是教育能兑换成一份稳定薪水。而人工智能正在打断这种兑换。当一台机器就能起草报告、归纳案情、生成提案初稿时，普通文凭的溢价就被侵蚀，剩下的要么是最顶尖的精英认证，要么是最狭窄的技术本领。《南华早报》这篇报道，实际上是一则悄然的讣告：它宣告「随便一张文凭、在哪里都管用」的假设正在失效。那些把社会流动押在大众高等教育之上的人，如今才发现这项资产也会折旧。国际劳工组织（ILO）估计，近8000万东南亚劳动者——超过地区劳动力五分之一——将被人工智能波及，其中1170万可能直接失去工作，或岗位任务被彻底改写。

## 慌没有用，但装睡更危险

这当然不值得恐慌，技术乐观派的回击——旧岗位消失后新岗位终会出现——在长期看也并非全错。但「长期」恰恰是这一代东南亚年轻人今年无福消受的奢侈。有用的回应不是否认，而是设计：把人工智能当作一个需要管理的劳动力事件、而不是一个值得庆祝的生产力礼物来对待。新加坡的应对——补贴实习、砸下10亿新元（约54亿元人民币）的技能提升计划——算是个开头；但更难的那道题是教育：那些仍在批量生产「错误技能」的课程体系，必须来一次坦诚清算。东南亚最大的优势从来都是人。如果自动化瞄准的正是那一级「入场台阶」，保护它就不是煽情，而正是增长故事本身的核心。

## 延伸阅读

**生成式人工智能（Generative AI）**：能依据提示词生成文本、代码或图像的AI系统；正是这类工具正渗入入门级办公室工作，替代起草、归纳与初稿。

**职场两极化（Job polarization）**：自动化倾向于掏空中低技能常规岗位、同时保留体力与高度创造性岗位的现象；本轮冲击正是它的东南亚版本。

**劳动力吸纳（Labour force absorption）**：经济体将新增求职者转化为在业劳动者的能力——正是当下被人工智能压缩的那项指标。

# "I'm terrified": How Artificial Intelligence Is Reshaping Southeast Asia's Graduate Job Market

From Bangkok to Singapore, a quiet dread is settling over the region's young jobseekers. The South China Morning Post reported on 31 October 2026 that generative AI is already scything through entry-level roles across Southeast Asia, with Singapore, Thailand, and Malaysia all showing rising graduate unemployment. The headline quote — "I'm terrified" — is not melodrama. It is the sound of a generation realising the first rungs of the career ladder are being pulled out before they arrive. This is not just another tech story; it is a stress test of whether the region's economies can still absorb their youth.

## "I'm terrified": puncturing a polite illusion

Southeast Asia has long sold itself — and been sold by outsiders — as a demographic sweet spot: young, cheap, and hungry. For two decades the story was that rising foreign investment and factory lines would absorb millions of new workers each year. The SCMP report punctures that comfort. The fear it documents sits not with factory hands but with university graduates in suits, the very cohort the region's boosters cite as proof of a coming Asian century. When the most credentialed young people say they are afraid, the reassuring narrative of endless absorption starts to look thin. Singapore's own Prime Minister Lawrence Wong has conceded that AI has already "broken the bottom rung" of the career ladder — a striking admission from a government that built its model on meritocratic upward mobility.

## The target is the bottom rung

What makes this moment distinct is precisely where AI lands. Automation is not starting at the top; it chews the bottom rung first — analyst trainees, junior copywriters, first-year researchers, back-office processors whose tasks are easiest to codify. These roles exist to train people, not to maximise output. Strip them away and you lose not just positions but the on-ramp. A ladder with its first three steps removed is not a ladder. The "I'm terrified" quote is the sound of people watching that on-ramp demolished ahead of them. In Malaysia, marketing graduates report that AI now produces the copy and visuals they once were hired to make; in Thailand, a Kasikorn Research Centre note observes that businesses no longer bother training fresh graduates for initial tasks because machines do them faster and cheaper.

## Most in need of jobs, least able to absorb the shock

Here is the regional irony. Southeast Asia is the part of the world that most needs large-scale entry-level employment — populations are young, welfare nets are thin, and the social contract rests heavily on the promise that education leads to a job. Yet it is also the region least equipped to cushion a shock to those jobs. Unlike wealthy economies that can lean on unemployment insurance, retraining budgets, or dense service sectors, many Southeast Asian states must bet on volume: enough new posts, fast enough, to keep the bargain intact. If AI thins the entry layer, the arithmetic turns brutal quickly. The numbers already whisper it: Singapore's share of job-seeking graduates employed within six months slipped to 83.4 per cent in 2025 from 87.1 per cent in 2024; in Thailand, roughly 44 per cent of the unemployed are graduates aged 20 to 24; Indonesia's youth unemployment hovers near 13 per cent.

## When the degree stops holding value

The deeper anxiety is about price. For a Filipino or Indonesian family that sinks a generation's savings into a degree, the diploma has functioned as a store of value — a bet that education converts into a stable salary. AI disrupts that conversion. When a machine drafts the report, summarises the case, or generates the first cut of a pitch, the premium on a generic degree erodes; what survives is either elite credentialing or narrow technical skill. The SCMP piece is, in effect, a quiet obituary for the assumption that any degree, anywhere, would keep paying. Societies that staked social mobility on mass higher education are discovering the asset can depreciate. The International Labour Organization estimates nearly 80 million Southeast Asian workers — more than a fifth of the regional workforce — will be touched by AI, with 11.7 million potentially losing jobs or seeing tasks fully reworked.

## Panic helps little, but playing dead is worse

None of this warrants panic, and the tech-optimist rebuttal — new jobs appear as old ones vanish — is not wrong in the long run. But "in the long run" is a luxury the region's young cannot afford this year. The useful response is design, not denial: treat AI as a labour-market event to be managed, not merely a productivity gift to celebrate. Singapore's answer — subsidised internships and a S$1 billion (about US$760 million) upskilling push — is a start, but the harder work is educational: curricula that still mass-produce the wrong skills need a frank reckoning. Southeast Asia's advantage has always been its people. If automation targets the entry rung, protecting it is not sentimentality; it is the core of the growth story itself.

## Further reading

**Generative AI**: AI systems that produce text, code, or images from prompts; the class of tools now entering entry-level office work.

**Job polarisation**: the tendency of automation to hollow out mid- and low-skill routine jobs while sparing both manual and highly creative roles.

**Labour-force absorption**: an economy's capacity to turn new entrants into employed workers — the metric now under pressure.
