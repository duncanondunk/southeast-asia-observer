# 中老铁路老挝段城际列车首发：磨丁到万象，从连通到成网

2026年9月28日早8时，C95次列车缓缓驶出老挝磨丁站，宣告中老铁路老挝段磨丁至万象城际列车正式首发。三个多小时后，列车于11时47分抵达万象；当天下午C96次反向返回。这条串起磨丁、纳堆、孟赛、琅勃拉邦、万荣、万象六座车站的城际线，让老挝北部省份民众到首都当天往返通勤、商务、探亲成为现实。

一条铁路的开新线，看似寻常。可放在老挝这个中南半岛唯一的真正内陆国身上，意义远不止多开一班车。

## 从陆锁国到陆联国：一条铁路改写国运

老挝长期被叫作陆锁国——不靠海、被邻国合围，物流成本长期高企。中老铁路2021年通车，全长约1035公里，其中老挝段约420公里，把昆明和万象连成一线。五年过去，这条线早已不只是连通，而是把老挝从被锁住的内陆，推进为连接的区域枢纽。

据运营方老中铁路有限公司数据，铁路开通以来客货运量持续攀升，老挝的香蕉、大米、铁矿石得以北上中国，中国的机电、建材、日用品南下老挝并中转泰国、马来西亚。交通成本降下来，老挝才真正有了枢纽的想象空间。

## 城际列车：把连通做成成网

首发的不只是跨境长途。C95与C96这组城际列车，填补的是老挝国内毛细血管——北部省份到首都万象的快速通勤空白。过去从磨丁一带去万象，公路动辄大半天；如今三个多小时即可抵达。

这才是普通老挝人最能感知的变化。一个国家的发展，往往不取决于首都修得多漂亮，而取决于偏远省份能否被接进国家网络。城际列车让北部山区的民众能当天往返首都办事、就医、探亲和经商，等于把国家缝合得更紧。

## 文旅红利：琅勃拉邦与万荣被点亮

列车途经的琅勃拉邦是联合国教科文组织世界遗产古城，万荣则以喀斯特山水和户外探险闻名。城际线开通，等于给这两张老挝旅游名片接上了高频班次的血管。

疫情后东南亚旅游强势复苏，老挝也想分一杯羹。但旅游拼的不只是景点，更是可达性。当琅勃拉邦、万荣与万象之间的通勤被压缩到几小时内，散客说走就走成为可能，沿线文旅产业才有持续造血的底气。铁路一旦把客流稳定下来，受益的将是沿线的家庭旅馆、餐饮与手工艺者，而不只是大资本。

## 经济走廊的交通注脚

老中铁路有限公司在说明里点明，这条城际线要助力中老经济走廊建设、高质量共建一带一路（Belt and Road Initiative）。这话不是套话。中老经济走廊要成势，前提是人和货能低成本、高频次地流动；城际列车正是这条走廊在老挝国土上的交通注脚。

但也别把话说满。一条线路能不能真正带动沿线发展，取决于站点周边的产业能不能跟上、配套能不能落地。铁路把可能性铺好了，能不能变现，还得看老挝自己的招商、营商与治理能力。

## 地缘视角：被重新连接的半岛

把镜头拉远，中老铁路是更大的泛亚铁路构想的关键一环。它北接中国西南、南望泰国，是中南半岛纵向联通的骨架。泰国、马来西亚近年来对这条线如何接入本国铁路网高度关注——一旦中老泰贯通，整个半岛的物流版图都会被改写。

有人担心，老挝因此对中国形成过重依赖，尤其是铁路融资多来自中方贷款。这类关切并非空穴来风：项目总投资约60亿美元（约420亿元人民币），老挝段由中方贷款融资，债务可持续性是绕不开的考题。但换个角度看，若铁路真能把老挝从陆锁变成陆联，增长的税收与贸易流量，反而可能成为偿债的活水。关键不在借不借，而在借来的钱有没有变成能生钱的基础设施。

## 克制一句：首发不是终点

中老铁路老挝段城际列车首发，是一带一路在东南亚最可见的落地样本之一。它让一个内陆国的日常通勤、文旅与贸易，被重新连进区域网络。

但首发只是起点。铁路的价值，最终要由普通老挝人能否更便宜地出行、沿线能否长出产业、债务能否被增长消化来检验。连通的故事，写得再漂亮，也得落地才算数。

## 延伸阅读

**中老铁路（China–Laos Railway）**：连接中国昆明与老挝万象的标准轨电气化铁路，全长约1035公里，2021年12月通车，是中南半岛纵向联通与中国—东盟互联互通的关键基础设施。

**中老经济走廊（China–Laos Economic Corridor）**：以中老铁路为骨架，串联两国产能、园区、文旅与口岸合作的经济合作框架，是一带一路在老挝的落地载体。

**陆锁国（Landlocked Developing Country）**：指深处内陆、无海岸线的国家，物流与贸易成本高企；老挝是中南半岛典型的陆锁国，中老铁路被视为其转型为陆联国的突破口。

# Laos Section Intercity Train Launches: From Boten to Vientiane, From Linked to Networked

On the morning of 28 September 2026, at 8:00 a.m., Train C95 pulled out of Boten station in northern Laos, marking the inaugural run of the Boten–Vientiane intercity service on the Lao section of the China–Laos Railway. A little over three and a half hours later it reached Vientiane at 11:47; the return service C96 left Vientiane at 14:05 and was back in Boten by 18:32. Threading six stations — Boten, Nateuy, Muang Xay, Luang Prabang, Vang Vieng and Vientiane — the line lets residents of Laos' northern provinces commute, do business or visit family in the capital and return the same day.

Opening one more train service sounds routine. Strapped onto Laos — the only truly landlocked country on the Indochinese peninsula — its significance runs far deeper than an extra timetable slot.

## From landlocked to land-linked: a railway rewriting a country's fate

Laos has long worn the label "landlocked" — no coastline, boxed in by neighbours, logistics costs chronically high. The China–Laos Railway opened in 2021, running about 1,035 km in total, of which roughly 420 km lie on Lao soil, linking Kunming and Vientiane. Five years on, the line has moved well beyond mere connection: it is pushing Laos from a sealed-up interior toward a connected regional hub.

According to the operator, Lao–Chinese Railway Co., Ltd., passenger and freight volumes have climbed steadily since opening. Lao bananas, rice and iron ore now move north into China, while Chinese machinery, building materials and consumer goods flow south into Laos and onward to Thailand and Malaysia. Only when transport costs fall can Laos begin to imagine itself as a hub.

## The intercity service: turning connection into a network

What launched was not only cross-border long-haul. The C95/C96 pair fills a domestic capillary gap — fast commuting between the northern provinces and the capital. Reaching Vientiane from the Boten area by road used to take the better part of a day; now it is a little over three hours.

This is the change ordinary Lao citizens feel most directly. A country's development is rarely decided by how polished its capital looks, but by whether its remote provinces get plugged into the national network. The intercity train lets people from the northern highlands transact, seek medical care, visit relatives and trade in the capital and be home the same day — it stitches the nation tighter.

## A tourism dividend: Luang Prabang and Vang Vieng light up

Two stops on the line carry weight far beyond their size. Luang Prabang is a UNESCO world heritage town; Vang Vieng is famed for karst scenery and outdoor adventure. The intercity service effectively gives these two flagship Lao tourism brands a high-frequency circulatory system.

Southeast Asian tourism has rebounded hard since the pandemic, and Laos wants its share. But tourism competes less on sights than on reachability. Once Luang Prabang and Vang Vieng sit within a few hours of Vientiane, independent travellers can go on impulse, and the towns' hospitality, food and handicraft sectors gain a steadier lifeline — not just big capital.

## A transport footnote for the economic corridor

The operator framed the service explicitly as supporting the China–Laos Economic Corridor and the high-quality building of the Belt and Road. That is not empty boilerplate. For the corridor to take shape, people and goods must move cheaply and frequently; the intercity train is the corridor's transport footnote on Lao soil.

But let us not oversell. Whether a line truly lifts the areas along it depends on whether industries, stationside and support services actually materialise. The railway lays out the possibility; whether it converts into real growth hinges on Laos' own investment promotion, business environment and governance.

## A geopolitical lens: a peninsula being reconnected

Zoom out, and the China–Laos Railway is a key link in the larger Trans-Asian Railway vision. It connects southwest China in the north and looks toward Thailand in the south, forming the spine of vertical connectivity across the Indochinese peninsula. Thailand and Malaysia have watched closely how this line might plug into their own networks — once China–Laos–Thailand is joined up, the peninsula's entire logistics map could be redrawn.

Some worry Laos is over-exposing itself to China, not least because much of the railway's financing came from Chinese loans. The concern is not baseless: total project cost was about US$6 billion (roughly ¥42 billion), with the Lao section financed through Chinese loans, and debt sustainability is a question that cannot be dodged. But turn it around — if the railway genuinely shifts Laos from landlocked to land-linked, the resulting tax and trade flows could become the very water that services the debt. The issue is not whether to borrow, but whether the borrowed money becomes infrastructure that earns.

## One restrained line: a launch is not a finish

The launch of the Lao-section intercity train is among the most visible on-the-ground samples of the Belt and Road in Southeast Asia. It re-links a landlocked country's daily commute, tourism and trade into the regional network.

But a launch is only a start. The railway's worth will ultimately be tested by whether ordinary Lao people travel more cheaply, whether industries grow along the line, and whether debt is digested by growth. Connectivity stories, however elegantly written, only count once they land.

## Further reading

**China–Laos Economic Corridor**: An economic cooperation framework built around the railway, chaining the two countries' production capacity, industrial parks, cultural tourism and border ports; the Belt and Road's delivery vehicle in Laos.
