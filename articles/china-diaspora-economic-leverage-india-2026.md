# 把侨胞当"经济延伸"：中国借力海外华侨华人的打法，印度为何学不像

一份对照很有意思。中国和印度都坐拥世界上规模最大的海外侨民群体，可怎么"用"这批人，两国的路子截然不同。《南华早报》近日一篇评论把这件事摆上了台面：北京早早把侨胞接回本土、变成经济延伸的一部分，新德里则更多把侨民视作汇回外汇、点缀门面的象征。这不是简单的情怀差别，而是一套制度与时间攒出来的能力差。

## 数字：两份不一样的"侨力账本"

先看账本。印度2024年收到侨汇约1290亿美元（约9200亿元人民币），相当于其国内生产总值的3%以上；中国同年收到约480亿美元（约3400亿元人民币），占国内生产总值不足0.2%。单比"汇回来的钱"，印度完胜。可换一个口径，故事就反过来了：中国出国留学人员的归国比例在二〇一三至2018年间升到约八成；一项2023年的研究指出，署名中国机构、有归国学者参与的论文占到中国地址论文的约38%，而在美国机构中、华裔出生的研究者参与的论文占美国地址论文的约27%。也就是说，中国把"人"接了回来，印度把"钱"留了下来。

## 中国打法：从"送出去"到"引回来"

中国的打法有清晰的时间线。1978年，邓小平拍板每年送3000名学生出国，把留学当成国策；待2001年加入世界贸易组织后，微软、英特尔、甲骨文等跨国公司在华设立研发中枢，高薪岗位把海外华人学者往回吸；2008年的"千人计划"更是明码标价地全球抢人。结果是中关村长出了足以与硅谷掰手腕的科创生态——月之暗面创始人杨植麟2019年从美国回来创办公司，就是这条链路上的最新注脚。一句话：中国把当年送出去培养的成本，连本带利收了回来。

## 制度底座：一台专门的"侨务机器"

光有意愿不够，得有抓手。中国有一套专门服务侨务的行政与社团体系——从国务院侨务机构到各级归国华侨联合会，再到遍布世界的华商社团，把"引资、引智、引才"做成了一套常态化机制。反观印度，2004年才设立专管海外印度人的部门，2016年又并回外交部，重心长期放在领事保护与"自豪感"叙事上，各邦参与极不均衡。换句话说，一个把侨务写进了发展规划的章节，一个把侨务塞进了外交部的抽屉。

## 印度的路子：汇款与"人才外流"的遗产

印度的路径带着历史包袱。1991年经济改革之前，向外移民长期被视作"人才外流"的遗憾；改革之后，政府才把侨民包装成通往资本、技术与全球市场的桥梁。可现实是，印度侨民对经济的贡献仍高度集中在侨汇和熟练劳工，真正能把前沿技术、风险资本与创新网络接回本土的机制始终单薄。每年上千亿美元的侨汇撑起了家庭消费与外汇存量，却很少转化成产业升级的发动机。

## 关键差异：要"回流的人"还是"汇回的钱"

两种模式的分野，说到底是短期账面与长期能力的取舍。汇款是即时的、可统计的，能立刻改善家庭收支、撑起外汇储备；但它不带来技术、不培养产业、不沉淀制度。中国押注的是"回流的人"——他们带回实验室、专利、管理经验和全球视野，构成的是可持续的创新底座。南华早报的评论点得很直白：中国着眼长期能力建构，印度侧重短期收益。这话说得刺耳，却戳中了要害。

## 隐忧与边界：侨力不是万能药

当然，把侨胞当"经济延伸"也不是没有代价。中国的引才高度依赖西方顶尖院校，一旦地缘政治把人才流动焊上铁门，这套打法就会承压；而归国人才在本土也常撞上科研评价、经费分配与行政冗余的暗礁。印度那边的短板则反过来——人留在了海外，本土缺乏把离散网络转化为产业能力的制度接口。侨力是杠杆，不是底座；能放大已有的积累，却补不上制度的窟窿。

## 区域镜鉴：东南亚华侨华人的双重角色

把镜头拉回东南亚，这里的华侨华人本就是全球规模最大的华裔离散群体，也是中印两种路径之外的第三种样本。他们既是所在国经济的中坚，又是中国招商引资、文化软实力的天然桥接点；可一旦大国博弈升温，这层"双重身份"又会变成风险——既可能被质疑忠诚，也可能被当作外部势力的注脚。对北京而言，东南亚侨胞是最顺手的延伸；对东南亚各国而言，这份侨力既是红利，也是需要小心安放的变量。

## 延伸阅读

**侨务政策（diaspora policy）**：一国为联系、服务并动员海外侨民而建立的制度总和；中国的特点是有专门行政体系与常态化"引资引智"机制，印度则长期由外交部统筹、偏重领事与象征维度。
**千人计划（Thousand Talents Program）**：中国2008年启动的全球高层次人才引进计划，以优厚待遇吸引海外学者回国，是中国"引才回流"战略的标志性工程。
**侨汇（Remittances）**：海外劳工或侨民向母国的汇款；印度2024年侨汇约占国内生产总值3%以上，是两国侨民经济贡献结构差异的核心指标。

# Treating the diaspora as an 'economic extension': how China leverages overseas Chinese — and why India cannot easily copy it

A telling comparison. China and India both command among the world's largest diasporas, yet they put these populations to work in markedly different ways. A recent South China Morning Post commentary lays it bare: Beijing moved early to pull its emigrants home and fold them into the domestic economy as an extension of it, while New Delhi has tended to treat its diaspora more as a source of remittances and a badge of pride. The gap is not one of numbers; it is one of institutions and time.

## The numbers: two very different diaspora ledgers

Start with the ledgers. India received about US$129 billion in remittances in 2024 — more than 3 per cent of its gross domestic product — while China took in roughly US$48 billion, under 0.2 per cent of its GDP. On "money sent home," India wins hands down. But switch the metric and the story inverts: China's rate of students returning after studying abroad climbed to about 80 per cent between 2013 and 2018, and a 2023 study found that papers authored from a Chinese address with returned-scholar involvement accounted for roughly 38 per cent of China-address publications, versus about 27 per cent for ethnic-Chinese-born researchers contributing to US-address papers. In other words, China brought the people back; India kept the money in.

## China's playbook: from "send out" to "bring back"

China's approach follows a clear timeline. In 1978 Deng Xiaoping decreed the annual dispatch of 3,000 students abroad, making overseas study a matter of state policy. After China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, Microsoft, Intel and Oracle set up research hubs on the mainland, and well-paid jobs began pulling ethnic-Chinese academics home. The 2008 Thousand Talents Program then put a global recruitment drive on a formal footing. The payoff is visible in Zhongguancun, a science-and-technology cluster now credible enough to rival Silicon Valley — and in founders like Yang Zhilin, who returned from the United States in 2019 to co-found the AI firm Moonshot. In short: China collected, with interest, on the cost of educating its people abroad.

## The institutional base: a dedicated diaspora machine

Intent alone is not enough; you need machinery. China maintains a dedicated administrative and associational apparatus for diaspora affairs — from the state council's overseas-Chinese offices to the federations of returned overseas Chinese at every level, down to overseas-Chinese business associations spanning the globe — turning "attracting investment, talent and expertise" into a routinised mechanism. India, by contrast, only created a ministry for overseas Indians in 2004 and folded it into the Ministry of External Affairs in 2016, with its focus long fixed on consular protection and a "pride" narrative, and highly uneven participation across states. One country wrote diaspora affairs into its development plan; the other filed it inside the foreign ministry's drawer.

## India's path: the legacy of remittances and "brain drain"

India's route carries historical baggage. Before the 1991 economic reforms, emigration was widely lamented as a "brain drain." Only after reform did the state recast the diaspora as a bridge to capital, technology and global markets. Yet in practice India's diaspora still contributes to the economy overwhelmingly through remittances and skilled labour; the mechanisms to channel frontier technology, venture capital and innovation networks back home remain thin. The tens of billions of dollars in remittances each year shore up household consumption and foreign-exchange reserves but rarely translate into an engine of industrial upgrading.

## The key divide: returning people versus returning money

The split between the two models is, at root, a choice between short-term bookkeeping and long-term capacity. Remittances are immediate and measurable; they lift household budgets and prop up reserves at once. But they bring no technology, build no industry and deposit no institutions. China bets on the "returning person" — who arrives with laboratories, patents, managerial know-how and a global vision — forming a sustainable base for innovation. The SCMP commentary puts it bluntly: China looks to long-term capacity building, India to short-term gains. Harsh, but it hits the mark.

## The caveats: diaspora leverage is no panacea

Of course, treating the diaspora as an economic extension has costs of its own. China's talent recruitment leans heavily on top Western universities; once geopolitics welds the doors of talent mobility shut, the model comes under pressure. And returnees at home still collide with the submerged rocks of research evaluation, funding allocation and administrative bloat. India's deficit runs the other way — its people stay abroad, and the homeland lacks the institutional interface to convert diaspora networks into industrial capability. Diaspora power is a lever, not a foundation; it amplifies accumulated strength but cannot patch a hole in the system.

## A regional lens: Southeast Asia's dual-role diaspora

Zoom in on Southeast Asia, home to the world's largest ethnic-Chinese diaspora and a third model beyond the China–India binary. Its ethnic Chinese are both the economic backbone of their host societies and a natural bridgehead for China's investment promotion and cultural soft power. Yet when great-power rivalry heats up, that dual identity becomes a liability — a community whose loyalty may be questioned at home and read abroad as an appendage of an external power. For Beijing, Southeast Asia's diaspora is the most convenient extension; for the region's states, it is both a dividend and a variable to be handled with care.

## Further reading

**diaspora policy**: The ensemble of institutions a state builds to connect with, serve and mobilise its emigrants. China's distinctive feature is a dedicated administrative system and a routinised "attract investment and talent" mechanism, whereas India has long run diaspora affairs through the foreign ministry with an emphasis on consular and symbolic dimensions.
**Thousand Talents Program**: China's global high-level talent recruitment scheme launched in 2008, which lured overseas scholars home with generous terms and became the flagship project of the country's "talent-return" strategy.
**Remittances**: Funds sent home by overseas workers or emigrants. India's 2024 remittances amounted to over 3 per cent of GDP, the core indicator of the structural difference between the two countries' diaspora economies.
