# 同月共圆：中柬中秋联欢晚会为何落在金边

当地时间9月23日晚，金边用一场名为"彩云追月·睦邻情深"的中秋联欢晚会，把中柬两国的艺术家与观众拉到同一轮明月之下。这场晚会由中共云南省委宣传部、中国驻柬埔寨大使馆、柬埔寨旅游部与新闻部联合主办，柬中友好协会主席艾森沃、柬埔寨副首相宋金达、旅游部大臣胡哈、中国驻柬大使汪文斌等政要悉数出席。中秋本是华人社会的传统节庆，如今被搬上两国官方合办的国家级舞台，这本身就值得玩味：一个源自民间的节日，正怎样被用作邻里外交的柔性载体？

## 一场由月亮串起来的外交

中秋的核心意象是月亮。晚会的策划者很聪明——他们不谈经贸、不签文件，而是让"同一个月亮"成为情感的最大公约数。柬中关系发展学会会长谢莫尼勒在台上讲述中柬友谊村与澜湄书屋的故事，把宏大的双边关系落到具体的村落与书架；中柬新媒体人互推世界遗产地，喊出"游云南看吴哥，世界遗产游"的口号。月亮之下，丰收与和平的向往被说成"始终一致"。

这其实是一种低成本的叙事策略。比起一纸协议，节庆更容易越过政治语言的过滤，直接触达普通人的情感。问题在于：当情感的政治色彩越来越浓，节庆还能不能保持它原本的"民间性"？这是个值得警惕的点。

## 云南的"柔性"出场：从茶香到街舞快闪

这场晚会最耐人寻味的，是云南的出场方式。作为主办方之一，云南没有摆出省域招商的阵仗，而是把佤族织锦、云南茶叶、街舞快闪塞进金边的公园、广场与街头。中秋创意集市里，中国云南茶叶的香气与柬埔寨咖啡的味道交织；云南民族街舞者用快闪把中国文化带进柬埔寨首都的日常场景。

这是典型的"柔性"省级外交：不靠中央政府自上而下推动，而由地方文化产品自下而上渗透。云南借中柬友好的大势，把自身文旅品牌（佤锦、茶叶、世界遗产）悄悄植入柬埔寨民众的日常生活。比起硬邦邦的经贸数据，"茶香加街舞"的记忆点更持久。但也要看到，这类渗透高度依赖两国关系的整体暖度，一旦政治气候转冷，最先被冷落的往往就是这些文化符号。

## 在柬华人：夹在两国之间的文化摆渡人

柬埔寨是东南亚华人扎根最深、同化最彻底的国家之一。华人在当地经济、社团与文化生活中长期扮演桥梁角色。中秋这类节庆，对柬埔寨华人而言从来不只是"过节"——它是一套确认身份、维系代际记忆的仪式。当晚会由两国官方联合主办，意味着华人的民间节庆被正式纳入双边关系的叙事框架，华人社区从"自娱自乐"走向"国家搭台"。

这既是机遇也是张力。机遇在于，华人文化获得更高层级的正当性背书；张力在于，节庆的政治化可能让社区在两国之间更难保持纯粹的"摆渡"姿态。华人历来善于在母国与住居国之间左右逢源，一旦节庆被赋予太多外交含义，这份灵活空间会不会被压缩？答案还不明朗。

## 节庆外交能走多远

把中秋办成国家级联欢晚会，中柬并非孤例。此前泰国也办过首届电视中秋晚会，新加坡、马来西亚的华人社团更是常年以中秋维系文化认同。可见节庆外交在东南亚已成常态。它的价值在于润物无声：用共同的文化记忆软化国家间的硬度。

但节庆终究是节庆。它可以拉近人心，却填不平利益的沟壑。中柬关系真正的压舱石，是产业链、基建与地缘格局，而非一轮明月。晚会散场后，金边的公园依旧要面对现实的经贸与治理课题。把节庆外交的温情当作双边关系的全部，是一种误读；把它当作润喉的糖，而非主菜，才更接近真相。

## 延伸阅读

**澜湄合作（Lancang–Mekong Cooperation）**：中国与湄公河五国（含柬埔寨）的次区域合作机制，文化互鉴是其五大支柱之外的软性补充。

**佤族织锦（Wa brocade）**：云南佤族传统手工织物，本次作为文化符号亮相金边，是省级文旅"出海"的微观样本。

**中柬关系（China–Cambodia Relations）**：两国自1958年建交，近年被双方概括为"铁杆友谊"，节庆外交是其民间层面的延伸。

# Under One Moon: Why the China–Cambodia Mid-Autumn Gala Landed in Phnom Penh

On the evening of 23 September, Phnom Penh staged a Mid-Autumn gala titled "Chasing Clouds, Pursuing the Moon — Deep Affinity Between Neighbours," gathering artists and audiences from both China and Cambodia under the same full moon. The event was jointly organised by the Publicity Department of the Yunnan Provincial Committee of the Communist Party of China, the Chinese Embassy in Cambodia, and Cambodia's Ministry of Tourism and Ministry of Information. Among the dignitaries were Ek Sam Ol, President of the Cambodia–China Friendship Association, Cambodian Deputy Prime Minister Songkim Heng, Tourism Minister Huot Hak, Chinese Ambassador to Cambodia Wang Wenbin, and Peng Bin, deputy head of Yunnan's propaganda department. The Mid-Autumn Festival is, at root, a folk celebration of the Chinese community; that it now headlines a state-level, binational stage is itself worth examining. How is a folk festival being repurposed as a soft vehicle of neighbourhood diplomacy?

## A diplomacy strung together by the moon

The organising motif was the moon. Rather than talk trade or sign documents, the planners made "the same moon" the greatest emotional common denominator. On stage, Chim Monirith, head of the Cambodia–China Relations Development Society, told stories of the China–Cambodia Friendship Village and the Lancang–Mekong Book House, translating grand bilateral relations into concrete villages and bookshelves. Chinese and Cambodian content creators cross-promoted each other's heritage sites under the slogan "Visit Yunnan, see Angkor — tour the world's heritage." Beneath the moon, the shared longing for harvest and peace was declared "always the same."

This is a low-cost narrative strategy. Compared with a written agreement, a festival more easily slips past the filter of political language and reaches ordinary emotions directly. The question worth raising is whether, as the emotional content grows more politically charged, the festival can still retain its original "folk" character. That is a point to watch.

## Yunnan's soft entrance: from tea aromas to street-dance flash mobs

The most intriguing part of the gala was Yunnan's mode of appearance. As a co-host, Yunnan did not stage a provincial investment pitch. Instead it tucked Wa brocade, Yunnan tea and street-dance flash mobs into Phnom Penh's parks, squares and streets. At the creative Mid-Autumn market, the scent of Yunnan tea mingled with Cambodian coffee; Yunnan ethnic dancers carried Chinese culture into the everyday scenes of the Cambodian capital through flash mobs.

This is classic "soft" provincial diplomacy: not top-down from the central government, but bottom-up penetration by local cultural products. Riding the broader current of China–Cambodia friendship, Yunnan quietly implants its cultural and tourism brand — Wa brocade, tea, world heritage — into the daily life of Cambodians. Compared with hard economic figures, the memory of "tea aroma plus street dance" lasts longer. Yet such penetration depends heavily on the overall warmth of bilateral ties; once the political climate cools, these cultural symbols are often the first to be shelved.

## Cambodian Chinese: cultural ferrymen between two countries

Cambodia is among the Southeast Asian countries where ethnic Chinese have taken the deepest root and assimilated most thoroughly. The community has long served as a bridge in the country's economy, associations and cultural life. For Cambodian Chinese, festivals like Mid-Autumn have never been merely about "celebrating" — they are rituals that confirm identity and sustain intergenerational memory. When the gala is jointly staged by both governments, the community's folk festival is formally folded into the narrative framework of bilateral relations, moving from "celebrating among ourselves" to "the state building the stage."

This is both opportunity and tension. The opportunity is that Chinese culture gains higher-level legitimisation. The tension is that the politicisation of the festival may make it harder for the community to keep its purely "ferrying" posture between the two countries. Ethnic Chinese have long been adept at navigating between motherland and country of residence; once a festival is loaded with too much diplomatic meaning, will that flexible space be compressed? The answer is not yet clear.

## How far can festival diplomacy travel

Staging Mid-Autumn as a national-level gala is not unique to China and Cambodia. Thailand has held its first televised Mid-Autumn gala, and Chinese associations in Singapore and Malaysia have long used the festival to sustain cultural identity. Festival diplomacy has plainly become routine in Southeast Asia. Its value lies in its quiet softening of hard state edges through shared cultural memory.

But a festival is, after all, a festival. It can draw hearts closer, yet it cannot fill the trenches of interest. The true ballast of China–Cambodia relations is supply chains, infrastructure and geopolitical configuration — not a full moon. After the gala ends, Phnom Penh's parks still face the real questions of trade and governance. To mistake the warmth of festival diplomacy for the whole of bilateral relations is a misreading; to treat it as a throat-soothing sweet rather than the main course is closer to the truth.
