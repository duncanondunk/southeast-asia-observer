# 湄公河的旱涝：气候变化与地缘博弈

> 一条跨国河流承载的生态危机与水权之争。

湄公河从青藏高原发源，流经缅甸、老挝、泰国（Thailand）、柬埔寨、越南（Vietnam），最后注入南海（South China Sea）。这条 4350公里的河流，养育着下游五国超过 6000万人口。而它近年来的旱涝交替，既是气候变暖的结果，也是一场关于水权的无声博弈。

## 两条河：澜沧江与湄公河

上游中国境内称澜沧江，下游称湄公河。上游已建成的梯级水坝，对径流的调节能力，远超下游任何一国的工程。这构成了湄公河水权争端的物理基础。

- **上游**：中国建有 11座大型水电站，具备显著的季节性调蓄能力。
- **中游**：老挝正大力推进"东南亚（Southeast Asia）电池"计划，湄公河干流上已规划数十座水电站。
- **下游**：三角洲的越南，承受着盐渍化与沉降的双重压力。

## 气候变暖：旱涝交替的底层推手

湄公河流域的季风降水，本就有明显的干湿两季。而气候变暖正在放大这种波动：雨季更集中、更猛烈，旱季更长、更干燥。

2024年的异常枯水期，让三角洲出现罕见的大面积盐水入侵，稻农损失惨重；而 2025年汛期的几次强降雨，又让柬埔寨与越南的洪泛区陷入内涝。

> 当上游水坝的调度，叠加气候异常的径流波动，下游的旱涝就成了一场谁也说不清责任的灾难。

## 数据共享的玻璃墙

湄公河委员会（MRC）成立多年，理论上六国共享水文数据。但实践中，上游与下游的数据共享始终存在"玻璃墙"——公开的数据，往往滞后或被简化。

这让下游国家在旱季异常时，很难判断径流下降有多少是气候使然，又有多少是上游蓄水的结果。**信息不对称，是水权博弈最锋利的武器。**

## 三角洲的困境

越南湄公河三角洲，是东南亚最重要的稻米与水产产区之一。但三角洲正面临三重压力：

1. **上游泥沙减少**——水坝拦截了原本维持三角洲地势的泥沙，地面沉降加剧。
2. **盐水入侵**——枯水期海水倒灌，稻米产区被迫改作水产养殖，或干脆弃耕。
3. **地下水超采**——为应对地表水不足，地下水被过度抽取，进一步加速沉降。

这是一场缓慢的生态塌陷，没有单一责任方，却每个人都难辞其咎。

## 结语

湄公河的旱涝，是气候变暖与人类工程叠加的复合结果，也是一条跨国河流上六国利益的镜像。

它提醒我们：**水权的博弈，从来不只是国家间的较量，更是数千万依赖这条河生存的普通人的命运。** 而他们，至今在这场博弈中几乎没有发言权。

## 延伸阅读

**湄公河（Mekong River）**：东南亚第一长河，中国境内段称澜沧江（Lancang River），出境后流经缅甸、老挝、泰国、柬埔寨、越南，注入南海。它拥有世界规模最大的内陆渔业之一，下游逾6000万人赖其为生。

**湄公河委员会（MRC）**：1995年4月泰、老、柬、越四国在泰国清莱签署《湄公河流域可持续发展合作协定》后成立，秘书处自2004年起设于老挝万象（Vientiane），负责协调水资源开发与水文数据共享。中国与缅甸自1996年起为“对话伙伴”而非成员国，这是文中“数据玻璃墙”的制度背景。2016年成立的澜沧江—湄公河合作机制（Lancang-Mekong Cooperation）则六国全参与。

**湄公河三角洲（Mekong Delta）**：越南南部冲积平原，占全国约12%土地与近两成人口，贡献全国约一半稻米产量和95%的大米出口，也是本文所述盐水入侵与地面沉降的重灾区。

# The Mekong's Droughts and Floods: Climate Change and Geopolitics

> A transnational river's ecological crisis and the contest over water rights.

The Mekong rises on the Tibetan Plateau and flows through Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia, and Vietnam before emptying into the South China Sea. Over 4,350 km, it sustains more than 60 million people in its lower basin. Its recent alternation of drought and flood is both a consequence of warming and a silent contest over water rights.

## Two Rivers: Lancang and Mekong

The upper reaches, in China, are called the Lancang; downstream, the Mekong. The cascade of dams built upstream can regulate runoff far more powerfully than any single downstream state's engineering. This is the physical foundation of the Mekong water-rights dispute.

- **Upstream**: China has built 11 large hydropower stations with significant seasonal storage.
- **Middle reaches**: Laos is pushing its "Battery of Southeast Asia" plan, with dozens of dams planned on the Mekong mainstream.
- **Downstream**: Vietnam's delta bears the twin pressures of salinization and subsidence.

## Warming: The Underlying Driver of Drought-Flood Swings

The Mekong basin's monsoon rainfall already has pronounced wet and dry seasons. Warming is amplifying that variability: the wet season grows more concentrated and intense, the dry season longer and drier.

The abnormal low-water period of 2024 brought rare, large-scale saltwater intrusion to the delta, devastating rice farmers; several intense downpours during the 2025 flood season submerged floodplains in Cambodia and Vietnam.

> When upstream dam operations compound climate-driven runoff swings, downstream drought and flood become a disaster with no clear culprit.

## The Glass Wall of Data Sharing

The Mekong River Commission (MRC) has existed for years, and in theory the six states share hydrological data. In practice, a "glass wall" persists between upstream and downstream — published data is often lagging or simplified.

This leaves downstream states, during dry-season anomalies, unable to disentangle how much of the runoff drop is climate and how much is upstream storage. **Information asymmetry is the sharpest weapon in the water-rights contest.**

## The Delta's Predicament

Vietnam's Mekong Delta is one of Southeast Asia's most important rice and aquaculture regions. But the delta faces a triple squeeze:

1. **Less upstream sediment** — dams trap the sediment that once sustained the delta's elevation, accelerating subsidence.
2. **Saltwater intrusion** — low-flow seasons let seawater push upstream, forcing rice fields into aquaculture or abandonment.
3. **Groundwater over-extraction** — to offset scarce surface water, groundwater is pumped excessively, deepening subsidence.

This is a slow ecological collapse with no single culprit — and everyone is implicated.

## Conclusion

The Mekong's droughts and floods are the compound result of warming layered on human engineering, and a mirror of six nations' interests on a transnational river.

It reminds us: **the contest over water rights is never just a contest between states — it is the fate of tens of millions who depend on this river to live.** And they, so far, have almost no voice in that contest.

## Further reading
**The Mekong River** — Southeast Asia's longest river, rising on the Tibetan Plateau and reaching the South China Sea. Known as the Lancang inside China, it then crosses Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia and Vietnam. It supports one of the world's largest inland fisheries and more than 60 million people in its lower basin.

**The Mekong River Commission (MRC)** — Founded in April 1995, when Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and Vietnam signed the Agreement on the Sustainable Development of the Mekong River Basin at Chiang Rai. Its secretariat has been based in Vientiane since 2004, and it coordinates water-resource planning and hydrological data sharing. China and Myanmar have been "dialogue partners" rather than members since 1996 — the institutional background to the "glass wall" described above. The Lancang-Mekong Cooperation mechanism, established in 2016, includes all six states.

**The Mekong Delta** — Vietnam's southern alluvial plain, holding about 12% of the country's land and close to a fifth of its people. It produces roughly half of Vietnam's rice and 95% of its rice exports, and is the area worst affected by the saltwater intrusion and subsidence discussed here.
