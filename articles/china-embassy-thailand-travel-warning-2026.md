# 中国驻泰使馆发旅游安全预警 泰国部长急忙“打包票”

据联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）10月20日报道，泰国旅游与体育部长素拉沙（Sorasak）在内阁会议前回应中国驻泰国大使馆日前发布的旅游安全预警时称，泰国政府高度重视游客安全，并澄清“普通游客不会在泰国被强行塞入车辆或押送至边境”。这番表态的直接诱因，是使馆在官网点名提醒中国公民：近期泰国发生多起入境后失踪失联、遭非法拘禁并被诱骗转运至泰缅边境的案件。一纸预警，把泰国旅游业的“中国心病”再次摆上了台面。

## 使馆预警：一纸通告的分量

中国驻外使馆就某一友好邻国发布旅游安全预警，本身就是一个值得玩味的信号。使馆措辞并不含糊：提醒赴泰中国公民警惕所谓“轻松赚钱”“高薪就业”的招聘陷阱，选择正规交通工具，谨慎接受陌生人或熟人的“免费顺风车”，并审慎评估前往泰缅边境及偏远地区的必要性。换句话说，风险被明确定位在“以旅游为名、实为被拐骗转运至边境诈骗园区”这一链条上。

这已不是使馆第一次就东南亚安全发出提醒。就在不久前，中国驻菲律宾使馆也因绑架案件发布过类似预警。两次预警的共同底色，是东南亚部分地区“以高薪诱骗、跨境转运、强迫从事电诈”的犯罪模式正从偶发走向常态化。当一个国家的使馆需要公开告诉本国公民“别轻信熟人免费车”，说明问题已经不是个别治安事件，而是系统性的信任危机。

## 泰国旅游业的“中国依赖”

泰国为什么紧张？答案写在数据里。中国曾长期是泰国第一大外国游客来源国。然而安全恐慌正在抽走这块基石：泰国旅游代理协会（ATTA）已将2026年中国游客预期从900万人次下调至700万人次；泰国国家旅游局（TAT）的官方预期更低，约为510万人次，虽较2025年约447万人次有所回升，却仍远未回到2019年水平。

更刺眼的是趋势对比。2025年赴泰外国游客总量约为3290万人次，同比下降7.23%，为除疫情外十年来首次下滑；其中中国游客较2019年暴跌超过六成。与此同时，越南、马来西亚、韩国正加紧争夺中国客源。泰国旅游业者私下感叹：泰国“失去了中国游客的三分之一，至今没能让他们回来”。据业内测算，2026年中国公民出境约1.79亿人次，支出约2580亿美元（约1.86万亿元人民币），这块巨型蛋糕的流向，正因安全叙事而重新洗牌。

## “中转站”困局：免签与边境的裂缝

素拉沙部长急于“打包票”，话里却藏着尴尬。他一方面强调泰国“绝不允许自身成为跨国犯罪的中转站”，另一方面又承认涉案人员“往往在原籍国就已与犯罪团伙建立联系”，并称这类安全问题“似乎只出现在中国游客身上”。这番解释，与其说是澄清，不如说是甩锅——把责任推回“出境前已被骗”。

但现实要复杂得多。泰国2024年推出的60天免签本为刺激复苏，却被安全部门发现存在“以游客身份入境、将泰国作为跨国犯罪基地”的漏洞，随后被迫收紧至30天。泰国与缅甸漫长的边界线、薄弱的边境管控，加上部分执法人员涉恶的丑闻（今年5月沙缴府5名警察曾假扮执法绑架中国公民勒索赎金），共同构成了“中转站”的温床。泰国政府已在普吉等地突击清查，缴获大麻产品总价值逾50万泰铢（约10.17万元人民币）。部长说“普通游客不会被强行押上车”，这话或许不假；可真正让中国家庭害怕的，是那些“自愿”入境、转眼失联的亲人。

## 社交媒体的放大效应

在中国，坏消息传播得格外快。微博、抖音上任何一条“中国人在泰国失联”“被绑架”的短视频，都会迅速引爆舆论。哪怕个案稀少、且多属被针对性诱骗，标题本身已足以让一个家庭重新考虑行程。泰国业者将此称为“寒蝉效应”：当一国目的地在中文社交媒体上被贴上“不安全”标签，形象修复远比数据回升艰难。

这也解释了为何素拉沙要亲自上电视喊话。对泰国而言，损失的不是几个游客，而是整个中国市场的信心。当安全成为出境决策的第一变量，任何旅游宣传都显得苍白。

## 领事保护：从被动到主动

把视野拉回外交层面，使馆预警折射出中国领事保护思路的变化。过去，领事保护多半是事发后的交涉；如今，提前发布风险提示、主动塑造出境安全预期，已成为常态。这种“主动式领事保护”，既是对公民负责的体现，也在客观上把“本国公民在境外是否安全”变成了可与东道国博弈的软筹码。

对东南亚国家而言，这是一道新考题：在争夺中国游客的竞争中，比拼的不再只是价格和景点，还有“我的国土对你的公民是否安全”这一底线承诺。泰国此番高调回应，正是这种博弈的缩影。

## 区域竞逐：谁在承接流失的中国客

安全恐慌叠加出行成本上升，正把中国游客推向别处。韩国预计今年迎来约700万中国游客，越南入境游客市场同比增长13.8%，马来西亚则以穆斯林中国客与乌鲁木齐直飞抢滩。泰国若不能在安全叙事上止血，其区域旅游枢纽的地位恐将进一步松动。

说到底，素拉沙部长的“打包票”能否奏效，不取决于话术，而取决于泰国能否真正堵住边境与免签的裂缝、清理执法队伍中的害群之马。否则，再多的喊话，也拦不住中国家庭用脚投票。

## 延伸阅读

**领事保护（Consular Protection）**：指一国驻外使领馆依据国际法及双边条约，保护本国公民在驻在国合法权益的实务机制；近年中国在东南亚由“事后交涉”转向“事前预警”。

**电信诈骗（Telecom Fraud）**：以虚假招聘、高薪就业为饵，诱骗受害人跨境转运至边境诈骗园区并强迫从事非法活动的犯罪模式，已成为东南亚跨境治理的核心难题。

**跨国犯罪（Transnational Crime）**：跨越国境的有组织犯罪活动，泰国因地理位置与签证政策常被利用为人口贩运与电诈的“中转站”。

# China's Embassy in Thailand Issues Travel Safety Alert — and Bangkok Rushes to Reassure

According to Lianhe Zaobao's 20 October report, Thailand's Minister of Tourism and Sports Sorasak told reporters ahead of a cabinet meeting that the government takes tourist safety "very seriously" and clarified that "ordinary tourists will not be forcibly bundled into vehicles and escorted to the border." The trigger was a notice posted on the website of the Chinese Embassy in Thailand warning citizens of multiple recent cases in which Chinese nationals disappeared after entry, were illegally detained, and were lured and transported toward the Thai–Myanmar border. A single advisory has once again exposed Thailand's "China anxiety" in tourism.

## The embassy advisory: the weight of a notice

For a Chinese embassy to issue a travel-safety warning about a friendly neighbouring country is itself a telling signal. The wording was blunt: citizens were told to beware "easy money" and "high-paying job" recruitment traps, use licensed transport, decline free rides from strangers or even acquaintances, and think twice before travelling to the Thai–Myanmar border or remote areas. In other words, the risk was located precisely on the chain of "enter as a tourist, then be trafficked to border scam compounds."

This is not the first such warning in Southeast Asia. Only weeks earlier the Chinese Embassy in the Philippines issued a similar alert after kidnapping cases. The common thread is a criminal model — lure with fake jobs, move across borders, force victims into telecom fraud — that is shifting from sporadic to structural. When an embassy must publicly tell its own citizens "don't trust a free ride from an acquaintance," the problem is no longer isolated crime but a systemic crisis of trust.

## Thailand's tourism "China dependency"

Why is Bangkok nervous? The data tells the story. China was long Thailand's single largest source of foreign visitors. Yet safety fears are pulling that foundation away: the Association of Thai Travel Agents (ATTA) has cut its 2026 Chinese-visitor forecast from 9 million to 7 million, while the Tourism Authority of Thailand (TAT) puts the official figure even lower, at about 5.1 million — a recovery from 2025's roughly 4.47 million but still far below the 2019 peak.

The trend contrast is sharper still. In 2025 total foreign arrivals to Thailand were about 32.9 million, down 7.23% year on year — the first decline in a decade outside the pandemic — with Chinese visitors down more than 60% from 2019. Meanwhile Vietnam, Malaysia and South Korea are racing to capture Chinese demand. Thai operators privately concede that the country "lost a third of its Chinese visitors and has not got them back." Industry estimates put China's 2026 outbound travel at roughly 179 million trips and about US$258 billion in spending — a giant pool whose flow is being reshuffled by the safety narrative.

## The "transit hub" dilemma: visa-free loopholes and porous borders

Minister Sorasak rushed to reassure, yet his words betrayed the awkwardness. He insisted Thailand would "never allow itself to become a transit station for transnational crime," but also conceded that those involved "had often already established contact with criminal groups in their home country," and claimed the safety problem "seems to appear only among Chinese tourists." The explanation reads less as clarification than as deflection — pushing the blame back to "deceived before departure."

Reality is messier. Thailand's 60-day visa-free policy, launched in 2024 to spur recovery, was found by security agencies to harbour a loophole — foreigners entering as tourists and using Thailand as a base for transnational crime — and was later tightened to 30 days. Thailand's long, weakly controlled border with Myanmar, combined with scandals of complicit officers (in May, five police in Sa Kaeo province staged a fake raid to kidnap and extort five Chinese nationals), together breed the "transit hub." Thai authorities have already raided tourist areas such as Phuket, seizing cannabis products worth more than 500,000 baht (about US$14,000). The minister says ordinary tourists won't be dragged into vehicles; that may be true. But what terrifies Chinese families is the relative who "voluntarily" entered and then vanished.

## The social-media amplification effect

In China, bad news travels fast. Any short video on Weibo or Douyin about a Chinese national "missing in Thailand" or "kidnapped" goes viral within hours. Even when cases are rare and mostly targeted deception, the headline alone is enough to make a family rethink its plans. Thai operators call it the "chilling effect": once a destination is tagged "unsafe" on Chinese social media, image repair is far harder than data recovery.

That is precisely why Sorasak went on camera himself. For Thailand, what is at stake is not a handful of tourists but the confidence of the entire Chinese market. When safety becomes the first variable in outbound decisions, no amount of tourism promotion can substitute.

## Consular protection: from passive to proactive

Zoom out to the diplomatic level, and the advisory reveals a shift in China's consular-protection posture. In the past, protection was largely post-incident negotiation. Today, issuing advance risk warnings and actively shaping outbound-safety expectations has become routine. This "proactive consular protection" is both a duty of care and, objectively, a soft lever that turns "whether my citizens are safe on your soil" into a bargaining chip with the host state.

For Southeast Asian states, this is a new test: in competing for Chinese tourists, the contest is no longer only price and attractions but the baseline promise that "your citizens are safe on my territory." Thailand's high-profile response is a miniature of that competition.

## A regional scramble: who is catching the diverted Chinese traveller

Safety fears compounded by rising travel costs are pushing Chinese tourists elsewhere. South Korea expects about 7 million Chinese visitors this year; Vietnam's inbound tourism market is up 13.8% year on year; Malaysia is courting Muslim Chinese travellers and launching direct Urumqi flights. If Thailand cannot stop the bleeding in its safety narrative, its status as the region's tourism hub will loosen further.

In the end, whether Sorasak's reassurance works depends not on rhetoric but on whether Thailand can truly seal the cracks at its borders and in its visa policy, and clean the bad apples from its law-enforcement ranks. Otherwise, no amount of messaging will stop Chinese families from voting with their feet.

## Further reading

**Consular Protection**: The practical mechanism by which a state's missions abroad safeguard the legitimate rights of their citizens under international law and bilateral treaties; China has in recent years shifted in Southeast Asia from "post-incident negotiation" to "pre-incident warning."

**Telecom Fraud**: A criminal model using fake recruitment and high-salary jobs to lure victims across borders into scam compounds and force them into illegal activity — now a core challenge for Southeast Asia's transnational governance.

**Transnational Crime**: Organised criminal activity crossing borders; Thailand's geography and visa policy have repeatedly been exploited as a "transit hub" for human trafficking and telecom fraud.
