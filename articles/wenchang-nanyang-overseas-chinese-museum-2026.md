# 文昌南洋华侨馆开馆：小镇里的南洋百年记忆

## 一座小镇博物馆的开张

10月29日，海南文昌龙楼镇多了一处新地标：文昌南洋华侨馆暨迈号咖啡非遗工坊正式开馆。文昌市侨联把侨界代表请到现场，以馆为媒，共叙侨史、共话侨情。据中国侨网（China Qiao Wang）报道，这座馆由中国绿发投资集团有限公司打造，建筑面积198平方米，体量不大，却把文昌的侨乡往事收拢进四个板块——「过番谋生」「垦殖南洋」「回报桑梓」「特色骑楼」，顺着时间脉络一一铺开。

一座镇级博物馆的开张，放在全国新闻里不过是一条软消息。但把它放进近年中国侨乡此起彼伏的「建馆热」里看，味道就不同了：从闽南的侨批文物馆到海南的南洋华侨馆，散落在县域、乡镇的侨史空间正被一间间点亮。文昌这一步，看似小，却踩在了一个清晰的趋势上。

## 过番：文昌人下南洋的百年路

「过番」是海南话里远渡南洋谋生的说法。馆内泛黄的史料与实物展品，还原的正是一代代文昌先辈漂洋过海、在异乡打拼的艰辛，也记录了他们立业之后不忘故土、捐资兴学、建设家乡的故事。这并非文昌一地的孤例——海南向来是知名侨乡，而文昌、海口、琼海一带的乡亲，尤其密集地流向了新加坡、马来西亚（Malaysia）、泰国（Thailand）、印尼（Indonesia）与越南（Vietnam）。

换句话说，南洋不是文昌人记忆里的远方，而是家族叙事的起点。一座198平方米的展馆，要承载的其实是横跨一个多世纪的离散经验：出洋时的不舍、谋生时的苛苦、扎根后的乡愁，以及衣锦还乡时修桥铺路、兴学育才的回馈。把这些都装进四面墙里，难度不小，但方向是对的——侨史若只停留在县志的寥寥数行，迟早会被遗忘。

## 一杯「歌碧欧」里的南洋余味

这座馆的特别之处，在于它不只用展板讲历史，还顺带开了一间咖啡非遗工坊。这不是闲笔。史料记载，早在1898年，远赴南洋谋生的文昌华侨返乡，带回咖啡种子与炒制技艺，在迈号、南阳一带落地培育，就此开启海南本土咖啡的百年历程。百余年间，当地民众守住柴火炒豆、纱布滤咖的古法，把南洋风味浓郁的「歌碧欧」饮咖习俗代代相传。

「歌碧欧」三个字，本身就是一段活着的迁徙史。它脱胎于马来语的「Kopi-o（黑咖啡）」，是海南人在南洋学会、又带回故乡的味觉记忆。一杯咖啡，从南洋的苦涩打拼，熬成了侨乡独有的烟火符号，成了联结海内外文昌侨胞的温情纽带。把咖啡工坊嵌进华侨馆，等于把抽象的「侨史」变成可品尝、可体验的感官记忆——这比单纯挂几张老照片高明得多。

## 从记忆收藏到情感联结

开馆现场，文昌市侨联相关负责人表态：要用好这一特色平台，持续挖掘、保护、活化本土侨文化资源，依托侨史展品、侨批故事、咖啡等载体，常态化举办侨文化交流体验活动，讲好有温度、接地气的文昌侨乡故事。

这句话值得玩味。过去，侨乡对侨史的处理多半是「收藏」——修馆、存物、立碑，重在保存。如今的关键词变成了「活化」与「联结」。原因不难理解：真正漂洋过海的那一代人正在老去，而生长在异国的华裔新生代，很多已不会讲方言、读不懂中文。要让侨乡记忆传下去，靠的不是档案馆里的卷宗，而是能让人「身临其境」的体验。咖啡、骑楼、侨批故事，恰好都是门槛低、代入感强的载体。

## 侨乡记忆的「实体化」竞赛

把视野再放宽，文昌南洋华侨馆的开馆，其实是全国各地侨乡一场静悄悄「实体化竞赛」的缩影。近年来，从福建土楼边的侨批馆，到浙江青田的侨乡博物馆，再到海南文昌的南洋华侨馆，县域层面的侨史空间密集落地。背后的动力并不单纯是怀旧：在地方发展语境里，侨乡文化既是需要保护的非物质遗产，也是能引来侨资、侨智与文旅消费的资产。

这当然有积极的一面。把离散族群的故事安放进实体空间，本身就是对流散记忆的一种郑重对待；而体验式、生活化的叙事，也比口号式的宣传更能打动年轻一代侨胞。但也要提个醒：若只把博物馆当成领导剪彩、游客打卡的「政绩盆景」，热闹一阵便门可罗雀，那再漂亮的展馆也不过是又一处闲置资产。文昌这次把咖啡工坊一并做起来，至少说明当地想得很务实——让记忆能「喝」得出来，才算真正活了。

## 延伸阅读

**侨批（Qiaopi）**：近代中国东南沿海侨胞通过民间渠道寄回国内的家书与汇款凭证，被誉为「侨史敦煌」，是研究中囯与东南亚（Southeast Asia）离散网络的一手史料。

**南洋（Nanyang）**：明清以来汉语对东南亚（尤其新马泰印汶越）的旧称，承载着下南洋移民的集体记忆，至今仍是华人离散叙事里的核心意象。

**歌碧欧（Kopi-o）**：源自马来语「黑咖啡」的海南话音译，是海南侨乡与南洋之间味觉与语言双重交融的活化石。

# Wenchang's Nanyang Overseas Chinese Museum Opens: A Century of Diaspora Memory in a Small Town

## A museum opens in a small town

On 29 October, the town of Longlou in Wenchang, Hainan, gained a new landmark: the Wenchang Nanyang Overseas Chinese Museum, together with a Maihao Coffee intangible-heritage workshop, officially opened its doors. The Wenchang Federation of Returned Overseas Chinese brought diaspora representatives to the site, using the museum as a medium to recount migration history and reconnect emotional ties. According to China Qiaowang, the museum was built by China Green Development Investment Group and covers 198 square metres. Modest in size, it nonetheless gathers Wenchang's qiaoxiang (emigrant-hometown) past into four exhibition zones — "Crossing the Seas to Make a Living", "Tilling the Nanyang", "Giving Back to the Homeland", and "The Characteristic Qilou Arcades" — laid out along a chronological thread.

The opening of a township-level museum is, in the national news cycle, little more than a soft item. Yet placed against the wave of "museum-building" now sweeping China's emigrant hometowns — from the qiaopi (remittance-letter) archives of southern Fujian to the Nanyang museums of Hainan — the move takes on a different flavour. Scattered across counties and townships, these spaces of diaspora memory are being lit up one by one. Wenchang's step may look small, but it lands squarely on a clear trend.

## "Guo Fan": a century on the Nanyang route

"Guo Fan" is the Hainanese phrase for voyaging to Nanyang (Southeast Asia) to seek a living. Inside the museum, yellowed documents and physical artefacts reconstruct exactly that journey: generation after generation of Wenchang forebears crossing the ocean to toil in foreign lands, and then, having made their fortune, remembering home — donating to schools, building their hometowns. This is no isolated Wenchang story. Hainan has long been a renowned qiaoxiang, and the people of Wenchang, Haikou and Qionghai in particular flowed densely into Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Vietnam.

In other words, Nanyang is not a distant horizon in Wenchang's memory but the very starting point of its family narratives. A 198-square-metre hall is asked to hold more than a century of diasporic experience: the reluctance of departure, the harshness of making a living, the homesickness of taking root abroad, and the repayments — bridges, roads, schools — made upon returning in glory. Fitting all that within four walls is no small task, but the direction is right. If emigration history lingers only in a few lines of a county gazetteer, it will sooner or later be forgotten.

## The aftertaste of "Kopi-o" in a cup

What makes this museum distinctive is that it does not tell history solely through panels; it also opens a coffee heritage workshop. This is no digression. Historical records show that as early as 1898, Wenchang emigrants returning from Nanyang brought back coffee seeds and roasting techniques, cultivating them around Maihao and Nanyang and thus launching Hainan's century-long indigenous coffee tradition. For over a hundred years, locals have preserved the old method — roasting beans over firewood, filtering through cloth — passing down the strongly Nanyang-flavoured "Gebi'ou" coffee custom from generation to generation.

The three characters "Gebi'ou" are themselves a living migration history. Derived from the Malay "Kopi-o" (black coffee), it is a taste memory that Hainanese learnt in Nanyang and carried home. A cup of coffee, distilled from the bitter struggle of Nanyang into a unique everyday symbol of the emigrant hometown, becomes a warm tie linking Wenchang compatriots at home and abroad. Embedding the coffee workshop inside the overseas-Chinese museum turns abstract "emigration history" into a sensory memory that can be tasted and experienced — far cleverer than simply hanging a few old photographs.

## From memory-keeping to emotional bonds

At the opening, an official from the Wenchang Federation of Returned Overseas Chinese pledged to make good use of this distinctive platform — continuously excavating, protecting and revitalising local qiaoxiang cultural resources, and using emigration exhibits, qiaopi stories and coffee as vehicles to hold regular cultural-exchange and experience activities, telling Wenchang's emigrant-hometown stories with warmth and a down-to-earth relatability.

That wording deserves a pause. In the past, qiaoxiang treated their history mostly by "preserving" it — building halls, storing objects, erecting monuments, with the emphasis on safekeeping. Today the keywords have shifted to "revitalisation" and "connection". The reason is not hard to see: the generation that actually crossed the ocean is ageing, while the new generations of ethnic Chinese raised abroad often no longer speak the dialect or read Chinese. To pass diaspora memory down, what is needed is not archives in a filing cabinet but experiences that let people "step inside". Coffee, qilou arcades and qiaopi stories are exactly the kind of low-threshold, highly immersive vehicles that work.

## The "materialisation" race of qiaoxiang memory

Zoom out, and the Wenchang museum's opening is a microcosm of a quiet "materialisation race" unfolding across China's emigrant hometowns. In recent years, from the qiaopi halls beside Fujian's earthen buildings to the qiaoxiang museums of Zhejiang's Qingtian and now the Nanyang museum of Hainan's Wenchang, diaspora-history spaces have been landing at the county level with unusual density. The drive behind this is not pure nostalgia: in the language of local development, qiaoxiang culture is both intangible heritage to be protected and an asset capable of attracting qiao capital (overseas-Chinese investment), overseas Chinese talent and cultural-tourism consumption.

There is, of course, a positive side. Anchoring a dispersed community's stories in physical space is itself a deliberate act of respect for scattered memory; and an experiential, lived-in narrative moves younger overseas Chinese far more than slogan-like propaganda. But a caveat is in order: if a museum is treated merely as a "political bonsai" for ribbon-cuttings and tourist photo-ops, bustling for a spell and then deserted, even the most beautiful hall becomes yet another idle asset. That Wenchang bundled the coffee workshop into the plan at least shows the locality is thinking pragmatically — only when memory can be "drunk" does it truly come alive.

## Further reading

**Qiaopi (侨批):** Remittance letters with money sent home by emigrants from China's southeastern coast through private channels in the modern era, hailed as the "Dunhuang of overseas-Chinese history" and a primary source for studying China–Southeast Asia diaspora networks.

**Nanyang (南洋):** The old Chinese term for Southeast Asia (especially Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Vietnam) from the Ming–Qing period onward, carrying the collective memory of migration "down to the Nanyang" and still a core image in Chinese diaspora narratives.

**Kopi-o (歌碧欧):** A Hainanese transliteration of the Malay "black coffee", a living fossil of the dual culinary and linguistic fusion between Hainan's emigrant hometowns and Nanyang.
