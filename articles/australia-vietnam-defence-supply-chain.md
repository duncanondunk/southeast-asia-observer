# 澳越深化军事合作：供应链韧性背后的南海棋局

8月11日，越南国家主席苏林（To Lam）首次以越共中央总书记兼国家主席的双重身份访问澳大利亚，与澳洲总理阿尔巴尼斯（Anthony Albanese）在堪培拉举行会谈。两国同意深化军事合作、支持关键贸易自由流通，并对南中国海（South China Sea）局势表达关切。这份看似常规的联合声明，实则释放出三重信号：防务关系升级、供应链绑定加深、以及在中美博弈的夹缝中，河内与堪培拉各自为"战略自主"落子。对东南亚观察者而言，澳越走近不是孤立事件，而是印太地缘板块重组的一个注脚。

## 一次访问，三重信号

此次访问的标志性，首先在于苏林的身份——集最高党、国职务于一身出访，本身就传递出越南对澳关系的权重。会谈成果也超出一般礼仪：双方同意进一步加强防务与安全合作，同时加强打击跨国有组织犯罪与人口走私。两国领导人共同见证了越南海岸警卫队与澳洲边防部队交换海上执法合作谅解备忘录，并签署另一份边防力量合作与互助备忘录。

更值得注意的是措辞的同步。双方对"扰乱全球供应链的政策"表达关切，并承诺在关键矿产、半导体、清洁能源等领域建立具韧性的供应链。这种把安全议题与经济议题打包表述的方式，正是近年来中等国家应对大国博弈的典型话术：用"韧性"与"规则"对冲单极冲击。

## 供应链韧性：从矿产到半导体

把供应链写入防务联合声明，是本次访问最务实的一笔。越南拥有稀土等关键信息矿产的禀赋，澳洲则在关键矿产加工与清洁能源技术上有积累，二者互补性强。在半导体与清洁能源成为战略资源的当下，澳越绑定有助于双方减少对单一市场的依赖。

但"韧性"二字也暴露出脆弱。全球供应链的扰动，源头往往在大国关税与出口管制；中等国家再怎么抱团，也难以完全隔绝外部冲击。因此澳越的供应链合作，更像是一种"减震器"而非"替代项"——它降低风险敞口，却无法消除系统性波动。对越南而言，借澳洲的技术与资金提升自身在产业链中的位置，同时避免被单一强国锁定，才是务实算计。

## 南海的共同话术与各自算计

南海是绕不开的议题。声明重申维护和平稳定、航行与飞越自由、合法商业活动不受阻碍，并呼吁依据国际法以和平方式解决争端、不以武力相威胁或使用武力。这套表述几乎是对《联合国海洋法公约》与"东盟—中国南海行为准则"共识的复诵，无过错，却也各取所需。

对澳大利亚，南海是其"印太战略"的前沿：澳洲定期在南海部署军舰与侦察机，今年4月更与加拿大、美国海军举行多边联合航行与战术行动，宣示自由开放。对越南，南海是直接的主权与资源利益所在，既要借外部力量平衡压力，又不愿被绑上大国的战车、破坏与邻国的微妙关系。苏林所说的"加强对话、建立互信"，恰是河内一贯的平衡术：在主权上不让步，在阵营上不选边。

## 超越安全：教育与经贸的黏合

若只看安全，会低估澳越关系的厚度。声明还包括越南恢复进口澳洲袋鼠肉与鹿肉，以及墨尔本皇家理工大学（RMIT University）扩大在越业务——计划在河内（Hanoi）兴建新校区，并扩大胡志明市（Ho Chi Minh City）的运营规模。目前双边贸易额已超过140亿美元（约180亿新元，约合1000亿元人民币），澳洲也是越南官方发展援助的重要来源国。

这些看似细碎的经贸与人文安排，恰恰是关系韧性的真正底座。当安全合作仍受战略疑虑约束时，教育、贸易与援助构成了日常而稳定的黏合层，使政治风向转变时关系不至于脱钩。对东南亚研究者来说，澳越案例提示了一个被忽视的维度：中等国家之间的"战略靠近"，往往不是靠一纸军事协议，而是靠长年累月的教育、贸易与人员往来织成的网。

## 延伸阅读

**苏林（To Lam）**：越南最高领导人，2024年起任越共中央总书记，2026年兼任国家主席，此访为其以双重身份首访澳大利亚。

**关键矿产（critical minerals）**：稀土、锂、镍等对国家安全和清洁能源至关重要的资源，近年成为大国博弈与供应链"韧性"建设的核心对象。

**印太战略（Indo-Pacific strategy）**：美国及其盟友以"自由开放的印太"为框架、强化海上安全与伙伴网络的地缘布局，澳洲与越南均在不同层面参与。

# Australia and Vietnam Deepen Defence Ties

On 11 August, Vietnamese President To Lam visited Australia for the first time in his dual capacity as General Secretary of the Communist Party and State President, holding talks with Prime Minister Anthony Albanese in Canberra. The two sides agreed to deepen military cooperation, support the free flow of critical trade, and voiced concern over the South China Sea. A seemingly routine joint statement in fact sends three signals: an upgrade in defence ties, tighter supply-chain binding, and — amid US-China rivalry — Hanoi's and Canberra's separate moves toward "strategic autonomy." For observers of Southeast Asia, the Australia–Vietnam rapprochement is no isolated event but a footnote to the reorganisation of the Indo-Pacific plate.

## One visit, three signals

The visit's symbolism begins with To Lam's status: travelling abroad at the head of both party and state conveys the weight Hanoi places on the relationship. The outcomes also went beyond ceremony — the two sides agreed to further strengthen defence and security cooperation while stepping up the fight against transnational crime and people smuggling. Leaders jointly witnessed the exchange of a maritime-law-enforcement memorandum between Vietnam's Coast Guard and Australia's Border Force, and signed a second on border-force cooperation and mutual assistance.

What stands out is the synchronised wording. Both expressed concern over policies "disrupting global supply chains" and pledged resilient supply chains in critical minerals, semiconductors and clean energy. Packaging security and economic issues together is the typical lexicon of middle powers navigating great-power competition: using "resilience" and "rules" to hedge against unilateral shocks.

## Supply-chain resilience

Writing supply chains into a defence joint statement is the most pragmatic stroke of the visit. Vietnam holds endowments of critical minerals such as rare earths; Australia brings processing capacity and clean-energy technology — a strong complementarity. As semiconductors and clean energy turn strategic, Australia–Vietnam binding helps both reduce dependence on a single market.

Yet the word "resilience" also exposes fragility. The source of global supply-chain disruption usually lies in great-power tariffs and export controls; no amount of middle-power clustering can fully insulate against it. Australia–Vietnam supply-chain cooperation is thus more a "shock absorber" than a "substitute" — it lowers exposure but cannot eliminate systemic volatility. For Vietnam, the pragmatic calculus is to use Australian technology and capital to climb the value chain while avoiding lock-in to any single power.

## The South China Sea script

The South China Sea is the unavoidable item. The statement reaffirmed peace, stability, freedom of navigation and overflight, unhindered lawful commercial activity, and settlement of disputes by law and diplomacy without threat or use of force. The phrasing is almost a recitation of UNCLOS and the ASEAN–China consensus — faultless, yet each side takes what it needs.

For Australia, the Sea is the frontier of its Indo-Pacific strategy: it routinely deploys warships and surveillance aircraft there, and this April joined Canadian and US naval forces in multilateral sailing and tactical drills to assert a free and open Indo-Pacific. For Vietnam, the Sea is a direct sovereign and resource interest — it wants external balancing without being harnessed to a great power's chariot or wrecking delicate ties with neighbours. To Lam's call to "strengthen dialogue and build mutual trust" is Hanoi's perennial balancing act: no concession on sovereignty, no side-taking in camps.

## Beyond security

Reading only the security angle understates the relationship's depth. The statement also covered Vietnam's resumption of Australian kangaroo and venison imports, and RMIT University's expansion in Vietnam — a new campus in Hanoi and enlarged operations in Ho Chi Minh City. Bilateral trade already exceeds US$14 billion (about S$18 billion), and Australia is a major source of official development assistance to Vietnam.

These seemingly minor economic and people-to-people arrangements are the true base of resilience. When security cooperation remains constrained by strategic suspicion, education, trade and aid form a daily, stable adhesive layer that keeps the relationship from decoupling when political winds shift. For Southeast Asia watchers, the Australia–Vietnam case highlights a neglected dimension: the "strategic proximity" of middle powers rests less on a military pact than on a web woven year after year through education, trade and human exchange.

## Further reading

**To Lam**: Vietnam's top leader, General Secretary of the Communist Party since 2024 and State President from 2026; this visit was his first to Australia in the dual role.

**Critical minerals**: Resources such as rare earths, lithium and nickel vital to national security and clean energy, central to great-power rivalry and "resilient" supply-chain building.

**Indo-Pacific strategy**: The US-led framework of a "free and open Indo-Pacific" that reinforces maritime security and partner networks; both Australia and Vietnam engage it at different levels.
