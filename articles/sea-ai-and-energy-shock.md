# 训完AI反被取代，又遇能源震荡：东南亚的“不对称”跃迁

> 菲律宾员工亲手训练了取代自己的模型；中东战事推高能源风险，东南亚加速转向太阳能。这是同一个结构性故事的两种面孔。

本周东南亚两条新闻，合在一起说的是同一件事：这个地区在全球经济里"向上供给、向下承受"的不对称位置。

## 训练了取代自己的人

菲律宾员工参与训练一套人工智能系统，随后却被裁撤——用他们自己的话说，像"自掘坟墓"。这句话不只是吐槽，它点出了全球 AI 经济的核心悖论：最有价值的"人类判断"被外包给低工资合同工，而模型成熟后，这批人又被自己训练出的工具淘汰。

## 从客服外包到"AI 就绪"

这并非菲律宾劳动力第一次既当引擎又当代价。此前的 BPO（业务流程外包）繁荣靠的是英语流利与低成本；如今同一比较优势正被它帮忙训练的技术自动化。

## 来自远方的能源震荡

另一端，中东战事推高能源风险，对进口油气依赖深的东南亚构成直接压力。转向太阳能，既是气候叙事，也是战略自主的赌注。

## 不对称是主线

两条新闻的共同点是"不对称整合"：东南亚向外供给劳动、向内消费别处生产的技术与能源，外部的震荡在本地落地。地区的能动性真实存在，却始终在它无法完全掌控的结构里运作。

## 结语

东南亚的"跃迁"不是线性的。它能铺太阳能、能给工人再培训，但它的繁荣始终带点倾斜——这正是观察这片区域时最该记住的视角。

# Training the Replacements: AI Labor and the Energy Shock in Southeast Asia's Lopsided Leap

> Filipino workers who trained the models that replaced them, and economies racing to solar as Gulf energy wavers — two faces of a region's uneven integration into the global economy.

Two items on the Southeast Asia desk this week, read side by side, tell a single story. One is intimate and human: employees in the Philippines who helped train an artificial-intelligence system and were then laid off, describing the experience, in their own words, as "like digging one's own grave." The other is macroeconomic: as conflict in the Middle East raises energy risk, Southeast Asian states are accelerating their turn to solar power. Separate headlines, same underlying logic — a region that supplies labor and consumes technology and energy produced elsewhere, and feels the shocks of both arriving from abroad.

## The Workers Who Trained Their Own Replacement

The Filipino story is a synecdoche for the global division of digital labor. The "human judgment" behind modern AI — the labeling, rating, and reinforcement learning that turns raw models into usable products — is overwhelmingly contracted out to low-wage workers in the Global South. When those workers are then dismissed as the models mature, the arrangement reveals its structural irony: the very labor that created the system's value is simultaneously devalued and made redundant by it.

This is not a glitch; it is the business model. Value is captured upstream, in Silicon Valley and its peers, where the models are owned. Costs and displacement are borne downstream, in places like Metro Manila and Cebu, where the annotation happens. The worker's "digging one's own grave" is analytically exact: they trained the tool that unemployed them.

## From BPO to "AI-Ready": A Familiar Comparative Advantage, Automated

This is not the first time Philippine labor has been both the engine and the casualty of a technology wave. The business-process outsourcing (BPO) boom of the 2000s and 2010s built entire metro economies on two advantages — English fluency and low labor cost. The AI wave is the next phase of that same story. The comparative advantage that anchored the BPO sector is now being automated by the very technology those workers helped train.

What is new is the speed and the self-referential loop. Call-center work was displaced by software that took years to mature; the annotation work that feeds AI is displaced by the model it improves, sometimes within the same contract. The region's position in the global tech chain is not just low; it is recursively undercut.

## The Energy Shock Arrives From Elsewhere

The second item operates on a different scale but the identical mechanism. Southeast Asian economies are heavily exposed to imported energy; Gulf instability transmits almost immediately to their current accounts and household bills. The pivot to solar is therefore never only a climate story. It is a hedging story — a way to substitute a volatile, geopolitically exposed import with a domestically generated flow.

Yet here too the autonomy is partial. The solar supply chain — polysilicon, wafers, cells, modules — is itself extraordinarily concentrated, with Chinese manufacturing dominating every stage. A state that swaps Gulf crude for Chinese panels has not escaped dependency; it has substituted one for another. The direction of the shock changes, not its foreign origin.

## Solar as Strategy, Not Just Climate

It is worth taking the region's agency seriously rather than casting it as mere victim. Indonesia's nickel-to-EV bet, Vietnam's manufacturing relocation, and the regional solar buildout are all active attempts to renegotiate terms. Solar, in this reading, is not surrender to climate pressure but a strategic wager on insulation from external volatility. The point is not that Southeast Asia is powerless — it is that its power is exercised within structures it did not design.

## Asymmetry Is the Through-Line

Seen together, the two stories share a grammar. Labor flows out as cheap, trainable input; technology and energy flow in as finished, priced goods. Shocks — a model that learns too well, a war across the world — land locally, on workers and on budgets. The "leap" everyone celebrates is lopsided by construction: upward integration on unfavorable terms.

None of this justifies fatalism. The region's demographic weight, its manufacturing catch-up, and its renewable endowments are real assets. But the two headlines are a useful corrective to the boosterism that surrounds "Southeast Asia's century." A boom that trains its own replacements and imports its own power is a boom with strings attached.

## Conclusion

The Filipino worker who trained the model that replaced him, and the finance minister watching Gulf energy prices, are participants in the same plot. Southeast Asia's integration into the global economy is deep and growing — but it remains asymmetric, and the asymmetries are precisely where the risks, and the region's room to maneuver, now live.
