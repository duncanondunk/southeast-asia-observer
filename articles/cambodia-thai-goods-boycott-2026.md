# 柬埔寨“拒泰货”浪潮：一场边境冲突如何烧进老百姓的菜篮子

## 导火索：边境之战溢出到购物车

自今年7月柬埔寨与泰国在争议边境交火以来，柬埔寨市场上出现了一桩耐人寻味的变化：消费者开始有意把泰国品牌留在货架上不买。两个东盟邻国之间的边境对峙，在短短几周内竟演化为一场带有真实经济后果的消费者抵制。这值得停下来想一想它释放的信号。在东南亚的许多地方，经济民族主义过去只是政客与社论作者口中的话术；如今它径直探入寻常人家的菜篮子——碗里的米粉、杯中的牛奶、洗发水的牌子，都成了立场的表达。柬埔寨的案例表明，外交裂痕可以多么迅速地从边境传导到餐桌，普通民众又多么轻易地把地缘怨气转化为一次购买决策。

## 数字：泰货进口跌37%，国货份额冲到30%

这组数字绝非无足轻重。据《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》报道，2026年前七个月，柬埔寨自泰国的进口下降了37%；同一窗口期内，柬埔寨本土制造的商品占国内市场的份额，从区区2%至3%一跃升至约30%。数以千计的新产品被推向市场，数百家新工厂开张，只为填补泰国供应商曾经满足的需求。对于一个长期存在贸易逆差、日常生活消费品高度依赖邻国的经济体而言，一条主要供给线在七个月内收缩37%，通常只会出现在经济衰退或货币危机之中。而这一次的不同之处在于，这是一道自己施加的伤口——以民族团结之名主动献上的经济牺牲。这份代价是否配得上它所象征的政治意义，最终要由柬埔寨的家庭而非将军们来回答。

## 本土赢家：Mee Chiet、Kirisu 与官方的“柬埔寨制造”动员

少数本土企业成了直接的受益者。柬埔寨本土的米粉生产商 Mee Chiet 与乳制品企业 Kirisu Farm 争相填补泰国品牌腾出的货架。在它们身后，是一轮由政府背书的“柬埔寨制造”（MadeInCambodia）运动，它把购买国货从一种消费偏好重新定义为公民义务。我们不必嫉妒柬埔寨产业夺得它长期拱手让给更便宜、更成熟的泰国产品的内需。然而，这场替代的速度所反映的，与其说是赢得的竞争力，不如说是被强制释放的需求。搭着边境战争顺风车而来的产业政策，其脆弱是注定的：边境一旦平息，泰国供应商在价格与品质上的优势就会重新显现，那层爱国的溢价也会随之消散。靠爱国情怀的补贴，换不来生产力的真正提升。

## 暗面：伪国货与名不副实的替代

这场繁荣也有肮脏的一面。柬埔寨媒体与《日经亚洲》的报道都提到，冒充“柬埔寨制造”的伪劣商品骤增——不少产品只是换了标签、伪装成本地货——与此同时，来自第三国的进口竞争也在升温。换句话说，部分“柬埔寨制造”的暴涨，只是名义上的替代。这是抵制型经济反复出现的悖论：举着国货大旗，底下藏着的往往是最古老的商业本能——套利。当爱国主义被发了一张营销许可证，那位以为自己在捍卫边境的消费者，或许只是在为一款从来就不是本地出产的商品多付了钱。对金边而言，风险在于这场运动的信誉恰恰会在它“成功”之时被掏空。

## 区域规律，而非常态化的情绪宣泄

柬埔寨绝非孤例。只要政治一转冷，经济民族主义就会在区域内轮回——从印尼的棕榈油与资源主权主义，到越南民众对中国投资的抵触，再到泰国与马来西亚间歇性的排外资本情绪，皆是一脉相承。柬埔寨的抵制，最好被理解为区域惯习的最新一例，而非一次性的情绪宣泄。对区域内各国政府而言，一个让人不安的教训是：边境争端若局限于前线，其危害尚且可控；一旦被武器化、搬进超市货架，它腐蚀的便是维系东南亚一代人和平与繁荣的经济相互依存。一场抵制可以凭一篇演说瞬间点燃；而它被焚毁的商业信任，却要数十年才能重建。对于两个共享边界、语言与一条河流的邻国来说，这比任何一车泰国米粉都要沉重。

## 延伸阅读

**经济民族主义（Economic Nationalism）**：以国家利益为名，将消费、投资与贸易选择政治化、道德化的倾向；在东南亚多随边境争端或外交降温而升温，往往以牺牲经济效率换取象征性自主。

**进口替代（Import Substitution）**：以本土产能取代进口商品的产业政策；柬泰抵制中的“柬埔寨制造”运动即其一例，但其可持续性取决于真实生产力，而非爱国溢价。

**柬泰关系（Cambodia–Thailand Relations）**：两国共享边界、语言与湄公河水系，亦长期存在边境划界与主权争议；2026年的冲突外溢至民间消费领域，是双边关系脆弱性的又一侧影。

# Cambodia's Thai-goods boycott: how a border clash spilled into the shopping cart

## The trigger: a border war spills into the shopping cart

Since Cambodia and Thailand exchanged fire along their disputed frontier in July, something telling has unfolded in Cambodian markets. Consumers are deliberately leaving Thai brands on the shelf. A border confrontation between two ASEAN neighbours has, in a matter of weeks, mutated into a consumer boycott with measurable economic consequences. It is worth pausing on what this signals. Across much of Southeast Asia, economic nationalism used to be the language of politicians and editorial writers. Today it reaches directly into the household basket — the noodles, the milk, the shampoo. The Cambodian case shows how quickly a diplomatic rupture can be transmitted from the frontier to the dinner table, and how readily ordinary citizens convert a geopolitical grievance into a purchasing decision.

## The numbers: Thai imports down 37%, local share to 30%

The figures are not marginal. Nikkei Asia reports that Thai imports into Cambodia dropped 37% in the first seven months of 2026. Over the same window, Cambodian-made goods leapt from a 2–3% sliver of the domestic market to roughly 30%. Thousands of new products have been introduced and hundreds of new factories opened to satisfy demand that Thai suppliers once met. For an economy that runs a persistent trade deficit and leans on neighbours for everyday consumer staples, a 37% contraction in a single major supply line within seven months resembles the kind of shock normally triggered by a recession or a currency crisis. The difference is that this wound is self-inflicted — a deliberate economic sacrifice offered up in the name of national solidarity. Whether the pain is worth the symbolism is a question Cambodian households, not generals, will ultimately answer.

## Local champions: Mee Chiet, Kirisu and the state's "#MadeInCambodia" push

A small group of domestic companies are the immediate beneficiaries. Mee Chiet, a Cambodian noodle manufacturer, and Kirisu Farm, a dairy producer, have scrambled to occupy the shelf space Thai brands vacated. Lending them cover is a government-led "#MadeInCambodia" campaign that recasts buying local not as a consumer preference but as a civic obligation. One should not begrudge Cambodian industry the opportunity to capture demand it long ceded to cheaper, better-established Thai products. Yet the velocity of the substitution reflects forced demand more than earned competitiveness. Industrial policy that rides on the back of a border war is fragile by definition: the moment the frontier calms, the price and quality advantages of Thai suppliers reassert themselves, and the patriotic premium evaporates. Subsidising patriotism is no substitute for building productivity.

## The underside: counterfeits and substitution that isn't

The boom carries a seamy underside. Both Cambodian outlets and the Nikkei account note a spike in counterfeit "Cambodian" goods — products merely relabelled to appear domestic — together with rising competition from imports sourced in third countries. In plain terms, part of the "Made in Cambodia" surge is substitution in name only. This is the recurring paradox of boycott-driven economies: the flag of national products frequently conceals the most basic commercial reflex of all, arbitrage. When patriotism is handed a marketing licence, the shopper who believes she is defending the border may simply be paying extra for a product that was never local to begin with. The risk for Phnom Penh is that the campaign's credibility erodes precisely as it succeeds.

## A regional pattern, not an isolated tantrum

Cambodia is far from unique. Economic nationalism cycles through the region whenever politics sours — from Indonesian palm-oil and resource sovereignism to Vietnamese consumer pushback against Chinese investment, to episodic hostility toward foreign capital in Thailand and Malaysia. The Cambodian boycott is best read as the latest iteration of a regional reflex rather than a one-off tantrum. The uncomfortable lesson for the region's governments is this. Border disputes are damaging enough when confined to the frontier. When they are weaponised inside the supermarket, they corrode the economic interdependence that has underwritten Southeast Asia's peace and prosperity for a generation. A boycott can be switched on by a single speech; the commercial trust it incinerates can take decades to rebuild. For two neighbours who share a border, a language and a river, that is a price heavier than any single shipment of Thai noodles.

## Further reading

**Economic Nationalism**: The tendency to politicise and moralise consumption, investment and trade in the name of the national interest; across Southeast Asia it tends to rise with border clashes or diplomatic chill, often trading economic efficiency for symbolic autonomy.

**Import Substitution**: An industrial policy that replaces imported goods with domestic production; the "Made in Cambodia" campaign during the Cambodian–Thai boycott is one example, whose durability depends on real productivity rather than a patriotic premium.

**Cambodia–Thailand Relations**: The two countries share a border, a language and the Mekong river system, yet have long fought over demarcation and sovereignty; the 2026 conflict's spillover into civilian consumption is another facet of their brittle ties.
