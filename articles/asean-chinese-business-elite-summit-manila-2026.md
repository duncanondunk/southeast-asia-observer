# 第二届东盟华商菁英峰会落幕马尼拉：华商网络何以成为东盟经济的黏合剂

## 一场“华商圈”的峰会，为何落在马尼拉

10月19日，第二届东盟华商菁英峰会在菲律宾首都马尼拉举行。这场由菲律宾菲华商联总会（当地历史最久、规模最大的华人商会联合组织之一）主办的会议，把东盟各国的政商界人士聚到一起，围绕贸易、投资、旅游与区域商贸合作展开交流。菲律宾贸易和工业部部长克里斯蒂娜·罗克、参议员罗宾·帕迪拉，以及菲华商联总会理事长林荣辉等人均到场。

表面看，这不过是一场华人商会的例行论坛。但若把它放回菲律宾今年出任东盟轮值主席国的大背景里，就不难读出别样的意味：一场由族裔商会主导的经贸峰会，恰巧赶在“东道主之年”登场，本身就耐人寻味。

## 主题之外的潜台词：轮值主席年的“主场红利”

本届峰会的主题是“引领东盟前行：创新、韧性与区域协作”。这句话听上去与东盟官方文件里的措辞如出一辙，几乎可以直接抄进任何一份区域合作白皮书。但措辞的雷同恰恰说明问题——菲律宾今年的头等外交议程，正是以轮值主席身份为区域议程“定调”。

于是便出现了一个有趣的错位：决定区域话语方向的，是菲律宾政府的外交日程；而把“创新、韧性、协作”这类关键词落到商业层面的，却是一群长期扎根东南亚、彼此以族裔与方言为纽带的华人企业家。轮值主席年给了菲律宾华人商会一个难得的“主场红利”——他们不必等到政府搭台，自己就能把区域华商请到马尼拉，顺势把商会的议程与国家的区域战略叠在一起。

## 华商网络：东盟经济的“黏合剂”

峰会主办方在致辞中反复强调“区域伙伴关系”与“拥抱创新技术”，称这有助于东盟经济体应对地缘政治不确定性、经济波动、气候变化与技术变革等多重挑战。把这套话术翻译成大白话就是：在正式制度仍显薄弱的东南亚，华人商业网络是一张现成的、低交易成本的协作网。

这不是客套。从战后至今，东南亚的族群华人长期活跃于贸易、投资与产业发展一线。他们横跨国界、共享语言与文化信任，往往能在政府间协议尚未落地之前，就先以私人信任完成跨境资源配置。正因如此，华商网络常常被形容为东盟经济的“黏合剂”——它未必写在自贸协定里，却真实地在每一次跨境投资与产业合作中起作用。

## 关键词：创新、韧性与区域协作

与会代表讨论的具体议题，倒是比主题口号更接地气：创新技术应用、产业合作、跨境投资与区域市场拓展。换句话说，峰会的重心不在喊口号，而在把“商机”变为可执行的合作项目。

当晚的“东盟卓越华商之夜”用表彰优秀华商的方式，肯定这群企业家在经营、创新与社会贡献上的成绩，还穿插了菲律宾传统舞蹈与本地歌手演出。这一安排看似只是晚宴点缀，实则透露出另一层逻辑：华商群体早已不是“外来者”，而是深度嵌入各国社会、既出钱又出文化的在地力量。

## 掌声之外的隐忧：财富集中与治理透明度

当然，把华商网络捧为“区域韧性”的支柱，也要看到硬币的另一面。在印尼、马来西亚等国，族群华人掌握不成比例的经济份额，既是事实，也长期是一根敏感神经。一旦经济下行或政治极化，这种财富集中很容易被政治人物拿来当作动员符号。

更现实的挑战来自治理层面。以族裔信任为内核的商业网络，固然高效，却也天生偏爱“熟人圈子”，在透明度与公平竞争上常受诟病。当峰会高喊“韧性”与“协作”时，真正要回答的问题是：这张网络能否在保持效率的同时，向更开放的制度和更透明的规则靠拢？否则，它越强，外界的疑虑也可能越深。

## 延伸阅读

**菲华商联总会（Federation of Filipino Chinese Chambers of Commerce and Industry）**：菲律宾历史最久、规模最大的华人商会联合组织，长期在菲中经贸与民间交流中扮演桥梁角色。

**轮值主席国（ASEAN Chair）**：东盟每年由成员国轮流出任主席国，主导当年区域议程与峰会安排；菲律宾为2026年轮值主席国。

**族群华人经济角色（Ethnic-Chinese economy）**：指东南亚各国华裔群体在贸易、金融与产业领域的结构性影响力，既是增长引擎，也常伴随财富集中与社会敏感议题。

# The 2nd ASEAN Chinese Business Elite Summit in Manila: How the Ethnic-Chinese Business Network Bonds ASEAN's Economy

## A summit of the "Chinese business circle," why Manila

On 19 October 2026, the second ASEAN Chinese Business Elite Summit convened in Manila, the capital of the Philippines. Organised by the Federation of Filipino Chinese Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FFCCCII)—one of the oldest and largest ethnic-Chinese business associations in the country—the meeting brought together politicians and business leaders from across ASEAN to discuss trade, investment, tourism and regional commercial cooperation. Among those present were Philippine Secretary of Trade and Industry Cristina Roque, Senator Robin Padilla, and FFCCCII president Lim Beng Hui.

On its face, this was just another routine forum hosted by a Chinese chamber of commerce. Yet placed against the backdrop of the Philippines holding the 2026 ASEAN chairmanship, a different reading emerges: a trade summit led by an ethnic chamber happens to land in the very year its host country chairs the bloc—a coincidence worth pausing over.

## Reading between the themes: the "home-field dividend" of the chair year

The summit's theme was "Leading ASEAN Forward: Innovation, Resilience and Regional Collaboration." The phrasing is barely distinguishable from the boilerplate of any ASEAN official document and could be copied verbatim into virtually any regional cooperation white paper. But the very sameness is the point: the Philippines' top diplomatic agenda this year is, precisely, to set the tone for the regional agenda as chair.

Here lies an interesting dislocation. The direction of regional discourse is set by the Philippine government's diplomatic calendar; yet it is a group of ethnic-Chinese entrepreneurs—rooted in Southeast Asia for generations, bound to one another by ethnicity and dialect—who translate keywords like "innovation, resilience, collaboration" into the commercial realm. The chair year gives the Filipino Chinese chamber a rare "home-field dividend": rather than waiting for the government to build the stage, it can invite regional Chinese business leaders to Manila on its own, and fold the chamber's agenda neatly into the country's regional strategy.

## The ethnic-Chinese business network: ASEAN's connective tissue

In their remarks, the organisers repeatedly stressed "regional partnership" and "embracing innovative technology," arguing that these help ASEAN economies cope with multiple challenges—geopolitical uncertainty, economic volatility, climate change and technological transformation. Stripped of rhetoric, the message is plain: in a Southeast Asia where formal institutions remain thin, the ethnic-Chinese business network is a ready-made, low-transaction-cost web of cooperation.

This is no mere pleasantry. Since the postwar era, ethnic Chinese communities across Southeast Asia have stayed at the front line of trade, investment and industrial development. They span borders, share language and cultural trust, and can often allocate cross-border resources through private trust well before intergovernmental agreements take effect. It is for this reason that the Chinese business network is frequently described as ASEAN's "connective tissue"—it may not be written into a free-trade agreement, yet it genuinely operates in every cross-border investment and industrial partnership.

## The keywords: innovation, resilience and regional collaboration

The specific topics the delegates debated were more down-to-earth than the slogans: applied innovative technology, industrial cooperation, cross-border investment and regional market expansion. In other words, the summit's centre of gravity was not slogan-shouting but converting "business opportunities" into executable cooperation projects.

That evening's "ASEAN Outstanding Chinese Business Night" honoured exemplary ethnic-Chinese entrepreneurs for their performance in management, innovation and social contribution, interspersed with traditional Filipino dance and performances by local singers. Seemingly just dinner entertainment, this arrangement reveals another logic: the Chinese business community is no longer an "outsider" but a locally embedded force that contributes both capital and culture to the societies it inhabits.

## Beyond the applause: wealth concentration and governance transparency

Of course, to hail the Chinese business network as a pillar of "regional resilience" also means looking at the other side of the coin. In countries such as Indonesia and Malaysia, the fact that ethnic Chinese hold a disproportionate share of the economy is both real and a long-standing sensitive nerve. When the economy slows or politics polarises, this concentration of wealth is easily weaponised by politicians as a mobilising symbol.

A more immediate challenge lies at the level of governance. A business network built on ethnic trust is efficient, yet it naturally favours the "circle of acquaintances" and is often criticised on transparency and fair competition. When the summit chants "resilience" and "collaboration," the question it truly must answer is whether this network can, while preserving efficiency, move toward more open institutions and more transparent rules. Otherwise, the stronger it grows, the deeper outside suspicion may become.

## Further reading

**Federation of Filipino Chinese Chambers of Commerce and Industry (FFCCCII)**: The oldest and largest umbrella organisation of ethnic-Chinese business associations in the Philippines, long serving as a bridge in Philippine–Chinese economic and people-to-people exchange.

**ASEAN Chair**: ASEAN is chaired by a member state on a rotating annual basis, steering that year's regional agenda and summit; the Philippines holds the chair for 2026.

**Ethnic-Chinese economy**: The structural influence of ethnic-Chinese communities across Southeast Asia in trade, finance and industry—an engine of growth that also carries the baggage of wealth concentration and social sensitivity.
