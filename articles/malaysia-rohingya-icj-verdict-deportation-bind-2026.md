# 种族灭绝判决之后5天：马来西亚把罗兴亚人推向两个方向

据《日经亚洲》报道，国际法院（International Court of Justice）11月19日裁定缅甸对罗兴亚人犯下种族灭绝罪；5天后，马来西亚宣布新一轮遣返安排。一边是世界法院历史上第一份认定主权国家犯下种族灭绝罪的判决，一边是准备载着被遣返者离境的军舰与飞机。这个时间差不是巧合，而是马来西亚在道义表态、国内反弹与行政承压之间被迫走钢丝的缩影。问题随之而来：当法院要求“安全、自愿、有尊严地返回”时，谁有资格替流亡者按下返回键？

## 两组数字：13.34万登记者与6500名待返者

先看分母。联合国难民署（United Nations Refugee Agency）数据显示，截至9月30日，马来西亚登记的罗兴亚难民和庇护申请者约13.34万人；截至10月31日，仍有6294人被扣在移民拘留中心。若把范围放大，截至8月，在马登记的难民与庇护申请者共约22.57万人，其中约九成来自缅甸；联合国此前统计显示，缅甸难民中约三分之二是罗兴亚人。这是一个长期沉淀的存量，不是一批可以靠几趟船运清空的积压件。

再看分子。总理安瓦尔在国会宣布，12月将遣返超过6500人：其中1500人分乘3艘海军舰艇于12月6日至7日返回，另有5303人由缅甸政府出资、以飞机运送。9月下旬已经有约1500人被送回，人权团体随后要求马方核实其中数百人是否遭军方拘押，安瓦尔在本次宣布中并未回应这一追问。把6500放进13.34万的分母，比例不足5%。这不是解决方案，而是把管理系统里最“可操作”的一部分先挪出去。

行政逻辑偏爱可计数的对象：已登记、被拘留、身份可核、愿意离开。最难处理的是那些没有国籍、没有证件、也不愿回到施暴者治下的人。数字越整齐，越容易掩盖这条分界线。

## 土耳其方案：一条被撤回的声明

11月20日，副首相阿末扎希在社交平台发布消息称，内阁已批准一项提案：通过3至6个月的职业技能培训，把1万名在马罗兴亚人安置到土耳其。声明写明要与土耳其方面、联合国难民署及相关部门协调，人员筛选、证件办理与就业均须依法推进。几个小时后，这条贴文被无声删除，办公室没有解释原因。第二天，土耳其总统府打击虚假信息部门公开表示该说法“毫无根据”，提醒公众不要轻信。11月22日，马来西亚外交部发表声明，尊重国际法院的判决并承诺继续推动全面、持久的解决方案。

这出“宣布—撤回—否认”的三幕剧，值得比八卦更认真的解读。第三国安置一直是马来西亚政策工具箱里最体面的一件工具：只要有一条外部出口，遣返就不必独自承担全部道德重量。可一件只在贴文里存活数小时、又被目的地公开否认的工具，实际承载力接近于零。它更像给国内舆论的一张安慰券，而不是给13.34万人的一条生路。

要公平地说，安置谈判失败不能全怪吉隆坡。接收国没有义务配合别国的人口管理目标，联合国难民署的协调也需要时间。正因为如此，把未经确认的方案先公布出来，反而暴露了政策深处的不自信：政府知道自己拿不出可核验的替代选项。

## 判决的门槛：安全、自愿、有尊严

国际法院由15名法官以9票对6票作出判决，认定缅甸安全部队在若开邦的杀戮、大规模性暴力与针对儿童的行为构成种族灭绝，并下令缅甸停止违法行为、调查起诉责任人、允许安全自愿且有尊严的返回、恢复财产与家庭团聚，且在两年内报告执行情况。这是该法院1945年成立以来首次判定一个国家犯下种族灭绝罪，判决终局且不得上诉。

判决的执行力是另一回事。国际法院没有警察，赔偿金额留待后续程序，强制力最终依赖联合国安理会与国际社会的政治压力。但“安全、自愿、有尊严”不是外交辞令的装饰，它是接收国与遣返国都必须面对的标尺。究竟什么算自愿？是本人书面签字，还是“与其继续被拘留不如上船”？什么算安全？是接收国政府口头保证，还是有独立观察员能进入拘留场所与安置点？

若开邦的现实让这些问题无法回避。战事仍在延续，若开军控制了与孟加拉接壤的大片地区，缅甸军方继续空袭并限制人道援助进入。多个武装行为体并存、粮食短缺、公民身份被剥夺——这些都是法院判决书里列出的风险背景。把人送回这样一个环境，再引用法院“允许返回”的表述为行动背书，是把判决读成了一半。

## 国内反弹：敏昂莱来访与政治账

据《日经亚洲》报道，围绕遣返的争议与马来西亚国内对难民问题的反弹交织在一起。政府多次强调收容难民已成为财政负担与安全风险；与此同时，前巴生区国会议员查尔斯·圣地亚哥公开质问安瓦尔，为何在10月8日的国事访问中接待缅甸领导人敏昂莱。批评者认为，在种族灭绝判决出台前后给予军政府领导人高规格礼遇，会削弱追究政权的国际努力，也让“安全遣返”的承诺显得前后矛盾。

安瓦尔并非没有承认复杂性。他公开表示，许多庇护申请者确实是缅甸政权暴行的受害者，但马来西亚已无力继续收容，寄望第三国安置也未能奏效。这是实话，却还不是完整的政策论证。一个中等收入的多元社会当然有权设定承载能力上限；问题在于上限如何设定、依据什么程序、是否允许申诉。若承载力只以冰冷的行政效率来定义，国内反弹就会被当作推迟权利审查的理由，而不是推动制度建设的压力。

政治账同样要算清。对安瓦尔政府而言，向军政府靠近是为了争取遣返配合；向国内选民展示“清理非法滞留者”则是为了回应不满。两笔账都想记赢，最后最容易被牺牲的，恰恰是判决要求逐案核验的“自愿”。

## 非公约国家的行政逻辑

马来西亚不是1951年《难民公约》缔约国，入境者通常被视为临时居民，而不是法律意义上的难民。政府推动建立难民申请者国家管理系统后，联合国难民署暂停了在马的登记与证件发放。筛查、拘留、身份归类、组织遣返，由此成为一套以国内行政权为核心的流程。

这套流程有过明确成果：2025年全年完成安置或遣返的人数，相对于二十多万登记存量只是零头。行政体系擅长把个案变成表格，却无法凭空创造保护地位。把“缅甸国民”作为统一标签，还会把最难的问题溶解掉——罗兴亚人的无国籍状态并不会因为改换称谓而消失，只是从国际保护议题变成普通的移民执法议题。

应当区分两种批评。一种说马来西亚做得太少，没有尽到人道义务；另一种说马来西亚做得不对，把筛查起点设在拘留营、把遣返速度当成绩效。后者才真正击中要害：法律真空不能由行政便利来填补，否则程序越完整，错误越难被纠正。

## 华社视角：侨民网络的声誉与劳务风险

这场争论离马来西亚华人社会并不远。华商与华社中介长期活跃在马来西亚与缅甸之间的贸易、招聘、汇款和担保链条上。当公共叙事把罗兴亚人简化为“非法劳工与治安问题”时，合法经营的华商也会被连带污名化；反过来，若中介把身份不清的求职者包装成普通劳工，后续一旦被遣返或遭拘押，声誉与法律风险都会回流到侨社。

侨社能做的不是替政策背书，而是补上政策最薄的一环：为无国籍者与庇护申请者提供法律咨询、语言协助与证件核验指引，并主动与联合国难民署认可的程序对接。侨务工作若只盯着“华侨华人权益”，就会漏掉一个基本事实——东南亚的族群共处，取决于最小众的那一群人被如何对待。

## 三问：谁定义“自愿”

第一问，名单怎么来的。被列入遣返名单的人是否被告知目的地的真实风险？是否知道拒绝返回后仍有合法居留或申诉的通道？如果答案只能在拘留营里得到，签字本身就不构成自愿。

第二问，担保谁来验。缅甸政府承诺接收，不等于有人在现场核验安置点、探视权与人身自由。没有独立观察员、没有可核查的名单与回访机制，“有尊严”三个字就只是新闻稿里的形容词。

第三问，出事谁负责。9月那批遣返者中据报有人被军方拘押，这是检验政策诚意的最好样本：政府是否追踪过他们的下落？是否公开交涉结果？如果只问船开了没有、不问人到了哪里，那么判决与遣返之间就只剩下修辞上的联系。

种族灭绝判决改变了词汇，却没有自动改变筹码。马来西亚可以用军舰和飞机清空拘留营的一角，却清不掉13.34万人的身份困境。可信的最低方案并不复杂：公开遣返遴选标准，允许独立监测，为无法返回者给出法律身份路径，并把负担分担重新摆上东盟议程。做不到这些，11月19日的判决就只会成为这场围城里的一行脚注。

## 延伸阅读

**不推回原则（Non-refoulement）**：国际难民法的核心原则，禁止缔约方将个人送回其可能遭受酷刑、残忍待遇或生命威胁的国家和地区；对非公约国家同样构成重要的规范约束。

**无国籍状态（Statelessness）**：个人不被任何国家依据法律承认其国民身份的状态；罗兴亚人因缅甸法律不承认其族群身份而长期处于这一处境，遣返与安置都因此更复杂。

**恢复原状（Reparation / Restitution）**：国际法院在种族灭绝案中可命令的责任承担方式，包括返还财产、恢复家园、家庭团聚与允许安全返回；区别于单纯的金钱赔偿。

# Five days after the genocide verdict: Malaysia pushes Rohingya in two directions

According to Nikkei Asia, the International Court of Justice ruled on November 19 that Myanmar had committed genocide against the Rohingya. Five days later, Malaysia announced a new round of repatriations. On one side stands the first ruling in the World Court's history to find a sovereign state guilty of genocide; on the other, naval vessels and aircraft preparing to carry people back. That timing is no coincidence. It captures a government walking a tightrope between moral signalling, domestic backlash and administrative exhaustion. The question is simple: when the court demands "safe, voluntary and dignified return," who is entitled to press the return button on behalf of exiles?

## The numbers: 133,400 registered and 6,500 due to leave

Start with the denominator. According to the UN Refugee Agency, about 133,400 Rohingya refugees and asylum seekers were registered in Malaysia as of September 30, and 6,294 remained in immigration detention as of October 31. Widen the lens and, as of August, Malaysia hosted roughly 225,740 registered refugees and asylum seekers, around 90 per cent of them from Myanmar; the UN has said about two-thirds of Myanmar refugees in the country are Rohingya. This is a long-settled stock, not a backlog that a few voyages can clear.

Now the numerator. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim told parliament that more than 6,500 people would be returned in December: 1,500 aboard three navy vessels on December 6–7, and 5,303 by air at Myanmar government expense. Some 1,500 people had already been sent back in late September; human-rights groups asked Kuala Lumpur to establish whether hundreds of them were being held in military detention, a question Anwar did not address in his latest announcement. Against a denominator of 133,400, 6,500 is under five per cent. This is not a solution; it is the most administratively tractable slice of the caseload being moved first.

Bureaucracies prefer countable objects: registered, detained, identifiable, willing to leave. Hardest to process are those without nationality, without papers and without any wish to return to the authorities that persecuted them. The neater the numbers, the more easily that line disappears.

## The Turkish option: a statement withdrawn

On November 20, Deputy Prime Minister Ahmad Zahid Hamidi posted on Facebook that the cabinet had approved a proposal to resettle 10,000 Rohingya in Turkey through a three-to-six-month vocational training programme. The statement said selection, documentation and employment would be coordinated with Turkish authorities, UNHCR and relevant agencies. Hours later the post was quietly removed, with no explanation. The next day, Turkey's presidential disinformation unit described the claim as "baseless" and warned the public against believing it. On November 22, Malaysia's foreign ministry issued a statement respecting the court ruling and pledging continued work towards a comprehensive, lasting solution.

Three acts — announcement, withdrawal, denial — deserve more than gossip. Third-country resettlement has long been the most respectable tool in Malaysia's policy kit: as long as an external exit exists, deportation does not have to carry the full moral weight alone. But a tool that survives a few hours on a social-media post and is publicly denied by its supposed destination has close to zero carrying capacity. It is a reassurance voucher for domestic readers, not a route out for 133,400 people.

In fairness, a failed resettlement negotiation cannot be blamed on Kuala Lumpur alone. Receiving states have no duty to meet another country's population-management targets, and UNHCR coordination takes time. Precisely for that reason, publishing an unconfirmed scheme first exposes how thin the government's bench really is: it knows it has no verifiable alternative.

## The court's thresholds: safe, voluntary, dignified

The court ruled 9–6. It found that Myanmar security forces' killings, large-scale sexual violence and attacks on children in Rakhine State constituted genocide. It ordered Myanmar to cease the violations, investigate and prosecute those responsible, allow safe, voluntary and dignified return, restore property and family unity, and report compliance within two years. It was the first time since 1945 that the court found a state guilty of genocide; the judgment is final and cannot be appealed.

Enforcement is another matter. The court has no police force, the exact form of reparations was left to later proceedings, and coercion ultimately depends on the UN Security Council and international political pressure. Yet "safe, voluntary and dignified" is not decorative language. It is the yardstick by which both the sending and receiving state must be measured. What counts as voluntary — a signed form, or "better the ship than another month in detention"? What counts as safe — a verbal assurance from the receiving government, or independent access to detention sites and resettlement locations?

Rakhine makes such questions unavoidable. Fighting continues, the Arakan Army controls large areas bordering Bangladesh, and the military continues air strikes while restricting humanitarian access. Multiple armed actors, food shortages and the stripping of citizenship are precisely the risk factors the judgment records. Returning people to that environment and then citing the court's language on return as endorsement reads only half the verdict.

## The backlash: Min Aung Hlaing's visit and the political ledger

Nikkei Asia reports that the repatriation dispute is entangled with domestic resistance to refugees. The government has repeatedly argued that hosting refugees has become a financial burden and a security risk. At the same time, former Klang MP Charles Santiago publicly asked Anwar why he welcomed Myanmar leader Min Aung Hlaing on a state visit on October 8. Critics argue that extending high-level courtesies to the junta, around the time of the genocide ruling, undermines international efforts to hold the regime accountable and makes the promise of safe return look self-contradictory.

Anwar has not denied the complexity. He acknowledged that many asylum seekers were victims of atrocities committed by the Myanmar regime, while insisting Malaysia can no longer afford to host them and that third-country resettlement has not materialised. That is honest, but it is not yet a full policy argument. A middle-income, multi-ethnic society is entitled to set a ceiling on what it can absorb. The real questions are how that ceiling is set, through what procedure, and with what right of appeal. If capacity is defined only by administrative throughput, domestic resentment becomes a reason to postpone rights review rather than a prompt to build institutions.

The political ledger also has to be balanced. Moving closer to the junta buys cooperation on returns; being seen to clear "illegal migrants" answers voters at home. Both entries want to be marked as wins. The item most likely to be written down as a loss is the case-by-case verification of consent that the judgment demands.

## The logic of a non-convention state

Malaysia is not party to the 1951 Refugee Convention. Arrivals are generally treated as temporary residents rather than refugees in law. After the government moved to establish a national management system for asylum applications, UNHCR suspended registration and document issuance in the country. Screening, detention, categorical labelling and organised returns have therefore become a process driven principally by domestic executive power.

The process has produced results: the number of people resettled or repatriated in 2025 remained a fraction of the registered stock of more than 200,000, yet the machinery exists. Administrative systems are good at converting cases into forms; they cannot manufacture protection status out of nothing. Labelling everyone a "Myanmar national" also dissolves the hardest problem. Rohingya statelessness does not disappear because the category changes — it is simply translated from an international-protection issue into an ordinary immigration-enforcement one.

Two kinds of criticism should be kept apart. The first says Malaysia does too little and fails its humanitarian duty. The second says it does the wrong things: starting screening inside detention centres and treating return speed as a performance indicator. The second goes to the heart of the matter. A legal vacuum cannot be filled by administrative convenience, or else the more complete the procedure, the harder it becomes to correct the error.

## A diaspora view: reputation and labour risks for Chinese networks

This debate is not remote from Malaysia's Chinese community. Chinese traders and brokers have long operated in the recruitment, remittance and guarantee chains linking Malaysia and Myanmar. When public narrative reduces Rohingya to "illegal workers and a security problem," legitimate Chinese-owned businesses inherit the stigma. Conversely, if brokers dress up people with unclear status as ordinary migrant workers, later deportation or detention sends legal and reputational risk straight back to the community.

What the community can offer is not endorsement of policy but reinforcement of its weakest layer: legal advice, language assistance and document-verification guidance for stateless people and asylum seekers, coordinated with procedures UNHCR recognises. Diaspora work that focuses only on the rights of ethnic Chinese misses a basic fact of Southeast Asian coexistence — how a society treats its smallest minority defines the terms on which everyone else lives together.

## Three questions: who defines "voluntary"?

First, how is the list compiled? Were those selected told the real conditions in the destination? Do they know that refusing return still leaves a lawful route to remain or appeal? If the only information available is delivered inside a detention centre, a signature is not consent.

Second, who verifies the guarantee? A Myanmar government promise to receive returnees is not the same as independent access to reception sites, visiting rights and checks on personal liberty. Without monitors, published lists and follow-up visits, "dignity" remains an adjective in a press release.

Third, who is accountable when things go wrong? Reports that some of the September returnees were detained by the military offer the cleanest test of intent: has Kuala Lumpur traced them, and has it made the outcome public? Asking only whether the ship sailed, never where the passengers ended up, leaves the judgment and the deportation connected by rhetoric alone.

The genocide verdict changed the vocabulary; it did not automatically change the leverage. Malaysia can empty one corner of its detention system with ships and aircraft, but it cannot clear 133,400 people's legal limbo that way. A credible minimum package is not complicated: publish selection criteria, allow independent monitoring, provide a legal-status pathway for those who cannot return, and put burden-sharing back on the ASEAN agenda. Without those steps, the November 19 ruling becomes a footnote to the siege rather than a way out of it.

## Further reading

**Non-refoulement**: The core principle of international refugee law prohibiting the transfer of a person to a country where they face torture, cruel treatment or a threat to life. It remains a powerful norm even for states outside the 1951 Convention.

**Statelessness**: The condition of being recognised as a national by no state under its laws. Rohingya have been left in that position by Myanmar's refusal to recognise their group identity, complicating both return and resettlement.

**Reparation / restitution**: Forms of responsibility the International Court may order in genocide cases, including return of property, restoration of homes, family reunification and safe return — distinct from purely monetary compensation.
