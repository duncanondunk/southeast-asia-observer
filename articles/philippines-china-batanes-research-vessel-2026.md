# 一艘科考船，一道窄海峡：嘉海科7号为何成了马尼拉最新的「爆点」

2026年10月1日，菲律宾海岸警卫队派出其最大海警船、97米长的「特蕾莎·马格巴努阿」号（BRP Teresa Magbanua）与一架巡逻机，前往巴丹群岛（Batanes）附近海域，意图「干扰并驱离」一艘在此活动的中国科考船「嘉海科7」号（Jia Hai Ke 7）。中国驻马尼拉使馆随即驳斥，称船只位于中国管辖水域、菲方行为形同骚扰，敦促马尼拉停止干扰中方正常的科研活动。一艘2000吨级的民用调查船，在多数人地图上都未必能找准的一片水域滞留数日，为何会成了南海博弈的最新爆点？本文认为，它折射出的，是台湾、吕宋与第一岛链之间一场正在收紧的战略挤压。

## 现场：91米的对峙

10月1日上午7时55分，菲律宾海警发言人塔里埃拉在社交媒体发文称，「特蕾莎·马格巴努阿」号抵近至距嘉海科7号不足100码（约91米），并放出硬壳充气艇绕行，意图打断其所谓「非法海洋科研」活动。菲方称，这艘中国船只自9月28日便在该海域停留——按船舶自动识别系统（AIS）轨迹，它更早的9月22日便已抵近伊特巴亚特岛。期间科考船多次机动规避菲方小艇，无线电喊话未获回应。随后，一艘舷号2503的中国海警船赶来为科考船护航，并对菲舰机喊话，形成双方近距离「贴身」对峙。菲方放话「将持续挑战、干扰并驱离，直到其离开菲律宾专属经济区（EEZ）」。值得玩味的是，全程未发生碰撞等恶性事件，双方都在刻意控制烈度——这是一次高调却克制的前推。

## 位置：巴士海峡的咽喉

巴丹群岛横亘在吕宋岛北端与台湾岛之间，所扼之处正是巴士海峡（Bashi Channel）/ 吕宋海峡（Luzon Strait）——南海通往西太平洋的关键水道。这里不仅是全球航运要冲，也是连接菲律宾与东北亚、印太多条海底通信电缆（submarine cable）的密集区，更是菲律宾离台湾最近的前哨。近年来，菲军在此设前进作战基地，美军也将联合演训向这片水域摆布。谁掌握了这片海域的态势感知，谁就握住了台海应急与岛链博弈的先手。一艘民用科考船之所以牵动神经，正因为它采集的海底地形、洋流与水深数据，本质上是一切海上执法与军事部署的地基。

## 法理：谁的海域，谁的科研

按《联合国海洋法公约》（UNCLOS），沿海国对其专属经济区内的海洋科研（MSR）享有管辖权，他国在他国专属经济区从事科研，须获沿海国同意。马尼拉称从未批准嘉海科7号的科研活动；北京则坚称船只位于中国管辖水域、系正常作业。分歧的根源，在于双方对主权与管辖权的认知错位：事发水域距伊特巴亚特岛约37海里，远超12海里领海，属专属经济区与大陆架的重叠争议带。双方都援引国际法，可国际法解决不了「究竟是谁的水域」这一前置问题。中国不承认2016年所谓南海仲裁案裁决，马尼拉则将其奉为底气。法律在此更像各说各话的武器，而非一锤定音的裁决者。

## 疑云：自动识别信号偏移与海底电缆

菲方的一大疑点，是船舶自动识别系统（AIS）信号异常：船只离开厦门数小时后，其信号一度出现在约2000海里外、巴布亚新几内亚近海，菲方据此怀疑中方人为修改定位以掩藏真实活动。另一重担忧是，嘉海科7号长期滞留的位置，正处巴丹群岛与吕宋海峡的海底电缆走廊附近。菲方与部分西方舆论由此将其活动与「威胁海底通信基础设施」「数据或可用于军事」挂钩。北京反驳称这是正常科研。客观来看，海洋水文调查天然具有军民两用属性——合法的地质勘测与战略性的海底测绘之间，边界本就模糊；而这种模糊，恰是此类对峙难以消弭的症结。

## 背景：日菲划界与北京的施压

南华早报将此事置于更宽的脉络：北京正加大对马尼拉—东京专属经济区与大陆架划界谈判的施压。这片海峡对日本同样要紧——其航运与能源通道经此连接西太平洋。中国反对将自身排除在外的双边划界，认为相关水域涉及中国主权与管辖权。自日菲围绕台岛以东海域划界接触以来，中国明显加强了在台湾岛周边及第一岛链方向的海上执法与科考存在，以宣示并巩固对相关水域的实际掌控。这是美日菲三边联动之下，中方以常态化存在对冲外部围堵的一环。

## 叙事：海上与舆论的双重博弈

马尼拉这次反应极快，第一时间高调发布「驱离」战报，把「中方船只闯入、菲方强硬驱离」的剧本先立住，意在争取美国、日本、澳大利亚等盟友的声援，巩固其在国际舆论中的「弱势方」形象。北京则以使馆声明加《环球时报》评论反制，指责菲方骚扰、倒打一耙。双方都留有沟通渠道、都不愿事态升级，却都忙着抢话语权。这场戏也有国内票房：距下届大选不足两年，对华强硬、迎合民族主义与亲美派，本就是菲政客争取选票的惯用套路。而所谓「驱离成功」，至今只是菲方自说自话——巡逻舰仍在尾随，生怕科考船掉头折返。

## 走向：可控的摩擦，未定的规则

可以预见，此类低强度对峙会愈发频繁。真正的症结不在一条船，而在于争议水域的海洋科研与调查活动，至今缺乏双方认可的行为规则。热线、意外相遇处置规范能避免碰撞，却化解不了主权主张的根本对立。中国以民用科考与海警力量织起的常态化存在，正被一步步「坐实」；而菲方的应对受实力差距掣肘，只能退而求其次，用舆论造势与盟友背书，来弥补硬实力的不足。说到底，巴丹群岛这一幕是症状，不是病灶——它映出的，是一道正在收紧的战略钳形，而非一次孤立的擦枪走火。

## 延伸阅读

**海洋科研（Marine Scientific Research）**：《联合国海洋法公约》第十三部分专章规范专属经济区内海洋科研，规定须沿海国同意；在争议水域，科研活动与主权主张往往互相缠绕，成为低烈度博弈的新前线。

**巴士海峡与第一岛链（Bashi Channel & First Island Chain）**：巴士海峡是南海连接西太平洋的咽喉，也是第一岛链上的关键节点；其态势感知能力，直接牵动台海与区域海上安全的神经。

**日菲专属经济区划界（Manila–Tokyo EEZ Delimitation）**：日本与菲律宾围绕台岛以东海域的专属经济区与大陆架划界谈判，被视为排除第三方（中国）的海上秩序安排，是中菲、中日菲多重博弈的焦点之一。

# A research vessel, a narrow strait: why a Chinese ship off Batanes became Manila's newest flashpoint

On 1 October 2026, the Philippine Coast Guard (PCG) dispatched its largest vessel, the 97-metre BRP Teresa Magbanua, and a patrol aircraft to waters off the Batanes Islands, aiming to "disrupt and drive away" a Chinese research ship, the Jia Hai Ke 7, operating in the area. China's embassy in Manila rejected the account, saying the vessel was in waters under Chinese jurisdiction and that Manila's action amounted to "harassment," urging the Philippines to stop interfering with normal Chinese scientific activity. How did a 2,000-ton civilian survey ship, loitering for days in a stretch of water most people could not place on a map, become the newest flashpoint in the South China Sea contest? This piece argues that what it reveals is a tightening strategic squeeze among Taiwan, Luzon and the first island chain.

## The scene: a 91-metre standoff

At 7:55 a.m. on 1 October, PCG spokesman Jay Tarriela posted on social media that BRP Teresa Magbanua closed to within 100 yards (about 91 metres) of Jia Hai Ke 7 and launched a rigid-hull inflatable boat to circle the vessel, aiming to disrupt what Manila called its "illegal marine scientific research." Philippine authorities said the Chinese ship had been in the area since 28 September; Automatic Identification System (AIS) tracks suggest it had in fact approached Itbayat Island as early as 22 September. During the operation the survey vessel repeatedly manoeuvred to evade Philippine boats, and radio challenges went unanswered. A China Coast Guard ship, hull number 2503, then arrived to escort the research vessel and issued its own radio challenges, producing a close-range, shoulder-to-shoulder standoff. Manila vowed to "continue to challenge, disrupt and drive away" the ship until it leaves the Philippine exclusive economic zone (EEZ). Tellingly, no collision or other serious incident occurred; both sides kept the escalation in check — a loud but restrained forward push.

## The geography: the throat of the Bashi Channel

The Batanes archipelago sits between the northern tip of Luzon and Taiwan, straddling the Bashi Channel (also called the Luzon Strait) — the key waterway linking the South China Sea to the Western Pacific. It is not only a global shipping chokepoint but also a dense corridor of subsea communications cables connecting the Philippines to Northeast Asia and the broader Indo-Pacific, and it is the Philippine territory closest to Taiwan. In recent years Manila has built forward operating bases here, and the United States has shifted joint exercises toward these waters. Whoever commands maritime-domain awareness here holds the initiative in any Taiwan contingency and island-chain contest. The reason a civilian research ship sets off alarms is simple: the seabed topography, currents and depth data it collects are, in essence, the foundation for any future maritime enforcement or military deployment.

## The law: whose waters, whose research

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), coastal states hold jurisdiction over marine scientific research (MSR) in their exclusive economic zones, and research by other states there requires the coastal state's consent. Manila says it never authorised Jia Hai Ke 7's activities; Beijing insists the vessel was in waters under Chinese jurisdiction conducting normal operations. The root of the dispute is a mismatch in how the two sides see sovereignty and jurisdiction: the incident area lies about 37 nautical miles from Itbayat, well beyond the 12-nautical-mile territorial sea, placing it in an overlapping EEZ and continental-shelf contest. Both invoke international law, yet the law cannot resolve the prior question of whose waters these are. China does not recognise the 2016 arbitral award; Manila treats it as its bedrock. Here, law functions less as arbiter than as rival rhetoric.

## The suspicions: AIS drift and subsea cables

One of Manila's main suspicions is an anomaly in the vessel's Automatic Identification System (AIS): hours after leaving Xiamen, its signal briefly appeared some 2,000 nautical miles away off Papua New Guinea, prompting Philippine authorities to allege deliberate position-spoofing to hide its true track. The other concern is location: Jia Hai Ke 7 loitered near the subsea-cable corridors around Batanes and the Luzon Strait. Manila and some Western commentary have linked the activity to "threats to undersea communications infrastructure" and the possibility that collected data could serve military ends. Beijing counters that this is normal research. Objectively, hydrographic survey is inherently dual-use — the line between legitimate geological survey and strategic seabed mapping is inherently blurry, and that blurriness is precisely why such standoffs are so hard to defuse.

## The backdrop: Tokyo–Manila delimitation and Beijing's pressure

The South China Morning Post frames the episode in a wider context: Beijing is stepping up pressure against Manila–Tokyo negotiations to delimit their overlapping exclusive economic zones and continental shelves. The strait matters to Japan too — its shipping and energy routes pass through it to reach the Western Pacific. China opposes bilateral delimitation that excludes it, arguing the waters involve Chinese sovereignty and jurisdiction. Since Tokyo and Manila began their delimitation contacts east of Taiwan, China has visibly expanded its maritime law-enforcement and survey presence around Taiwan and along the first island chain, to assert and consolidate de facto control. This is one strand of Beijing's response, through normalised presence, to external encirclement under the US–Japan–Philippines trilateral.

## The narrative: a dual game at sea and in the press

Manila moved fast this time, publicly releasing a "drive-away" report at the first opportunity and establishing the script of "Chinese vessel intrudes, Philippines firmly expels," aiming to rally sympathy and backing from allies such as the United States, Japan and Australia and to shore up its "underdog" image in international opinion. Beijing countered with an embassy statement and a Global Times commentary, accusing Manila of harassment and of blaming the victim. Both sides keep communication channels open and neither wants escalation, yet both rush to seize the narrative. The spectacle also plays to a domestic audience: with the next election less than two years away, a hard line on China — courting nationalist sentiment and pro-US factions — is a familiar vote-winning tactic. And the claimed "successful expulsion" remains, to this day, Manila's own account; the patrol vessel is still shadowing the ship, wary it might turn back.

## The outlook: manageable friction, unsettled rules

Expect such low-intensity encounters to grow more frequent. The real crux is not one ship but the absence of mutually accepted rules for survey and research activity in disputed waters. Hotlines and codes for unplanned encounters can prevent collisions but cannot resolve the fundamental clash of sovereignty claims. The normalised presence that China is weaving together through civilian research and coast-guard assets is being steadily consolidated; Manila's response, constrained by a capability gap, falls back on publicity and allied endorsement to compensate for hard-power shortfalls. In the end, the Batanes episode is a symptom, not the disease — what it mirrors is a tightening strategic pincer, not an isolated brush with fire.

## Further reading

**Marine Scientific Research**: Part XIII of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) governs marine scientific research in the EEZ and requires the coastal state's consent; in disputed waters, research and sovereignty claims entangle, turning science into a new front of low-level rivalry.

**Bashi Channel & First Island Chain**: The Bashi Channel is the throat between the South China Sea and the Western Pacific and a key node of the first island chain; command of domain awareness here bears directly on Taiwan contingencies and regional maritime security.

**Manila–Tokyo EEZ Delimitation**: Japan and the Philippines are negotiating the delimitation of their overlapping EEZ and continental shelf east of Taiwan, seen as a third-party-excluding maritime order and a focal point of the multi-layered China–Philippines and China–Japan–Philippines contest.
