# 柬埔寨3000万美元电子垃圾计划：循环经济的口号与硬约束

9月19日，柬埔寨环境部公布了一项为期五年、耗资3000万美元（约2.13亿元人民币；另据报道合约3900万新元，约合2.14亿元人民币）的电子垃圾治理计划。资金由两部分组成：柬埔寨政府与企业伙伴联合出资2600万美元（约18.5亿元人民币），全球环境基金另提供400万美元（约2.85亿元人民币）赠款；项目由联合国开发计划署合作执行。官方给出的目标相当具体：减少2万7000吨温室气体排放，控制汞与持久性有机污染物等有毒物质，阻止超过2万吨污染物进入土壤、水体和大气，并直接保护逾20万人的健康与安全。更关键的是，柬埔寨自今年6月起已全面禁止进口电子垃圾，明确表态"不让本国成为他国废弃电子产品的填埋场"。从纸面上看，这是一个后发国家赶在问题恶化前主动出手的好故事；可细节里藏着更硬的骨头。

## 数字：一笔为期五年、3000万美元的赌注

这笔账的头号数字是3000万美元，期限五年。其中2600万美元来自柬埔寨政府与企业伙伴的联合融资，剩下的400万美元是全球环境基金提供的赠款。计划要用这五年重塑整条电子产品的价值链——收集、回收与处置——摆脱环境部国务秘书佐巴里口中的线性"获取—制造—废弃"模式。

目标定得很细：减排2万7000吨温室气体，遏制汞与持久性有机污染物，阻止超过2万吨污染物进入土壤、水体和空气。联合国开发计划署驻柬代表加维利亚用一组消费数据来说明紧迫性：2024年柬埔寨境内在用的电子设备估计达2400万台，而且还在快速攀升。他警告，报废处理系统必须跟得上这个速度。说白了，问题不是将来才有，而是现在就已经在身后追了。

## 为什么是现在：从"洋垃圾"到自主治理

时机才是这件事最耐人寻味的地方。今年6月，柬埔寨宣布全面禁止电子垃圾进口，宣称不会沦为别国淘汰电子产品的倾倒地。这一步并非凭空而来。2018年中国以"洋垃圾"禁令关闭可回收物入境通道后，全球废弃物贸易便改道涌向东南亚。泰国与马来西亚一度成为处理枢纽，也几乎立刻陷入污染丑闻——两国在2019至2020年间不得不把大批受污染集装箱原路退回发货方。柬埔寨等于在货车尚未驶入之前，就从邻国的教训里学到了乖。

这里也有一层地缘政治逻辑。电子垃圾的产出端几乎全在发达国家，却以"回收"之名倾泻到全球南方。柬埔寨的禁令配合这笔新设的管理基金，意味着它正从被动接收者转向主动监管者——这正是2019年《巴塞尔公约》修正案收紧危险废弃物出口所体现的同一种思路。金边押下的是一个朴素判断：把垃圾挡在门外，比事后清理便宜得多。

## 9%的真相：循环经济的硬约束

然而最硬的约束不在政治意愿，而在物理与经济规律。正如柬埔寨环境部自己承认的，电子垃圾组件中仅有约9%能以经济可行的方式回收，其余91%需要先进回收技术、专门处置工艺和无害化处理——每一样都昂贵且技术门槛极高。3000万美元或许能搭起制度框架和首批收集网络，却无法单凭自身弥合这道鸿沟。

这正是执法历史的用武之地。柬埔寨过去并非没有雄心勃勃的环境法规——砂石开采、伐木、土地等领域都立过严法，却往往在薄弱的监管与盘根错节的利益面前慢慢失效。循环经济计划成败，系于检查、发牌、起诉这些不起眼的苦工。基金买来的是愿景，并不能自动变出监察员。

## 钱够不够：3000万美元的分量

相对于问题本身，3000万美元并不宽裕。全球环境基金自身的出资仅400万美元，在东南亚废弃物洪流面前不过是零头。对比别处的清理账单便知：泰国东部省份与马来西亚的非法回收城镇，花了数年与巨资才勉强挽回几批劣质货柜造成的破坏。柬埔寨的赌注在于"预防成本远低于治理"，这话不假——但前提是把禁令真正卡在港口。

企业伙伴的2600万美元联合出资，才是更耐读的数字。它暗示柬埔寨本土企业看好一个值得建设的国内回收市场，而不只是把它当作合规成本。若这个市场成熟，基金或能起催化之效；若不能，金边不过是把手里的依赖从"进口垃圾"换成了另一种——它目前还买不起的进口回收技术。

说到底，柬埔寨的电子垃圾计划是一笔不大却认真的赌注。它读对了区域教训，也拿出了真金白银。至于那9%的真相最终能否向循环经济的口号低头，要看的不在启动仪式的排场，而在仪式之后到底雇了多少监察员。

## 延伸阅读

- **《巴塞尔公约》(Basel Convention)**：1989年联合国条约，管控危险废弃物越境转移；2019年修正案进一步限制发达国家向贫穷国家出口电子垃圾。
- **"洋垃圾"禁令(中国国门利剑)**：中国2018年禁止进口可回收物，促使全球废弃物贸易改道东南亚。
- **循环经济(Circular economy)**：通过再利用、维修与回收让资源持续流转的经济模式，取代"获取—制造—废弃"的线性路径。

# Cambodia's US$30 Million E-Waste Plan: Circular Rhetoric, Hard Realities

On 19 September, Cambodia's Ministry of Environment unveiled a five-year, US$30 million initiative to overhaul how the country handles electronic waste. Backed by US$26 million in co-financing from the government and private partners plus a US$4 million grant from the Global Environment Facility, and delivered with the UN Development Programme, the programme promises to cut 27,000 tonnes of greenhouse-gas emissions, keep more than 20,000 tonnes of pollutants out of nature, and protect the health of over 200,000 people. Phnom Penh has also, since June, banned the import of e-waste outright. On paper, it is a tidy story of a poor country getting ahead of a problem. The details tell a harder one.

## The numbers: a five-year, US$30 million wager

The headline figure is US$30 million (about S$39 million, or roughly US$30 million at the current rate). Of that, US$26 million comes as co-financing from the Cambodian government and private partners, with the remaining US$4 million a grant from the Global Environment Facility. Over five years the plan aims to reshape the entire electronics value chain — collection, recycling, and disposal — away from the linear "take-make-dispose" model that the environment ministry's own secretary of state, Chuop Paris, says Cambodia must abandon.

The targets are specific: 27,000 tonnes of avoided greenhouse-gas emissions, mercury and persistent organic pollutants contained, and more than 20,000 tonnes of pollutants prevented from entering soil, water, and air. The UN Development Programme's resident representative, Enrico Gaveglia, frames the urgency in consumer terms: Cambodia had an estimated 24 million electronic devices in use in 2024, and that number is climbing fast. End-of-life systems, he warns, must keep pace. In plain terms, the problem is not someday — it is already gaining on the country.

## Why now: from "foreign garbage" to self-rule

The timing is the most revealing part. In June 2026 Cambodia announced a total ban on e-waste imports, declaring it would not become a dumping ground for other countries' discarded electronics. That move did not come from nowhere. When China shut its borders to foreign recyclables under its "National Sword" policy in 2018, the waste trade rerouted to Southeast Asia. Thailand and Malaysia became processing hubs — and, quickly, pollution scandals. Both countries spent 2019 and 2020 shipping contaminated containers back to their senders. Cambodia is, in effect, learning from its neighbours' mistakes before the trucks arrive.

There is a geopolitical logic here too. The e-waste stream is overwhelmingly generated in the Global North and offloaded onto the Global South under the label of "recycling." Cambodia's ban, paired with this new management fund, signals a shift from passive recipient to active regulator — the same instinct that produced the 2019 Basel Convention amendments tightening hazardous-waste exports. Phnom Penh is betting that keeping waste out is cheaper than cleaning it up later.

## The 9% truth: the hard constraint on circularity

Yet the hardest constraint is not political will but physics and economics. As Cambodia's own environment ministry has acknowledged, only about 9% of e-waste components can be recycled in an economically viable way. The remaining 91% requires advanced recycling technologies, specialised treatment, and environmentally sound disposal — all of it costly and technically demanding. A US$30 million fund may build the institutions and the first collection networks, but it will not, on its own, close that gap.

This is where enforcement history matters. Cambodia has passed ambitious environmental rules before — on sand mining, on logging, on land — only to watch them erode under weak monitoring and entrenched interests. A circular-economy plan lives or dies on the unglamorous work of inspection, licensing, and prosecution. The fund pays for the vision; it does not automatically produce the inspectors.

## Is the money enough? The weight of US$30 million

Measured against the problem, US$30 million is modest. The Global Environment Facility's own contribution is just US$4 million — a rounding error against the scale of Southeast Asia's waste flows. Compare it with the cleanup bills elsewhere: Thailand's eastern provinces and Malaysia's illegal-recycling towns have spent years and large sums undoing the damage from a few bad shipments. Cambodia's bet is that prevention costs a fraction of remediation, which is true — but only if the ban is actually enforced at the ports.

The private co-financing of US$26 million is the more interesting number. It suggests Cambodian firms see a domestic recycling market worth building, not just a compliance cost. If that market matures, the fund could be catalytic. If it does not, Phnom Penh will have traded one dependency — imported waste — for another: imported recycling technology it cannot yet afford.

Cambodia's e-waste plan is, in the end, a small but serious wager. It reads the regional lesson correctly and puts money behind it. Whether the 9% reality bends to the circular-economy rhetoric will depend less on the launch ceremony than on the inspectors hired afterwards.

## Further reading

- **Basel Convention**: The 1989 UN treaty controlling transboundary movements of hazardous waste, tightened in 2019 to restrict exports of e-waste from rich to poor countries.
- **National Sword policy**: China's 2018 ban on imported recyclables that redirected the global waste trade toward Southeast Asia.
- **Circular economy**: An economic model that keeps resources in use through reuse, repair, and recycling, replacing the linear "take-make-dispose" pattern.
