# 拉松的九十亿美元赌注：菲律宾水电与天然气能否撑起「低碳」转型

据《日经亚洲》报道，菲律宾最活跃的能源投资者之一、由亿万富翁恩里克·拉松（Enrique Razon）掌控的第一基建公司（Prime Infra）首席执行官吉约姆·卢奇（Guillaume Lucci）表示，公司计划在未来五年向水电与天然气项目投入「接近」九十亿美元（约648亿元人民币），押注菲律宾「强劲的低碳前景」。一句话，一家靠港口与水务起家的本土基础设施巨头，正把能源转型当成下一个利润中心。这桩买卖该怎么看？

## 一笔九十亿美元的赌注

九十亿美元不是小数。以菲律宾经济规模计，这约等于其年度国内生产总值（GDP）的百分之二。第一基建把这笔钱锁死在水电与天然气两条线上，逻辑很清楚：水电提供低碳基荷，天然气作为「过渡燃料」填补风光的间歇性缺口。在东南亚，敢把身家押在能源转型上的私人资本并不多，拉松算是少数几个既握现金、又懂基建交付的人。

但问题也随之而来：九十亿美元听起来气壮，落到群岛国家的电网里，却要先过审批、征地与并网三道关。

## 拉松的「基础设施帝国」为何转向能源

拉松的发家靠的是国际集装箱码头服务公司（ICTSI）——在全球二十多个国家运营码头；后来又切入水务、机场与度假村。第一基建是他近年搭建的基础设施控股平台，先后把供水、垃圾发电与可再生能源资产收进囊中。从码头到电厂，看似跨界，实则一脉相承：都是资本密集、特许经营、长周期回报的「重资产」生意。能源转型恰好给了这个帝国一块又大又稳的新拼图。

值得玩味的是，拉松向来以「押注国运」著称。当年接手马尼拉码头时，菲律宾还被视为投资风险地，如今他把筹码挪到能源，赌的同样是菲律宾未来十年的用电增量与脱碳压力。

## 「过渡燃料」的两难：水电打底，天然气补位

水电与天然气并称「过渡组合」，背后是菲律宾能源结构的一道算术题。这个群岛国家地热发电规模全球前列，水力与光伏资源也不缺，但电网分散、调度困难，风电光伏一多就「看天吃饭」。水电可充当稳定基荷，可遇上旱季与生态争议又自身难保；于是天然气被请来当「补位队员」——启停快、碳排放比煤低，被视为通向净零的桥梁。

可这座桥有多长，谁也说不准。一旦天然气电站建多了，二十年的资产寿命就会把排放「锁」在系统里，与净零目标自相矛盾。所谓过渡，究竟是真过渡，还是给化石能源续命，外人很难分辨。

## 群岛电网的软肋：间歇性与储能缺口

菲律宾由七千多个岛屿组成，电网本就支离破碎，吕宋、米沙鄢、棉兰老三大系统互联不足。可再生能源比例越高，对调峰与储能的要求就越苛刻。第一基建若真要上马数十亿美元级的水电与天然气项目，配套的输电线路、电池储能与调度系统才是真正的吞金兽。设备可以买，但跨岛联网的政治与工程难度，远非一家公司能独自化解。

这也解释了为何菲律宾一面高喊脱碳，一面仍要拼命进口煤炭与液化天然气（LNG）——基荷不够，化石兜底。转型的口号很响，落地的账单很贵。

## 中国工程商的舞台：水电建设的隐形主角

水电是资本与工程的双重游戏，而工程这块，中国企业的存在感极强。从大坝到机组，中国电建、中国能建等公司在东南亚的水电总承包市场上长期占据主导。菲律宾若真掀起一轮水电建设潮，中国工程商几乎是绕不开的承包商选项。

这层关系微妙：一边是菲律宾在安全上靠拢美国、在南海与中国角力；另一边是能源基建高度依赖中国工程能力。经济靠中国建、安全靠美国保，马尼拉的「两面下注」在能源领域表现得格外赤裸。

## 能源主权与中东阴影：进口燃料的地缘风险

天然气终究要进口。菲律宾几乎没有自有产能，液化天然气（LNG）终端的接连上马，意味着它把能源安全的筹码压在了海上通道与中东局势上。一旦霍尔木兹海峡生变，亚洲的LNG现货价格就会应声跳涨——而菲律宾的发电厂正是这些现货的买家。

据《日经亚洲》此前披露，日本机构已派专家赴马尼拉，协助菲方设计石油储备方案。一个连石油都要找邻国帮忙规划的国家，其天然气命脉又有多少自主可言？能源主权，对群岛国家从来都是奢侈品。

## 「低碳」标签下的争议：天然气仍是化石能源

第一基建把这笔投资包装成「低碳前景」，话术无可厚非，但逻辑要拆开看。水电确属低碳，天然气却仍是化石燃料——只不过比煤「干净」一些。把两者打包叫「低碳转型」，既迎合了资本市场的叙事，也模糊了天然气长期锁碳的事实。

环保组织与学界对此早有警惕：若九十亿美元大头流向天然气，菲律宾无非是把对煤炭的依赖，换成对进口天然气的依赖，换汤不换药。真正的脱碳，需要的是电网互联、储能与需求侧管理，而这些恰恰最难、最慢、最不性感。

## 落地之难：审批、征地与执行拖延

菲律宾并非没有蓝图。政府年度基础设施支出目标高达约220亿美元（约1584亿元人民币），却连续数月拨款放缓，折射出审批冗长、地方层级征地受阻、环保合规反复的痼疾。第一基建再财大气粗，也绕不开这套体制摩擦。

更大的隐忧是电价。菲律宾居民电价本就位居亚太前列，若转型成本一味转嫁用户，脱碳就会变成另一种形式的「生活成本危机」——这正是邻国泰国的教训。

## 私人赌注，国家难题

说到底，拉松的九十亿美元是一笔私人赌注，却照见了菲律宾的国家难题：既要保增长，又要促绿色，还要稳电网，三者难以兼得。私人资本愿意下注，说明方向没错；但把国家能源转型压在少数巨头的商业判断上，本身就是一种脆弱。

对区域而言，这也是一面镜子——东南亚的脱碳，终究要靠资本、工程与制度三条腿走路。拉松迈出了资本这条腿，剩下的，需要马尼拉与整个东盟（ASEAN）共同补齐。

## 延伸阅读

**液化天然气（LNG）**：天然气经超低温液化后的运输形态，菲律宾靠进口LNG填补燃气电厂缺口，却也将能源安全绑上中东与海上通道。

**地热发电（Geothermal）**：菲律宾地热装机全球前列，是少数可稳定调峰的本土低碳电源，却受地质与环评制约难以无限扩张。

**能源转型（Energy Transition）**：从化石向可再生能源的结构性切换，本文所谓「过渡燃料」争论，正是转型路径选择的核心分歧。

# Razon's $9 Billion Gamble: Can Philippine Hydropower and Gas Underpin a "Low-Carbon" Shift

Nikkei Asia reports that Prime Infrastructure Capital — one of the Philippines' most active energy players, controlled by billionaire Enrique Razon — plans to pour "close to" $9 billion over the next five years into hydropower and gas projects, betting on what its chief executive Guillaume Lucci called the country's "robust low-carbon prospects." In plain terms, a homegrown infrastructure giant built on ports and water is now treating the energy transition as its next profit centre. How should we read this deal?

## A $9 billion bet

Nine billion dollars is no small sum. Measured against the Philippine economy, it is roughly 2% of annual GDP. Prime Infra is committing that money to two lines — hydro and gas — and the logic is straightforward: hydro supplies low-carbon baseload, while gas serves as a "transition fuel" filling the intermittency gap left by wind and solar. In a region where few private players dare to stake their balance sheets on the energy transition, Razon is among the rare few who combine cash with delivery experience.

But the question follows immediately: $9 billion sounds grand, yet dropped into an archipelagic grid it must first clear three hurdles — permitting, land acquisition and grid connection.

## Why Razon's infrastructure empire is turning to energy

Razon made his name through International Container Terminal Services Inc (ICTSI), which runs ports across more than twenty countries; he later branched into water, airports and resorts. Prime Infra is the infrastructure holding platform he assembled in recent years, folding water supply, waste-to-energy and renewables assets under one roof. From wharves to power plants looks like a leap, but it is the same game: capital-intensive, concession-based, long-cycle "heavy assets." The energy transition happens to offer this empire a new, large and stable piece of the puzzle.

What is telling is Razon's habit of "betting on the national story." When he took over Manila's terminals, the Philippines was still viewed as a risky investment destination; now he is moving chips to energy, wagering on the country's electricity-demand growth and decarbonisation pressure over the next decade.

## The dilemma of a "transition fuel": hydro as base, gas as filler

Hydro and gas form a so-called "transition combo," behind which sits an arithmetic problem in the Philippine energy mix. The archipelagic state ranks among the world's leaders in geothermal capacity and is rich in hydro and solar, yet its grid is fragmented and hard to dispatch; the more wind and solar, the more it "depends on the weather." Hydro can provide stable baseload, but droughts and ecological disputes undermine it; gas is then invited as the "filler" — quick to ramp, lower-carbon than coal, a bridge to net zero.

How long that bridge is, no one can say. Once gas plants are built, their twenty-year asset life locks emissions into the system, contradicting net-zero goals. Whether the "transition" is genuine or merely a lifeline for fossil fuels is hard for outsiders to tell.

## The archipelago grid's weak spot: intermittency and storage gaps

Composed of more than 7,000 islands, the Philippines has a fractured grid, with the Luzon, Visayas and Mindanao systems poorly interconnected. The higher the renewable share, the more demanding the requirements for peaking and storage. If Prime Infra truly launches several billion-dollar hydro and gas projects, the accompanying transmission lines, battery storage and dispatch systems are the real money pits. Equipment can be bought, but the political and engineering difficulty of cross-island interconnection is beyond any single company to resolve alone.

This also explains why the Philippines, while loudly declaring decarbonisation, still imports coal and LNG voraciously — when baseload falls short, fossil fuels backstop it. The slogan of transition is loud; the bill of delivery is expensive.

## A stage for Chinese engineers: the invisible lead in hydro construction

Hydropower is a dual game of capital and engineering, and on the engineering side Chinese firms are overwhelmingly present. From dams to turbine units, companies such as PowerChina and Energy China have long dominated the regional hydropower EPC market in Southeast Asia. Should the Philippines truly trigger a hydro construction boom, Chinese engineering contractors are almost unavoidable.

The relationship is delicate: on one side the Philippines tilts toward the United States on security and jousts with China in the South China Sea; on the other its energy infrastructure leans heavily on Chinese engineering capacity. "Economy built by China, security guaranteed by the US" — Manila's two-sided hedge is nowhere more naked than in the energy sector.

## Energy sovereignty and the Middle East shadow: the geopolitical risk of imported fuel

Gas, in the end, must be imported. The Philippines has almost no domestic production; the successive launches of LNG terminals mean it is staking its energy security on sea lanes and Middle East stability. Should the Strait of Hormuz flare up, Asian LNG spot prices jump in response — and Philippine power plants are precisely those spot buyers.

As Nikkei Asia disclosed earlier, Japanese agencies have sent experts to Manila to help design an oil-reserve scheme. A country that must ask a neighbour to plan even its petroleum reserves — how much autonomy does its gas lifeline really enjoy? Energy sovereignty has always been a luxury for archipelagic states.

## The dispute behind the "low-carbon" label: gas is still fossil

Prime Infra dresses this investment as "low-carbon prospects" — fair as marketing, but the logic must be unpacked. Hydro is genuinely low-carbon; gas remains a fossil fuel, merely "cleaner" than coal. Bundling the two as a "low-carbon transition" both caters to capital-market narratives and blurs the fact that gas locks in carbon for the long term.

Environmental groups and academics have long warned: if the bulk of $9 billion flows to gas, the Philippines merely swaps dependence on coal for dependence on imported gas — same medicine, different bottle. Real decarbonisation requires grid interconnection, storage and demand-side management, all of which are the hardest, slowest and least glamorous.

## The difficulty of delivery: permits, land and delay

The Philippines is not short of blueprints. The government's annual infrastructure spending target reaches 1.3 trillion pesos (about $22 billion), yet disbursements have slowed for months, reflecting chronic ills of lengthy approvals, local-level land-acquisition blockages and repeated environmental-compliance friction. However deep Prime Infra's pockets, it cannot bypass this institutional friction.

A bigger worry is the power tariff. Philippine household electricity rates already rank among the highest in Asia; if transition costs are simply passed to users, decarbonisation becomes another form of "cost-of-living crisis" — precisely Thailand's lesson.

## A private bet, a national puzzle

Ultimately, Razon's $9 billion is a private wager that illuminates a national puzzle: the Philippines must pursue growth, greenness and grid at once, and the three are hard to reconcile. Private capital's willingness to bet signals the direction is right; but leaning a country's energy transition on the commercial judgment of a few tycoons is itself a fragility.

For the region, this is also a mirror — Southeast Asia's decarbonisation ultimately rests on three legs: capital, engineering and institutions. Razon has moved the capital leg; the rest must be filled by Manila and the wider ASEAN together.

## Further reading

**Liquefied natural gas (LNG):** Gas cooled to a super-low temperature for transport; the Philippines relies on imported LNG to feed gas-fired plants, yet this also ties its energy security to the Middle East and sea lanes.

**Geothermal power:** The Philippines ranks among the world's leaders in geothermal capacity — a rare stable, dispatchable domestic low-carbon source, yet constrained by geology and environmental assessment from unlimited expansion.

**Energy transition:** The structural shift from fossil fuels to renewables; the "transition fuel" debate here is exactly the core divide in choosing transition pathways.
