# 一条光缆，四方下注：越南MViSTA海底电缆与日本10亿美元的数字基建棋局

## 东京签字桌：一份10亿美元的谅解备忘录

11月9日，东京。据《日经亚洲》（Nikkei Asia）报道，越南斯塔维安集团（Stavian Group）与日本米达斯资本（Midas Capital）在此签署数字基础设施战略合作谅解备忘录，预计总投资10亿美元（约72亿元人民币）。签字现场并不寻常——越共中央总书记、国家主席苏林正在对日本进行国事访问，亲自见证了这份文件的交换。企业签约上升到元首见证的规格，说明这已不只是两家公司的事。

不妨先泼一盆冷水：谅解备忘录不是合同。双方明确表示，具体项目还需进一步评估、尽职调查和后续实施协议。换句话说，10亿美元是意向，不是已经到账的钱。但同样的逻辑反过来也成立：如果只是一张没有内容的意向书，不会惊动国家元首。真正值得追问的是，这笔意向背后到底装了什么。

## MViSTA是什么：2500公里、400太比特的四国拼盘

合作框架下的旗舰项目名为MViSTA，一条开放接入的国际海底光缆系统。它从越南头顿（Vung Tau）出发，连接马来西亚柔佛州的彭加兰（Pengerang）、新加坡的大士（Tuas）以及泰国，全长约2500公里，采用16对光纤，设计容量400太比特每秒。按行业网站披露，海缆系统本身的资本开支就超过3亿美元（约21.6亿元人民币）。

更有意思的是股东名单：越南斯塔维安、日本米达斯、马来西亚OMS集团、新加坡LitUp，四个国家的企业凑在一张桌前；项目由新加坡注册的特殊目的公司亚洲中心一号（AsiaHub One）负责融资、建设与运营。日方还拉来了日本电信电话公司（NTT）签署意向书，充当锚定客户。这是一条把越、日、马、新、泰五方利益绑在一起的数字航道——注意，它没有中国大陆和台湾地区参与。在海底光缆这个高度政治化的领域，这份名单本身就是一种表态。

## 为什么先修这条线：被割断的旧路由

要理解越南为什么急着修这条线，得先看它过去承受了什么。越南的国际互联网长期依赖少数几条经过南海的海缆，这些线路多次因故障或外力受损而中断，一旦出事，整个国家的跨境带宽就会掉到危险的低位。对一个人均国内生产总值刚跨过5000美元（约3.6万元人民币）、正把数据中心和云计算当作下一轮增长引擎的国家来说，带宽中断不只是网速问题，而是投资环境问题。

MViSTA打的是"绕开旧路"的算盘：它走的是越南—马来—新加坡—泰国的近海走廊，定位为的一条"特快通道"，把区域算力集群直接接驳到东亚的一级互联网骨干。这里要点评一句：越南这些年在制造业上尝到了"China+1"的甜头，如今想在数字基建上复制同样的逻辑——不把鸡蛋放在一条海缆里。这个思路本身没有错，但它把数字主权这件事，简化成了路由多元化的一道技术题。

## 日本的算盘：从数字丝路到电缆外交

东京在这件事上的热心，不能只用"援助"来解释。近年来中国企业在东南亚海底光缆建设中动作频频，从投资规模到施工速度都形成了明显优势，东京方面对此一直有焦虑。据行业分析，日本此番以民间资本牵头、拉上马来西亚与新加坡伙伴、再让NTT做锚定客户，本质上是想证明：不靠中国，同样能在东盟拼出一条高标准海缆。

这套打法和日在能源、电网领域的布局一脉相承——用"规则+融资+技术标准"的组合，在东南亚铺开一张以日本为枢纽的基建网络。区别在于，海底光缆比油罐和电网更接近数据主权这个敏感神经。谁掌握光缆，谁就在一定程度上掌握了一国的数据出入口。日本把MViSTA包装成"开放接入"的公共产品，听起来很中性；但当一条光缆的投资方、运营方和锚定客户都来自同一个国家阵营时，"开放"二字到底向谁开放，是个值得留到后面慢慢看的问题。

## 谅解备忘录不是合同：10亿美元的注水与真实

回到那个最容易被误读的数字。10亿美元是整个合作框架的预计规模，横跨四大领域：连接国际市场（含日本）的海缆、数据中心与数字园区及人工智能算力、国际融资与伙伴对接、技术转让与人才培养。把它全部记在MViSTA一条海缆头上，是媒体标题制造的第一个幻觉——海缆系统本身的资本开支超过3亿美元（约21.6亿元人民币），其余的大头指向的是胡志明市那座规划近300公顷的斯塔维安数字园（Stavian Digital Park）。

第二个幻觉是进度。谅解备忘录签署后，项目还要过尽调、融资闭合、海底勘察、多国登陆许可等一道道关。跨境海缆需要穿越四国海域，每一处登陆点都意味着一套监管审批；行业里从签字到通缆拖上三五年并不罕见。前文提到的那句"具体项目需进一步评估"，翻译过来就是：现在谁都不能保证它一定会建成。观察者网式地问一句——如果三年后这条缆还在纸面上，谁来负责？目前没有答案。

## 数据园区与算力：光缆只是上半场

真正让越南动心的，恐怕不是那条缆本身，而是缆后面的东西。斯塔维安集团在胡志明市推进的数字园，规划近300公顷，直指超大规模云服务商、人工智能数据中心和算力密集型企业；其可行性研究由美国贸易发展署（USTDA）资助，戴德梁行牵头，APTelecom担任电信专业分包。把这条信息拼进来看，逻辑就清楚了：先铺国际海缆解决"数据进得来出得去"，再建园区解决"数据留得下算得动"，最后用开放接入把两者焊成一个生态。

这是东南亚国家追赶数字经济的典型路径——新加坡早就这么干了，马来西亚的柔佛正在这么干，如今轮到越南。但要害在于，海缆和园区是两条完全不同的赛道：前者靠海底工程与多国协调，后者靠电力、土地和稳定的监管。越南在数据中心的电力供应和监管确定性上仍有明显短板。一边是资本愿意下注的故事，一边是基础设施能否兑现的现实，这道落差不会因为一份谅解备忘录就自动填平。

## 华商的位置：从承建方到规则接受者

对中国及东南亚华商而言，这条光缆不是一条与自己无关的新闻。马来西亚OMS集团、新加坡LitUp都身处华人主导的区域商业网络之中；在柔佛、新加坡、胡志明市布局数据中心与云服务的华商资本，更是这条"数字航道"潜在的客户与竞争者。过去二十年，华商在东南亚基建中的角色主要是承建与供货；数字基建时代，他们有机会成为运营方和生态组织者。

但机会的另一面是身份的尴尬。以美国为首的阵营近年不断以"数据安全"为由，审查中国背景资本参与的海缆与数据中心项目；一条由日、马、新、越企业主导、刻意排除中国大陆的"开放"光缆，很可能成为区域数字规则的新样板。华商若想进入这样的生态，就要接受别人定的接入规则、合规门槛与股权审查。这里必须说清楚：参与规则制定，和被动接受规则，是两回事。华商需要争取的是前者，而不是满足于当一个被审核的承租人。

## 三个待解之问：登陆权、融资、开放接入

第一个问题是登陆权。海缆要靠岸，越南、马来西亚、新加坡、泰国四国的审批缺一不可；任何一国以安全或环保名义拖延，整条链的工期就会连锁后移。第二个问题是融资。10亿美元框架里，究竟多少是股权、多少是债务、谁来做担保、日方机构是否提供出口信贷，目前一概未披露；没有这些信息，就无从判断项目的资金强度。

第三个问题，也是最关键的，是"开放接入"怎么落实。开放接入意味着第三方运营商可以按公开、非歧视的价格购买容量，这是它区别于中国式"私有专线"的卖点。但如果锚定客户拿走了大部分初始容量，或者定价权集中在运营方手里，所谓开放就会变成一句公关辞令。据行业消息，NTT的意向书已经签下，其余客户的容量分配规则却尚未公布。判断这条缆成色几何，不看它宣称多少太比特，而看它的容量怎么分、价格怎么定。

## 结语：数字主权不能靠一条光缆兑现

平心而论，MViSTA不是坏消息。它给越南多了一条出海通道，给东盟多了一个由区域内资本主导的数字基建样本，也让日本在数字丝路之外提供了一个替代选项——多元供给总比单一依赖好。

但要警惕把基建项目包装成战略胜利的冲动。一条光缆解决不了数据本地化立法、跨境数据流动的互认、算力的电力瓶颈，更解决不了"谁来控制数据"这个根本问题。越南想做区域数据枢纽，靠的是稳定的监管、合格的工程和可持续的需求，而不是签字桌上的元首见证和新闻标题里的10亿美元。同样，对华商来说，真正的启示不是又一条缆要通了，而是：当区域数字基建的规则在东京、在吉隆坡、在新加坡被一条条写定时，我们有没有坐在写规则的桌前。

## 延伸阅读

**海底光缆（Submarine Cable）**：铺设于海底、承载跨国互联网绝大部分数据流量的光纤通信线路。全球超过95%的洲际数据流量经由海缆传输，其登陆点与安全一直是大国竞争的焦点。

**开放接入（Open Access）**：一种海缆运营模式，要求容量以公开、非歧视的价格向第三方运营商开放，避免单一股东垄断。它与"私有专线"相对，是多边数字基建倡议常用的卖点，但落实程度取决于定价与容量分配机制。

**数字主权（Digital Sovereignty）**：一国对境内数据的存储、流动与基础设施拥有自主控制能力的主张。近年被东南亚国家广泛用于论证数据本地化立法与本土算力投资的合理性。

# One Cable, Four Bets: Vietnam's MViSTA Subsea Cable and Japan's US$1 Billion Digital Infrastructure Gamble

## The signing in Tokyo: a US$1 billion memorandum

On 9 November, in Tokyo. According to Nikkei Asia, Vietnam's Stavian Group and Japan's Midas Capital signed a memorandum of understanding on digital infrastructure cooperation, with planned investment of US$1 billion. The setting was unusual: To Lam, Vietnam's general secretary and state president, was on a state visit to Japan and personally witnessed the exchange of documents. When a corporate signing is elevated to head-of-state level, it is no longer merely a matter for two companies.

Let us pour some cold water first. An MoU is not a contract. Both parties made clear that specific projects require further assessment, due diligence and subsequent implementation agreements. The US$1 billion is intent, not money in the bank. But the reverse also holds: a hollow letter of intent would not command a head of state's attention. The question worth asking is what, exactly, this intention contains.

## What MViSTA is: 2,500 kilometres and 400 terabits in a four-country consortium

The flagship project under the framework is MViSTA, an open-access international subsea cable system. Running from Vung Tau in Vietnam to Pengerang in Johor, Malaysia, Tuas in Singapore and Thailand, it spans roughly 2,500 kilometres with 16 fibre pairs and a design capacity of 400 terabits per second. Industry sources put the cable system's own capital expenditure at more than US$300 million.

More telling is the shareholder list: Stavian of Vietnam, Midas Capital of Japan, Malaysia's OMS Group and Singapore's LitUp, with a Singapore-incorporated special purpose vehicle, AsiaHub One, handling financing, construction and operation. Nippon Telegraph and Telephone (NTT) has signed a letter of intent as anchor customer. This is a digital corridor binding the interests of Vietnam, Japan, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand — note that neither mainland China nor Taiwan is involved. In the highly politicised world of subsea cables, that roster is itself a statement.

## Why this route first: the severed legacy lines

To understand Vietnam's urgency, look at what it has endured. The country's international connectivity has long depended on a handful of cables crossing the South China Sea, several of which have suffered repeated outages from faults or external damage. Each incident has pushed national cross-border bandwidth into dangerous territory. For a country whose GDP per capita has just crossed US$5,000 and which is betting on data centres and cloud computing as its next growth engine, an outage is not a matter of slow browsing; it is an investment-climate problem.

MViSTA is designed to bypass the old routes: an express coastal corridor linking Vietnam to regional computing clusters and, through them, to tier-one East Asian internet backbones. Here is the commentary: Vietnam has tasted the gains of "China+1" in manufacturing, and now wants to replicate that logic in digital infrastructure — not putting all its eggs in one cable. That instinct is sound. But it reduces digital sovereignty to a technical exercise in route diversification.

## Japan's calculus: from Digital Silk Road to cable diplomacy

Tokyo's enthusiasm cannot be explained by "aid" alone. Chinese companies have become prominent in Southeast Asian subsea cable construction, with advantages in scale and speed that have long unsettled Tokyo. By leading with private capital, bringing in Malaysian and Singaporean partners and installing NTT as anchor tenant, Japan is essentially demonstrating that a high-standard cable can be built in ASEAN without China.

This approach echoes Japan's plays in energy and grids: a combination of rules, financing and technical standards that spreads an infrastructure network centred on Tokyo across Southeast Asia. The difference is that subsea cables sit much closer to the nerve of data sovereignty. Whoever controls the cable partly controls a country's data gateway. Packaging MViSTA as an "open-access" public good sounds neutral. But when a cable's investors, operator and anchor customer all come from one camp, the question of who exactly this openness serves deserves to be asked.

## MoU is not a contract: the water in the US$1 billion headline

Back to the most misread number. The US$1 billion is the estimated scale of the entire framework, spanning four pillars: cables linking Vietnam to international markets including Japan; data centres, digital parks and AI computing infrastructure; international financing and partner mobilisation; and technology transfer, standards and workforce development. Attributing the whole sum to MViSTA alone is the first illusion created by the headline — the cable itself accounts for only the US$300 million-plus, with the bulk pointing to the nearly 300-hectare Stavian Digital Park in Ho Chi Minh City.

The second illusion is timing. After the signing, the project must clear due diligence, financial close, marine surveys and landing permits in multiple jurisdictions. A cross-border cable passing through four countries' waters needs a regulatory approval at every landing point; three-to-five-year gaps between signature and service are common in this industry. That phrase — "subject to further assessment" — translates as: nobody can guarantee it will be built. So ask the blunt question: if the cable is still on paper three years from now, who is accountable? There is no answer yet.

## Data parks and compute: the cable is only the first half

What truly excites Hanoi is probably not the cable itself but what sits behind it. Stavian's digital park in Ho Chi Minh City, planned at nearly 300 hectares, targets hyperscale cloud providers, AI data centres and compute-intensive enterprises; its feasibility study is funded by the US Trade and Development Agency, led by Cushman & Wakefield with APTelecom as telecom subcontractor. Assemble the pieces and the logic becomes clear: lay the international cable so data can move in and out, build the park so data can stay and be processed, then weld the two into an ecosystem through open access.

This is the standard path for Southeast Asian countries chasing a digital economy. Singapore did it years ago; Johor is doing it now; Vietnam wants its turn. The catch is that a cable and a park are two different races: one is marine engineering and multilateral coordination, the other is power, land and regulatory certainty. Vietnam still has visible gaps in electricity supply and regulatory predictability for data centres. On one side is a story capital is willing to back; on the other is the reality of whether infrastructure can actually be delivered. An MoU will not close that gap by itself.

## Where Chinese businesses fit: from builder to rule-taker

For Chinese and Southeast Asian ethnic-Chinese businesses, this cable is not unrelated news. Malaysia's OMS Group and Singapore's LitUp sit within the region's Chinese-led commercial networks, while Chinese capital with data-centre and cloud positions in Johor, Singapore and Ho Chi Minh City is a potential customer and competitor of this digital corridor. For two decades, Chinese businesses were mainly contractors and suppliers in Southeast Asian infrastructure. In the digital era they have a chance to become operators and ecosystem organisers.

But opportunity comes with an awkward identity. A US-led camp has increasingly scrutinised Chinese-backed cables and data centres on "data security" grounds. A nominally "open" cable led by Japanese, Malaysian, Singaporean and Vietnamese firms and built without mainland China may become a new template for regional digital rules. If Chinese businesses want into such an ecosystem, they must accept someone else's access rules, compliance thresholds and equity reviews. Let us be precise: helping write the rules and passively receiving them are two different things. Chinese business communities should fight for the former rather than settle for being an audited tenant.

## Three open questions: landings, financing, open access

First, landing rights. A cable must come ashore, and approvals from Vietnam, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand are all indispensable; a delay in any one, on security or environmental grounds, pushes back the entire schedule. Second, financing. How much of the US$1 billion framework is equity and how much debt, who provides guarantees, and whether Japanese institutions extend export credit — none of this has been disclosed. Without it, the project's funding strength cannot be judged.

Third and most decisive: how "open access" actually works. Open access means third-party carriers can buy capacity at published, non-discriminatory prices — the feature that distinguishes it from privately held lines and the selling point of multilateral digital initiatives. But if the anchor customer absorbs most initial capacity, or pricing power sits with the operator, openness becomes a public-relations phrase. NTT's letter of intent is signed; the allocation rules for everyone else are not public. To judge this cable, do not look at how many terabits it advertises. Look at how its capacity is allocated and how its prices are set.

## Conclusion: digital sovereignty cannot be cashed in with one cable

To be fair, MViSTA is not bad news. It gives Vietnam another route to sea, offers ASEAN a digital-infrastructure sample led by regional capital, and provides an alternative to the Digital Silk Road — diverse supply is better than dependence on one source.

But resist the urge to package an infrastructure project as a strategic victory. A cable cannot settle data-localisation legislation, mutual recognition of cross-border data flows, the power bottleneck behind computing, or the fundamental question of who controls data. If Vietnam wants to be a regional data hub, it needs stable regulation, competent engineering and sustained demand — not a head of state at the signing table and a US$1 billion figure in the headline. For Chinese businesses, the real lesson is not that another cable is coming. It is that as the rules of regional digital infrastructure are written, in Tokyo, Kuala Lumpur and Singapore, we should ask whether we are sitting at the table where they are written.

## Further reading

**Submarine cable**: a fibre-optic line laid on the seabed that carries the overwhelming majority of cross-border internet traffic. More than 95% of intercontinental data flows through such cables, whose landing points and security have become a focus of great-power competition.

**Open access**: a cable operating model requiring capacity to be offered to third-party carriers at published, non-discriminatory prices, preventing a single shareholder from monopolising it. Contrasted with privately held lines, it is a staple selling point of multilateral digital initiatives, though its substance depends on pricing and capacity-allocation mechanisms.

**Digital sovereignty**: the claim that a country should control the storage, movement and infrastructure of data within its borders. Southeast Asian governments have increasingly invoked it to justify data-localisation laws and domestic computing investment.
