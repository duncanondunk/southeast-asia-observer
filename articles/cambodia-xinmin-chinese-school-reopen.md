# 停办半个世纪后，柬埔寨一所华校重新开学：沉默如何被打破

2026年8月24日，在柬埔寨贡布省土城市，一口沉寂了约半个世纪的上课铃重新响起。创办于上世纪60年代的新民学校，曾是当地华侨华人子弟学习中文、传承中华文化的重要阵地。这一次复校开学，两百多名学生家长和地方政府官员共同出席了典礼。旧校舍毁于战乱、校园一度沦为平地；在近五十年里，当地始终没有一所正规的华文学校。

这看似只是一则地方教育新闻，但细读之下，它其实是东南亚华侨华人百年命运的缩影，也是一个自下而上被改写的、安静的历史注脚。

## 现场：一所学校重获新生

这次复校，并非政府工程，而是由侨社商界推动。据中国侨网报道，柬埔寨港澳侨商总会会长黄瑞华去年初发起募捐，号召香港热心侨商出资，筹得善款兴建起两栋教学楼；土城理事会则负责协调与筹备办学事宜。开学典礼上，黄开杨会长宣布新民学校正式恢复教学，并感谢柬华理事总会、黄瑞华及社会各界热心人士的支持。校方承诺将逐步完善课程建设，为当地培养更多通晓中柬双语的年轻一代。

有一个细节意味深长：学校自己定下的目标，不是要在海外"培养中国人"，而是培养"双语的柬埔寨年轻人"。这一表述很关键——它意味着这所机构既想扎根柬埔寨，又要保留住与中华文化相连的一根线。这正是现代离散群体"归属协商"的典型姿态：既不脱离居住国，也不割断故土。

## 漫长的沉默：华校为何在柬埔寨消失

要理解一所偏远小镇的华校复校为何能成为新闻，必须先回想华文教育在柬埔寨经历了什么。上世纪70年代以前，散居全国的华侨华人社区曾拥有密集的方言学校网络。随后是连绵的战乱，再然后是红色高棉（1975—1979）—其政权曾一律禁止少数民族语言与文化教育。全国范围内的华校被勒令关闭，教师四散，课本被焚。那不是一次暂停，而是一道断裂。新民学校的旧楼被毁之后，两代人的时间里都未能重建。

正因为如此，这半世纪的空白才格外沉重。它并非官僚程序上的拖延，而是一段试图抹去族群身份的历史留下的残渣。一所学校在2026年重新开学，本身就是一种微小而真实的历史修复。

## 谁来重建：侨社网络才是真正的引擎

这是通讯社报道往往轻描淡写的部分。这所学校的重生，没有哪个部委"下发"下来。把它拼起来的，是一张侨社网络：由一位与香港渊源深厚的商界人士担任会长的商会、当地的华人理事会，以及把分散的社区学校连成体系的柬华理事总会。在东南亚，华侨华人的机构从来都是这样建起来的——自下而上，由商人与社团出力，用社区自己的钱与力。

对于观察中国海外影响力的人，这种"自下而上"的特征恰恰是重点。北京的文化外交——由高校承办的"中华文化大乐园"夏令营、孔子学院、不断膨胀的生源——往往占据头条。但在柬埔寨的地面上，华文教育那套持久的基础设施，至今仍由离散群体自己拥有、自己运营。国家的项目是补充，侨社的社团才是地基。这一区分，应当同时给北京的鼓吹者和它的批评者降降温：谁都无法完全决定一所社区学校究竟教什么。

## 不止一所学校：华文教育在东南亚的复兴

新民学校只是区域森林里的一棵树。在整个东南亚，华文教育正迎来数十年来最强的复兴。在印尼，苏哈托时代被禁（2000年才解禁）的华校已遍地重生；在泰国，华语从边缘科目变成私立教育里的主流选择；在马来西亚，由华社自办的华文独立中学（独中）悄然成为区域内学术竞争力最强的学校之一；即便在华文基础较新的老挝，语言班也在快速扩张。

真正的驱动力不是意识形态，而是经济。当中国成为该地区最大的贸易伙伴与最大的投资来源，华语便从"遗产的包袱"变成了"可变现的技能"。曾经担心华文教育会让孩子被边缘化的家长，如今把它看作一种优势。那些在萧条年代守住学校的离散群体，现在正被一股更广泛、更务实的需求所加入、所资助。

## 更难的问题：文化、影响力与软权力的边界

这一切并非与政治无涉。柬埔寨常被描述为中国在东南亚最亲密的伙伴，而华文机构在那里扩张，不可避免地伴随着北京日益增长的足迹。河内或华盛顿的读者，把一所重新开张的华校读作"换了个名字的软权力基础设施"，情有可原。

这种读法不算错，但不够完整。重建新民学校的那个社区，其初衷早于、也将长于任何一个单一的大国时刻：让孩子拥有一门祖辈讲过的语言，让一种文化不至于消散，让一个少数族裔在它已世代栖居的国度里站稳脚跟。北京收获的软权力红利是真实的，但它所立足的地基，是侨社出于自身理由铺下的。两种逻辑并存，有时并不轻松。

诚实的结论是：这所学校的复校，两者皆是——既是一场真正的文化修复，也是一幅更宏大地缘拼图中的一小块瓷砖。只把它读作其中之一，都是误读。对贡布省的华侨华人而言，2026年8月24日那天，它只是这样一件事：一口沉默了五十年的铃，又响了。

# Fifty Years On: How a Khmer Rouge-Era Silence Ended at a Cambodian Chinese School

On 24 August 2026, in the small town of Tuek Chhou in Cambodia's Kampot Province, a school bell rang for the first time in roughly half a century. The Xinmin School (新民学校), founded in the 1960s as a place where local ethnic Chinese learned their heritage language, reopened its doors. More than 200 parents and local officials attended the ceremony. The old campus had been destroyed in war and the land flattened; for nearly fifty years the area had no dedicated Chinese school at all.

What looks like a local education story is, on closer reading, a condensed history of the overseas Chinese experience in Southeast Asia — and a quiet marker of how that history is being rewritten from the ground up.

## The scene: a school returns to life

The reopening was not a government initiative. It was driven by the overseas Chinese business community. According to China Qiaowang, Huang Ruihua (黄瑞华), chairman of the Cambodia Hong Kong–Macau Overseas Chinese Chamber of Commerce, last year began rallying Hong Kong patriotic merchants to donate funds, raising enough to build two teaching blocks. The local Tuek Chhou council (土城理事会) handled coordination and preparation. At the ceremony, Huang Kaiyang (黄开杨) announced the resumption of classes and thanked the Cambodian Chinese Federation (柬华理事总会), Huang Ruihua, and other supporters. The school pledged to gradually improve its curriculum and to cultivate a young generation fluent in both Chinese and Khmer.

There is a telling detail in the school's own promise: the goal is not to produce "Chinese" students abroad, but bilingual young Cambodians. That framing matters. It signals an institution that wants to belong to Cambodia while keeping a thread to Chinese culture — exactly the negotiation of belonging that defines the modern diaspora: rooted in the country of residence, yet not severed from the homeland.

## The long silence: why Chinese schools vanished in Cambodia

To understand why a reopened school in a provincial town makes news, one has to recall what happened to Chinese education in Cambodia. Before the 1970s, ethnic Chinese communities across the country ran a dense network of vernacular schools. Then came the war, and then the Khmer Rouge (1975–1979), whose regime banned minority-language and cultural education outright. Chinese schools across Cambodia were shuttered; teachers dispersed; textbooks burned. The interruption was not a pause but a rupture. When the Xinmin School's old building was destroyed, it was not rebuilt for two generations.

That rupture is the reason a 2026 reopening feels extraordinary. The half-century gap is not bureaucratic lag; it is the residue of one of the twentieth century's most violent attempts to erase communal identity. The fact that the school is coming back at all is a small but real piece of historical repair.

## Who rebuilt it: the diaspora network as the real engine

Here is the part the wire reports tend to understate. The rebirth of this school was not delivered by any ministry. It was assembled by a diaspora network: a chamber of commerce chaired by a Hong Kong–connected merchant, a local council of ethnic Chinese, and the Cambodian Chinese Federation that ties scattered community schools into a system. This is how ethnic Chinese institutions have always been built in Southeast Asia — from below, by merchants and associations, with the community's own money and labor.

For observers of China's overseas influence, that bottom-up character is the key point. Beijing's cultural diplomacy — the "Chinese Culture Paradise" (中华文化大乐园) camps run by Chinese universities, the Confucius Institutes, the swelling enrollments — tends to draw the headlines. But on the ground in Cambodia, the durable infrastructure of Chinese-language learning is still owned and operated by the diaspora itself. The state's programs arrive as supplements; the community's associations are the foundation. That distinction should temper both the enthusiasm of Beijing's boosters and the anxiety of its critics. Neither fully controls what a community school actually teaches.

## Beyond one school: Chinese-language education's Southeast Asian revival

The Xinmin School is one tree in a regional forest. Across Southeast Asia, Chinese-language education is enjoying its strongest revival in generations. In Indonesia, schools suppressed under the Suharto-era ban — lifted only in 2000 — have multiplied. In Thailand, Mandarin has moved from marginal to mainstream in private education. In Malaysia, the independent Chinese secondary schools (华文独中), run by the ethnic Chinese community, have quietly become some of the region's most academically competitive institutions. Even in Laos, where the Chinese-language base is newer, language classes are expanding fast.

The driver is not ideology but economics. As China became the region's largest trading partner and its biggest source of investment, Mandarin shifted from a heritage burden to a marketable skill. Parents who once worried that Chinese schooling might marginalize their children now see it as an edge. The diaspora, which preserved the schools through the lean decades, is now being joined — and funded — by a broader, pragmatic demand.

## The harder question: culture, influence, and the limits of soft power

None of this is innocent of politics. Cambodia is often described as China's closest partner in Southeast Asia, and the spread of Chinese-language institutions there inevitably travels alongside Beijing's growing footprint. A reader in Hanoi or Washington could be forgiven for reading a reopened Chinese school as soft-power infrastructure by another name.

That reading is not wrong, but it is incomplete. The community that rebuilt the Xinmin School did so for reasons that predate and outlast any single great-power moment: to give children a language their grandparents spoke, to keep a culture from dissolving, to anchor a minority's place in a country it has called home for centuries. The soft-power dividend for Beijing is real, yet it rests on a foundation the diaspora laid for its own reasons. The two logics coexist, sometimes uneasily.

The honest conclusion is that the school's reopening is both — a genuine act of cultural restoration and a small tile in a larger geopolitical mosaic. Pretending it is only one or the other is to misread it. For the overseas Chinese of Kampot, on 24 August 2026, it was simply this: a bell that had been silent for fifty years, ringing again.

## Further reading

**Cambodian Chinese Federation (Jinhua / 柬华理事总会)**: Cambodia's largest umbrella organization of ethnic Chinese associations, which coordinates Chinese schools and Chinese-language education nationwide and serves as the core hub for the community's self-organization and cultural transmission.

**Independent Chinese Secondary Schools (华文独中)**: Secondary schools in Malaysia run by the ethnic Chinese community and using Mandarin as the medium of instruction, outside the national examination system — a benchmark of the autonomy and resilience of Chinese education in Southeast Asia.

**Chinese Culture Paradise (中华文化大乐园)**: A flagship project under China's local overseas-affairs system that dispatches teachers to overseas Chinese schools for short-term courses in Chinese culture — martial arts, calligraphy, painting, dragon-lion dance — as a vehicle for people-to-people bonding.
