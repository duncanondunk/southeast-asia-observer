# 谷歌150亿美元加码马来西亚：算力热潮下的豪赌与算计

11月4日，谷歌母公司字母表公司（Alphabet）总裁兼首席投资官露丝·波拉特（Ruth Porat）与马来西亚（Malaysia）首相安华（Anwar Ibrahim）在吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）马来亚大学同台，宣布谷歌计划在2027至2028年向马来西亚追加150亿美元（约1080亿元人民币；谷歌方面折算约192亿新元，约1040亿元人民币）发展数码基础设施。这不是一笔普通的投资，而是东南亚算力竞赛升级的一个标志性注脚。

## 数字：七年前的七倍半

这一数字，是谷歌2024年承诺的20亿美元（约144亿元人民币）的七倍半。据联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）报道，资金将用于在工业区建设数据中心园区，同时扩大洁净能源供应、并加强马来西亚的水资源安全。

谷歌预计，新园区建设阶段每年平均为马国国内生产总值（GDP）贡献11亿美元（约79亿元人民币），并支持超过3.7万个本地就业岗位；数据中心投运后，每年可维持超过9000个本地岗位，平均工资比马国全国中位数高出45%。更早前，谷歌已宣布出资1.6亿美元（约11.5亿元人民币），在吉隆坡以南的森美兰州建设共享供水基础设施，兼顾自身运营与社区用水。

## 为什么是马来西亚

马来西亚并非偶然被选中的落脚点。相对低廉的运营成本、可靠的基础设施，以及毗邻新加坡等商业中心的地理位置，使它成为东南亚（Southeast Asia）最重要的数据中心枢纽。甲骨文、亚马逊、阿里巴巴、字节跳动等巨头早已在此布局，争抢人工智能热潮催生的存储与算力需求。

对谷歌而言，马来西亚既是其2024年首个东南亚数据中心与云端区域的延伸，也是绕开土地资源紧绷、成本高企的新加坡、在东盟（ASEAN）腹地就近服务区域市场的一步棋。从新加坡到吉隆坡不过一小时航程，却拉开了成本与空间的落差。截至今年7月，马国已批准27个数据中心项目，总额约2259.7亿林吉特（约554亿美元，约3990亿元人民币）。

## “绿色”承诺背后的算计

真正值得注意的是谷歌对环境的“捆绑式”承诺。安华特别强调，谷歌将与国家能源公司合作，为马国电网新增超过1吉瓦（GW）的洁净能源装机容量；谷歌承诺自行承担所需电费，不依赖政府补贴，也不与马国家庭争夺电力。

水资源方面，谷歌承诺补充的淡水量将超过其营运消耗，并计划兴建离河蓄水池与水处理厂，每日供应6500万公升饮用水；工程完工后，设施所有权移交雪兰莪州政府，成为永久公共资产，并可降低季候风期间水灾风险、保障旱季水源。

这套说辞并非凭空而来。过去一年，东南亚数据中心热潮已引发公众对水电紧张、社区许可的反弹——耗电巨兽与居民抢电、抽水的画面，正不断侵蚀科技公司的社会许可（social licence）。谷歌把洁净能源与“水正效益”打包进投资案，更像是一场未雨绸缪的“社会许可”续费：先用绿色承诺买下扩张的门票。

## 谁真正受益

就业账本看似漂亮：3.7万个建设期岗位、9000个运营期岗位、工资高出中位数45%。但冷静拆解，建设期岗位多为阶段性、临时性的工程与安装岗位；运营期岗位虽稳定，却集中在技术员、机电工程师、物流与安保等执行层。

更关键的是价值归属。数据中心的核心——算力、算法、数据与知识产权——仍牢牢掌握在谷歌等美国科技公司手中。马来西亚收获的是税收、部分就业与“数字强国”的叙事，而真正的高附加值与战略主动权，依旧留在硅谷。这恰是发展中国家承接算力基建时普遍面临的“价值链捕获”难题。

## 地缘政治的“中立避风港”

这场投资潮的另一层底色，是中美科技博弈。一边是谷歌、微软、亚马逊、甲骨文等美国巨头，一边是阿里巴巴、字节跳动等中国科技企业，竟不约而同地把马来西亚当作区域算力的承接地。马来西亚凭借不选边的中立姿态，同时吸纳两方的资本与技术。

对吉隆坡而言，这是难得的战略红利：在大国角力中左右逢源，把“避风港”地位转化为实打实的外商直接投资（FDI）。但红利的另一面，是当大国关系生变时，这座“中立枢纽”能否独善其身，仍是未知数。

## 冷静看热潮

热闹之外，仍需几句冷话。其一，150亿美元落子于2027至2028年，时间跨度不短，政治与监管变数犹存；其二，马来西亚电网仍以燃煤为主，1吉瓦洁净能源的并网速度存疑；其三，马国刚规定数据中心须含至少三成本地成分，外资供应链被绑定在地企业，合规成本将抬升；其四，“水正效益”“自行承担电费”等承诺，仍待第三方核验。

算力热潮汹涌，马来西亚站在了风口。但风口之上，究竟是腾飞，还是被吹得晕头转向，时间会给出答案。

## 延伸阅读

**外商直接投资（Foreign Direct Investment, FDI）**：指一国企业对他国企业或项目进行的长期、可控投资，通常伴随技术转移与就业创造，是发展中国家承接产业转移的主要渠道。

**数据中心（Data Centre）**：集中部署服务器、存储与网络设备的设施，为云计算与人工智能服务提供算力支撑，其扩张高度依赖稳定且廉价的电力与水资源。

**数字主权（Digital Sovereignty）**：一国对境内数据、算力与数字基础设施保有控制权与规则制定权的能力，是发展中国家在承接全球算力基建时日益重视的议题。

# Google's US$15 Billion Malaysia Bet: Hype, Hedging, and Hard Questions

On 4 November, Alphabet president and chief investment officer Ruth Porat and Malaysian prime minister Anwar Ibrahim took the stage together at the University of Malaya in Kuala Lumpur to announce that Google plans to pour a further US$15 billion into Malaysia's digital infrastructure across 2027 and 2028. This is no ordinary investment — it is a marker of how sharply the Southeast Asian compute race has escalated.

## The numbers: seven and a half times the 2024 pledge

The figure is seven and a half times the US$2 billion Google committed in 2024 — a step change, not a top-up. According to Lianhe Zaobao, the money will fund new data-centre campuses in industrial zones while expanding clean-energy supply and strengthening the country's water security.

Google estimates the construction phase alone will add some US$1.1 billion a year to Malaysian GDP on average and support more than 37,000 local jobs; once operational, the centres are expected to sustain over 9,000 positions, paying wages roughly 45 per cent above the national median. Earlier, Google had already committed US$160 million to build shared water infrastructure in Negeri Sembilan, south of Kuala Lumpur, serving both its own operations and the surrounding community.

## Why Malaysia

Malaysia is no accident of geography. Lower operating costs, reliable infrastructure, and proximity to commercial hubs such as Singapore have made it Southeast Asia's single most important data-centre hub. Oracle, Amazon, Alibaba, and ByteDance have all planted flags there, racing to meet the storage and compute demand unleashed by the artificial-intelligence boom.

For Google, Malaysia extends its first Southeast Asian data centre and cloud region announced in 2024, and offers a way to serve the regional market from the ASEAN hinterland while sidestepping Singapore's land constraints and sky-high costs. Kuala Lumpur is barely an hour's flight from the city-state — yet the gap in cost and space is cavernous. As of July, Malaysia had approved 27 data-centre projects worth RM225.97 billion (about US$55 billion).

## The calculus behind the "green" pledge

What is striking is how Google has bundled environmental commitments into the deal. Anwar stressed that Google will work with the national utility to add more than 1 gigawatt (GW) of clean-energy capacity to the grid, and that the company will bear its own electricity costs without government subsidy and without competing with households for power.

On water, Google promises to replenish more freshwater than it consumes, building an off-river reservoir and a treatment plant capable of supplying 65 million litres of drinking water a day; once completed, the assets will be handed to the Selangor state government as permanent public property, while also reducing monsoon flood risk and securing dry-season reserves.

This framing is not accidental. Over the past year, Southeast Asia's data-centre frenzy has triggered public backlash over strained power and water and the question of community consent — the image of electricity- and water-guzzling behemoths competing with residents has eroded the social licence of tech firms. By packaging clean energy and "water positivity" into the investment, Google looks less like a polluter and more like a partner pre-buying the permission to expand.

## Who really benefits

The jobs ledger looks impressive: 37,000 construction-phase roles, 9,000 operational roles, wages 45 per cent above the median. But unpack it and the construction jobs are largely temporary, project-bound engineering and installation work, while operational roles, though stable, sit at the execution layer — technicians, mechanical and electrical engineers, logistics and security staff.

The bigger question is where the value lands. The core of a data centre — compute, algorithms, data, and intellectual property — remains firmly in the hands of Google and its American peers. Malaysia collects taxes, some employment, and a "digital powerhouse" narrative, while the high-value-added strategic control stays in Silicon Valley. That is the familiar "value-chain capture" dilemma facing developing economies that host compute infrastructure.

## A neutral haven in the geopolitical contest

Underneath the investment wave lies the US–China technology contest. On one side, American giants such as Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Oracle; on the other, Chinese players including Alibaba and ByteDance — all, improbably, have chosen Malaysia as their regional compute landing pad. Kuala Lumpur, by refusing to pick sides, absorbs capital and technology from both camps.

For Malaysia, this is a rare strategic dividend: hedging between great powers and converting "neutral haven" status into concrete foreign direct investment. The flip side, of course, is the open question of whether a neutral hub can stay insulated if great-power relations sour.

## A sober look at the hype

Beyond the fanfare, a few cold notes. First, the US$15 billion is staged for 2027–2028, a long enough horizon for political and regulatory shifts. Second, Malaysia's grid remains coal-heavy, so the pace of integrating 1 GW of clean energy is uncertain. Third, the country has just mandated that data centres contain at least 30 per cent local content, binding foreign supply chains to domestic firms and raising compliance costs. Fourth, pledges of "water positivity" and "self-funded electricity" still await independent verification.

The compute gold rush is real, and Malaysia is squarely in its path. Whether it takes off or simply gets blown about — that answer will come from time, not press releases.

## Further reading

**Foreign Direct Investment (FDI):** Long-term, controlling investment by a firm from one country into another, typically bringing technology transfer and jobs — the main channel through which developing economies absorb industrial relocation.

**Data Centre:** A facility housing servers, storage, and networking equipment that underpins cloud computing and artificial-intelligence services; its expansion depends heavily on stable, cheap power and water.

**Digital Sovereignty:** A state's capacity to retain control and rule-making authority over its data, compute, and digital infrastructure — an increasingly salient concern for developing countries hosting global compute.
