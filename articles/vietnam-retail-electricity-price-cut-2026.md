# 越南下调零售电价2%：制造神话背后的能源账单

越南政府10月31日宣布，自11月1日起将全国平均零售电价下调2%。据路透社与越南媒体综合报道，这一调整由越南电力集团（EVN）于10月30日正式公布：不含增值税的平均零售电价将降至每千瓦时2159.9越南盾（约0.62元人民币），降幅恰为现行均价的2%。副总理阮文胜在国会就经济、财政与预算议题答询时表示，政府正指示EVN紧急压缩成本，力争在即日起至2027年3月31日期间把零售电价压低约2%。

表面看，这不过是一纸2%的调价通知。但若把它放进越南近年「世界工厂2.0」的叙事里，电价的每一次松动，都折射出这个国家增长模式里最被忽视的那笔账。

## 数字：2%与2159.9越南盾

先讲清事实。越南的零售电价由国有垄断企业EVN统一制定、政府审批，并非市场自由浮动。此次下调前，越南平均零售电价约为每千瓦时2204越南盾（约0.63元人民币）；下调2%后为2159.9越南盾（约0.62元人民币，原文以新加坡元折算约为0.1新元，约0.54元人民币）。降幅虽小，却是方向性的——在通胀与出口放缓的双重压力下，河内选择让电力这一基础成本「先降一步」。

河内理工大学能源技术研究院的阮春光博士指出，此举契合当前经济走势，在经济、社会保障与能源政策管理上「都具有重要意义」。对企业而言，电费下降可直接节省投入、提升利润，省下的钱还能投入能源审计与产线改造；对家庭而言，则减轻了高耗电家庭的基本开销。

## 为什么是现在：被电力掐住的增长

问题来了：电价为什么偏偏在此时下调？答案藏在越南电力部门的张力里。

过去两年，越南的电力供需持续紧绷。2023年北部地区曾爆发严重电荒，工厂被迫轮流停电，外资电子与纺织企业叫苦不迭；干旱反复冲击水电出力，而水电历来是越南的支柱电源之一。为填补缺口，越南一方面大举进口煤炭（推高发电成本），另一方面从中国与老挝购入电力——后者暴露出其能源自主的软肋。与此同时，外资涌入带动的制造业与数据中心用电激增，电网负荷屡创新高。

EVN本身已长期亏损。作为价格受政府管制的国有买家与卖家，它既要以较高成本进口煤与电，又不能以市价转嫁用户。每一次「为民生让利」的降价，都是在它本就单薄的资产负债表上再削一刀。

## 制造的悖论：廉价电还能撑多久

这便引出更深的一层矛盾。越南被视作供应链东迁的最大受益者之一，服装、电子、电动汽车与光伏产能纷纷落地。但这些产能的运转，高度依赖稳定且相对廉价的电力。一旦电价随成本真实上行，越南相对中国的成本优势就会被稀释。

2%的降价，短期是给企业与家庭减负的政治姿态，长期却可能加剧EVN的财务窟窿。谁来填？要么是财政补贴，要么是日后更大幅度、更不可控的涨价——把今天的让利，变成明天的账单。

从区域视角看，越南从云南购入电力的现实，也提醒人们：所谓「能源自主」，在跨境互联的电网里从来都是相对的。电价下调的背面，是一张越来越依赖外部能源输入的网。

## 一笔先甜后苦的账

说到底，这次2%的下调，更像是一次政治与经济之间的平衡术，而非真正的电价改革。越南电价体制的核心病灶——EVN垄断、价格受政治摆布、发电端成本刚性——一个都没动。

真正的破局，需要的是电网投资、电源多元化与定价机制改革（tariff reform），而非在亏损的国企身上反复「割肉」。阮文胜把时限划到2027年3月31日，潜台词很清楚：这是阶段性让利，不是永久降价。届时期满，越南或将面临一道更难的选择题——是让EVN继续失血，还是把成本如实交还给市场与消费者。

对观察者而言，越南这2%的降价，恰是一扇窗：它让我们看见，在「世界工厂」光鲜的出口数据背后，那张脆弱、昂贵且高度外部化的能源之网，正如何悄悄定义这个国家的增长天花板。

## 延伸阅读

**越南电力集团（EVN）**：越南国有电力垄断企业，统管全国发电、输电与售电，零售电价需政府审批；长期受煤价与进口电成本挤压，财务盈亏高度依赖行政定价。

**零售电价（retail electricity tariff）**：终端用户（居民与工商业）支付的电价，与批发或上网电价相对；在越南由EVN依成本法报请政府核定，并非市场浮动。

**能源自主（energy sovereignty）**：一国对能源供应来源与价格的掌控能力；越南从中国与老挝购电、依赖煤炭进口，显示其区域互联下的相对脆弱性。

# Vietnam Cuts Retail Electricity Tariff by 2%: The Hidden Energy Bill Behind the Manufacturing Miracle

On 31 October the Vietnamese government announced a 2% cut to the national average retail electricity tariff, effective 1 November. According to Reuters and Vietnamese media, the state monopoly Vietnam Electricity Group (EVN) formally announced the adjustment on 30 October: the average retail tariff excluding value-added tax falls to 2,159.9 dong (about US$0.086) per kilowatt-hour, exactly 2% below the prevailing average. At the National Assembly, Deputy Prime Minister Nguyen Van Thang, fielding questions on the economy, finance and budget, said the government was instructing EVN to urgently compress costs so as to lower the retail tariff by around 2% from now until 31 March 2027.

On the surface this is a routine 2% price notice. But placed against Vietnam's recent "Factory of the World 2.0" narrative, every loosening of the power price reveals the most overlooked line item in the country's growth model.

## The numbers: 2% and 2,159.9 dong

Let us be clear about the facts. Vietnam's retail tariff is set by the state-owned monopoly EVN and approved by the government; it does not float freely on the market. Before the cut, the average retail tariff stood at roughly 2,204 dong (about US$0.088) per kWh; after the 2% reduction it becomes 2,159.9 dong (about 0.1 Singapore dollars, roughly US$0.075). Small as the reduction is, it is directional — under the twin pressures of inflation and slowing exports, Hanoi has chosen to let this foundational cost "step down first."

Dr Nguyen Xuan Quang of the Institute of Energy Technology at Hanoi University of Science and Technology argued that the move fits the current economic situation and is "significant" for the economy, social protection and energy-policy management. For firms, cheaper power directly trims input costs and lifts margins, and the savings can be reinvested in energy audits and production-line upgrades; for households, it eases the basic expenses of heavy users.

## Why now: growth throttled by power

Why cut the tariff precisely now? The answer lies in the strain on Vietnam's power sector.

For the past two years Vietnam's electricity supply and demand have been persistently tight. In 2023 the northern region suffered a severe power crunch that forced factories into rotational blackouts, drawing loud complaints from foreign-invested electronics and textile firms; droughts repeatedly hit hydropower output, long one of Vietnam's backbone sources. To fill the gap, Vietnam has ramped up coal imports — pushing up generation costs — and purchases power from China and Laos, exposing a soft spot in its energy autonomy. Meanwhile, surging electricity demand from incoming foreign investment, manufacturing and data centres has pushed grid loads to record highs.

EVN itself has run losses for years. As a state buyer and seller whose prices are administratively regulated, it must pay higher costs for imported coal and electricity yet cannot pass them on to users at market rates. Every "relief for the people" price cut shaves another slice off an already thin balance sheet.

## The manufacturing paradox: how long can cheap power last

This exposes a deeper contradiction. Vietnam is seen as among the biggest beneficiaries of the supply-chain shift east, with apparel, electronics, electric-vehicle and solar-capacity all landing on its soil. But the operation of that capacity depends heavily on stable, relatively cheap power. Once tariffs rise to reflect true costs, Vietnam's cost advantage over China is diluted.

The 2% cut is, in the short run, a political gesture to lighten the burden on firms and households; in the long run it may deepen EVN's financial hole. Who fills it? Either fiscal subsidies, or a later, larger and less controllable price hike — turning today's relief into tomorrow's bill.

From a regional perspective, Vietnam's purchases of electricity from Yunnan are a reminder that so-called "energy sovereignty" is always relative inside cross-border interconnected grids. Behind the tariff cut lies a network increasingly dependent on external energy inputs.

## A sweet-now, bitter-later ledger

In the end, this 2% cut looks more like a balancing act between politics and economics than a genuine tariff reform. None of the core ailments of Vietnam's pricing system — EVN's monopoly, prices bent by politics, rigid generation-side costs — has been touched.

The real way out requires grid investment, diversified generation and tariff reform, not repeated "bleeding" of a loss-making state enterprise. Nguyen Van Thang drew the deadline at 31 March 2027; the subtext is clear — this is a phased relief, not a permanent cut. When it expires, Vietnam may face a harder choice: let EVN keep bleeding, or hand the cost back to the market and the consumer.

For observers, Vietnam's 2% cut is a window. It reveals that behind the glossy export figures of a "factory of the world," a fragile, expensive and highly externalised energy web is quietly defining the country's growth ceiling.

## Further reading

**Vietnam Electricity Group (EVN)**: Vietnam's state-owned power monopoly that manages generation, transmission and distribution nationwide; retail tariffs require government approval and its finances are squeezed by coal and imported-power costs under administratively set prices.

**Retail electricity tariff**: the price paid by end users (households and industry), as distinct from wholesale or feed-in tariffs; in Vietnam it is filed by EVN on a cost basis for government approval rather than floating on the market.

**Energy sovereignty**: a state's control over the sources and prices of its energy supply; Vietnam's purchases of power from China and Laos and its reliance on coal imports reveal its relative vulnerability under regional interconnection.
