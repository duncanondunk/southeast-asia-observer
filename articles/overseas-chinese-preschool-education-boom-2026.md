# 海外学前华文教育升温：侨二代、侨三代的「母语保卫战」

近日，中国新闻网一则报道点出了一个长期被忽视的现象：随着新一代华裔儿童在海外出生、成长，海外学前阶段的华文教育需求正在明显上升。意大利、日本等地的华校已经率先感受到这股压力，而业界围绕低龄儿童的适龄教学探索，也正在从「能不能教」转向「怎么教得好」。这看似是海外华校的一桩日常，背后却是全球华侨华人社区一场静默的代际语言传承之战。

## 需求从何而来：侨二代、侨三代的出生地之变

报道援引意大利威尼斯屿你中文学校校长王智超的观察：老一辈侨胞多将子女送回中国家乡接受教育；而新一代华侨华人的「侨二代」「侨三代」自小在当地出生长大，日常语言环境早已是所在国母语，中文反成了需要专门补课的「外语」。需求上升，本质上不是赶时髦，而是华侨华人家庭对代际语言断层的一种焦虑。说得直白些，当祖辈还在用方言维系乡音，孙辈却要在幼儿园里重新学「你好」，这根链条一旦在某个节点断开，后面再接就难了。

## 样本：从个位数到三个班

这种压力已经压到了具体园所的课表上。王智超介绍，该校学前阶段学生早前仅有个位数，如今已针对四岁至六岁儿童新开设三个班级。日本花漾汉语学校校长小林美华也指出，完善学前华文教育体系，需要从教材、课程设计、师资培训与稳定办学条件多方入手。两个分处欧洲与东亚的样本说明同一件事：需求不是纸上谈兵，它已经挤进了真实课堂的排课表。

## 解法探索：从「小学化」到游戏化浸润

教学上，报道提到，屿你中文学校以汉语拼音为切入点，结合绘画、手工、角色扮演等游戏形式开展教学。温州大学华侨学院华裔儿童教育研究中心主任陈娴则指出，早期海外学前华文教育存在明显的「小学化」倾向，缺少针对语言敏感期幼儿的教学设计；其培训课程转而借鉴中国学前教育经验，设置绘本表演、游戏互动、节庆活动、操节编排、营养健康等内容。一句话总结：把「灌输」换成「浸润」，抓住三到六岁的语言敏感期，比硬赶进度更重要。小林美华说得更直接——培养中文兴趣和熟悉感，比追赶教学进度更有价值，因为语言不只是交流工具，更连着家庭关系、文化认同与一个人的成长路径。

## 师资短板：云端千人，仍嫌不够

需求起来了，老师却跟不上。陈娴介绍，温州大学自二〇二一年首次开办学前华教师资培训以来，线上参训教师逐年增多，二〇二六年度线上培训的云端学员已超千人。千人在线，既说明缺口之大，也说明单靠少数高校的培训供给，远未填满全球华校的师资窟窿。线上培训能解燃眉之急，却难替代面对面的幼教现场——低龄儿童要的不是网课，而是一个会蹲下来陪他念儿歌的老师。

## 家庭场景：把中文从课堂延伸到餐桌

课堂之外，家庭才是语言真正存活的土壤。王智超认为，在华校学前教育体系尚待完善之际，营造家庭中文语言环境，能培养孩子生活中使用中文的习惯。小林美华则发现，不少家长重视华文教育，却苦于不知如何日常引导、陪伴孩子学中文。未来需要给家长一套简单易行的陪伴方法与资源，让中文从教室延伸到生活。说白了，学校再好，回家不说中文，一切归零。

## 东南亚的镜鉴：最大华校网络也有「学前缺口」

如果说意大利、日本是这股需求的「先遣信号」，那么东南亚才是这场母语保卫战的「主战场」。这里聚居着全球规模最大、制度最成熟的华侨华人族群，也拥有马来西亚华文小学、印尼解禁后复办的华校、泰国华人补习体系等百年网络。但一个被反复验证的短板是：这些体系多数覆盖中小学，学前华文教育同样薄弱。更关键的是，东南亚华人基数庞大、母语保持意愿强烈，一旦侨三代在学前阶段就丢掉中文，后面再补，成本高出数倍。马来西亚的华文小学体系、印尼一九九〇年代解除华文禁令后的「补课式」复兴、泰国华人家庭的母语焦虑，都指向同一结论——低龄化、家庭化、本地合规化，是东南亚华文教育下一步无法回避的方向。

## 延伸阅读

- **侨二代（Second-generation diaspora）**：指出生在移居国、父母为移民的华裔子女；其子女即「侨三代」，母语传承压力随代际递增。
- **语言敏感期（Language sensitivity period）**：心理学概念，多指三至六岁为自然语言习得窗口，错过则需更高学习成本。
- **华文教育（Chinese-medium education）**：以华语为教学语言的学校教育体系，在东南亚多表现为华文小学与补习学校，区别于孔子学院的语言推广。

# Overseas Chinese pre-school education heats up: a "mother-tongue defence" for the second and third generations

Recent reporting from China News Service, carried on 22 October 2026, points to a quietly growing phenomenon: as a new generation of ethnic Chinese children is born and raised abroad, demand for pre-school Chinese-language education overseas is rising sharply. Schools in Italy, Japan and beyond are feeling the pressure first, and educators are shifting their focus from "whether we can teach" to "how we teach well." What looks like a routine item on overseas Chinese schools' agendas is, in truth, a silent, generational battle over mother-tongue transmission playing out across the global diaspora.

## Where the demand comes from: a shift in where the second and third generations are born

The report cites Wang Zhichao, principal of the Yuni Chinese School in Venice, Italy. In the past, he notes, many first-generation migrants sent their children back to their ancestral hometowns in China for schooling. Today, the "second generation" (children of migrants) and "third generation" (their grandchildren) are born and raised entirely in the host country, where the everyday language is no longer Chinese. Chinese becomes, paradoxically, a language that must be relearned through deliberate study. The rise in demand, then, is less about fashion than about diaspora families' anxiety over an intergenerational language rupture. To put it plainly: when grandparents still keep a dialect alive, their grandchildren may have to relearn "nihao" in kindergarten — and once that chain breaks at any link, reconnecting it later is far harder.

## The sample: from single digits to three new classes

That pressure has already reached the timetables of concrete classrooms. Wang says his school's pre-school enrolment used to be in single digits; it has now opened three new classes for children aged four to six. Kobayashi Miwa, principal of the Huayang Chinese School in Japan, argues that building a sound pre-school Chinese system requires action on textbooks, curriculum design, teacher training and stable operating conditions. Two cases in two very different countries tell the same story: the demand is not abstract. It is already crowding the scheduling of real schools.

## New approaches: from "primary-schooling" to play-based immersion

On method, the report notes that Yuni uses Hanyu Pinyin as an entry point, weaving it into drawing, handicrafts and role-play. Chen Xian, director of the Centre for Ethnic-Chinese Children's Education at Wenzhou University's Overseas Chinese College, observes that early overseas pre-school Chinese education tended toward a "primary-schooling" model — premature, rote, and blind to the language-sensitive window of early childhood. Her training courses instead borrow from China's own pre-school experience, building in picture-book performance, game interaction, festival activities, rhythm exercises and nutrition. In one phrase: swap cramming for immersion, and seize the three-to-six-year window rather than racing through a syllabus. Kobayashi puts it more bluntly — fostering interest and familiarity matters more than keeping pace, because language is not merely a tool but a thread tying together family bonds, cultural identity and a child's life path.

## The teacher gap: a thousand online, still not enough

Demand surges, but teachers lag. Chen notes that since Wenzhou University launched its first pre-school Chinese teacher training in 2021, online participation has grown year by year; the 2026 online cohort already exceeds one thousand. A four-digit online enrolment signals both the scale of the gap and the limits of supply: a handful of universities cannot fill the teacher vacuum across global Chinese schools. Online training eases the immediate crunch but cannot replace the face-to-face realities of early-childhood classrooms — what young children need is not a webinar but a teacher who will crouch down and recite a nursery rhyme with them.

## The home front: carrying Chinese from the classroom to the dinner table

Beyond the classroom, the home is where a language actually lives. Wang argues that while formal pre-school systems are still maturing, cultivating a Chinese-speaking home environment trains children to use the language in daily life. Kobayashi finds that many parents value Chinese education yet struggle with how to guide and accompany their children day to day. What is needed, she says, is a simple, usable set of parenting methods and resources that extend Chinese from the schoolroom into life. To put it plainly: the best school is worthless if no one speaks Chinese at home.

## Lessons from Southeast Asia: even the largest Chinese-school network has a pre-school gap

If Italy and Japan are the early-warning signals, Southeast Asia is the main battlefield of this mother-tongue defence. It is home to the world's largest and most institutionalised ethnic-Chinese populations, and to century-old networks — Malaysia's Chinese primary schools, Indonesia's revived schools after the post-1990s lifting of the Chinese-language ban, Thailand's supplementary Chinese tutoring system. Yet a repeatedly confirmed weakness is that these systems mostly cover primary and secondary levels; pre-school Chinese is just as thin. More decisively, Southeast Asia's huge ethnic-Chinese base and strong desire to preserve the mother tongue mean that once the third generation loses Chinese at the pre-school stage, the cost of recovery later multiplies severalfold. Malaysia's Chinese-primary network, Indonesia's "catch-up" revival after the 1990s ban, and Thai families' mother-tongue anxiety all point to the same conclusion: going younger, going home-based, and going locally compliant are the directions Southeast Asian Chinese education cannot avoid next.

## Further reading

- **Second-generation diaspora**: Ethnic-Chinese children born in the host country to migrant parents; their own children form the "third generation," with mother-tongue transmission pressure rising with each generation.
- **Language sensitivity period**: A psychological concept, often referring to the natural language-acquisition window of roughly ages three to six; missing it raises the cost of later learning.
- **Chinese-medium education**: Schooling conducted in Mandarin as the language of instruction. In Southeast Asia it appears mainly as Chinese primary schools and supplementary schools, distinct from the Confucius Institute's language-promotion model.
