# 泰国拟放宽初创企业融资：松绑能否补上创新短板？

泰国内阁通过《创业企业促进法案》草案，放宽股权与募资限制，但真正的考验在于资本、人才与区域竞赛。

## 为何此时"松绑"：泰国创新经济的短板

泰国常以东南亚天然经济枢纽自居——制造业基地、旅游大国、安稳的中等收入社会。然而在衡量现代经济活力的关键指标，即本土初创企业的数量与质量上，泰国已悄然落后于邻国。2026年9月8日，泰国内阁通过《创业企业促进法案》草案，意在通过放宽长期束缚年轻企业融资与股权管理的规则，修补这一短板。

这一动作并非骤然转向，而是对结构性弱点的迟来回应。泰国的增长模式长期依赖跨国工厂、旅游收入与家族财团，带来了稳定，却也造成初创生态的浅薄：不愿向无抵押企业放贷的保守银行、偏向大型在位企业的证券交易所，以及宁愿安稳领薪也不愿冒险创业的文化偏好。正如历任政府所承认，其结果是一个创新不足、高度依赖外国技术的经济体。

## 草案改了什么：五年优惠与资本运作空间

草案若获通过，将一次性松绑多项约束。符合条件的初创企业可获得为期五年的权利与优惠，特定战略领域企业最长可延至十年。申请门槛为：成立不超过十年的有限公司，过去三年平均年收入不超过3亿泰铢（约6000万元人民币），且此前未进行利润分配。

更具实质意义的改动在于资本募集与股权结构。草案拟允许初创企业向公众发行股票、发行公司债券、将债权转为股权，并将优先股转换为普通股——这些举措在纸面上打开了此前繁琐或关闭的多个募资通道。它还允许以股份归属机制吸引和留住人才，并允许企业持有自身股份，用于员工持股计划及吸引投资者。简言之，这部法案试图把新加坡或雅加达同业早已习以为常的公司金融工具箱，交到泰国创始人手中。

## 国家创新局：从"九龙治水"到"一站式"

管理权收归泰国国家创新局，由其作为牵头协调方提供"一站式"服务。该机构负责认证初创企业、对接融资与政策支持，并打通知识产权、税收、外籍人才与政府采购之间的接口。这一设计吸取了过往失败的清晰教训：泰国的问题很少是缺政策，而是政策碎片化，各部会各吹各的号，小企业在缝隙中迷失。一个统一的前台若能运转，将降低那种悄悄扼杀早期企业的合规成本。

## 区域坐标：泰国为何跑输新加坡与越南

将紧迫感置于区域坐标系中最易读懂。新加坡仍是东盟无可争议的创业之都，拥有深厚的风投资金、宽容的监管沙盒，以及吸引全球资本的门户地位。印尼凭借庞大的国内市场，在金融科技、电商与物流领域批量孵化独角兽。越南常被视作低成本工厂，近年来却凭借年轻劳动力与供应链从中国外溢的多元化，催生出快速崛起的软件与人工智能初创群体。

泰国尴尬地居于其间——比越南富裕，某些方面比印尼多元，却在初创企业数量与风投流入上持续落后。政府自身关于"双速经济"的叙事，恰如其分地捕捉到这种焦虑：旅游与汽车装配强劲，决定未来税基的高价值、知识密集型板块却迟滞不前。这部法案，等于承认单靠志愿式产业政策无法补齐差距。

## 中国维度：中泰数字经济的资本接口

对本书读者而言，中国线索尤为关键。泰国初创企业身处一个中国资本、平台与供应链早已深度嵌入的经济体——从在东部沿海设厂的电动车企，到觊觎东盟第二大消费市场的金融科技与电商巨头。更具包容性的募资制度，恰恰降低了此类跨境投资所面临的摩擦，而中资企业正具备供给能力。中国风投与战略投资者获得更干净的法律载体去投资泰国创新者；泰国创始人则得以对接中国的技术、制造与庞大的邻近市场。

这并非没有张力。泰国多年来在华盛顿与北京之间保持平衡，而一场部分由中国资本驱动的创业热潮，将考验这种平衡术——尤其是在数据、半导体与两用技术等敏感领域。草案对"战略领域"给予延长优惠的设定，暗示了国家期待回报所在，也暗示了审查将集中的方向。

## 结语：松绑只是第一步

《创业企业促进法案》是一项明智却迟来的"清淤"工程。它移除了真实障碍，也释放出一个信号：曼谷明白创新短板是政策问题，而非天气问题。但法律不会凭空造出独角兽。泰国仍需要耐心的本土资本、更深的技术人才储备，以及一种将失败视为学习成本而非污点的文化转向。区域领跑者并未原地踏步：新加坡巩固优势，越南攀升，印尼扩张。曼谷已经松开了束缚——泰国创始人能否借自由起跑，将是未来五年要讲的故事。

## 延伸阅读

**初创企业（Startup）**：指成立时间短、以创新模式或技术快速成长、通常依赖风险资本而非传统信贷的中小企业。

**国家创新局（National Innovation Agency, NIA）**：泰国政府的科技与创新主管机构，负责初创认证、政策协调与"一站式"企业服务。

**双速经济（Two-speed economy）**：形容旅游与传统制造业强劲、高附加值创新产业相对滞后的结构性分化，泰国决策层用以自剖国情。

# Thailand Moves to Untie Startup Financing — But Can Deregulation Close the Innovation Gap?

Bangkok's new Startup Enterprise Promotion Act relaxes equity and fundraising rules, yet the real test lies in capital, talent, and regional competition.

## Why now: Thailand's innovation gap

Thailand likes to present itself as Southeast Asia's natural economic hub — a manufacturing base, a tourism giant, and a comfortable middle-income society. Yet on the measure that increasingly defines a modern economy, the number and vitality of home-grown startups, the country has quietly fallen behind its neighbours. On 8 September 2026 the cabinet approved the draft Startup Enterprise Promotion Act, a long-telegraphed attempt to fix that deficit by loosening the rules that have long constrained how young companies raise money and manage equity.

The move is less a sudden pivot than a belated response to a structural weakness. Thailand's growth model has rested on multinational factories, tourism receipts, and family-controlled conglomerates. That model delivered stability but produced a shallow startup ecosystem: risk-averse banks that will not lend to firms without collateral, a stock exchange tilted toward large incumbents, and a cultural preference for secure salaried careers over entrepreneurial risk. The result, as successive governments have acknowledged, is an economy that innovates less than its potential and depends heavily on foreign technology.

## What the bill changes: five-year incentives and capital freedom

The draft law, if enacted, rewires several constraints at once. Qualifying startups receive a package of rights and privileges for five years, extendable to ten for firms in designated strategic sectors. To be eligible, a company must be a limited company incorporated no more than ten years earlier, with average annual revenue over the past three years not exceeding 300 million baht (about US$8 million), and with no prior profit distribution.

The more consequential changes sit in how these firms can raise and structure capital. The bill would let startups issue shares to the public, sell corporate bonds, convert debt into equity, and transform preferred shares into common shares — moves that, on paper, open multiple fundraising channels that were cumbersome or closed before. It also permits share-vesting arrangements to attract and retain talent, and allows companies to hold their own stock for employee share-ownership plans and to court investors. In short, the Act tries to give founders the corporate-finance toolkit that their peers in Singapore or Jakarta take for granted.

## The National Innovation Agency: from fragmented to one-stop

Administration is consolidated under the National Innovation Agency (NIA), which becomes the lead coordinator offering a "one-stop" service. The agency is tasked with certifying startups, linking them to financing and policy support, and smoothing the interfaces with intellectual property, taxation, foreign talent, and government procurement. The design signals a clear lesson from past failures: Thailand's problem was rarely the absence of policy but the fragmentation of it, with ministries pulling in different directions and small firms lost in the gaps. A single front door, if it works, could lower the compliance tax that quietly kills early-stage ventures.

## Regional context: why Thailand trails Singapore and Vietnam

The urgency is best understood against the regional scoreboard. Singapore remains the undisputed startup capital of ASEAN, with deep venture funds, a permissive regulatory sandbox, and a gateway status that draws global capital. Indonesia leverages its vast domestic market to mint unicorns in fintech, e-commerce, and logistics. Vietnam, often dismissed as a low-cost workshop, has in recent years produced a fast-rising cohort of software and AI startups, buoyed by a young workforce and supply-chain diversification away from China.

Thailand sits awkwardly in between — wealthier than Vietnam, more diversified than Indonesia in some respects, yet consistently outpaced in startup creation and venture inflows. The government's own narratives about a "two-speed economy" capture the anxiety: strong in tourism and automotive assembly, sluggish in the high-value, knowledge-intensive segments where the future tax base will be decided. The Act is an admission that voluntarist industrial policy alone has not closed the gap.

## The China dimension: a capital interface for digital ties

For this blog's readers, the China thread matters. Thai startups operate in an economy where Chinese capital, platforms, and supply chains are already deeply embedded — from EV makers setting up on the eastern seaboard to fintech and e-commerce giants eyeing ASEAN's second-largest consumer market. A more permissive fundraising regime lowers friction for precisely the kind of cross-border investment that Beijing's firms are positioned to supply. Chinese venture and strategic investors gain a cleaner legal vehicle to back Thai innovators; Thai founders gain access to Chinese know-how, manufacturing, and a vast adjacent market.

This is not without tension. Thailand has spent years balancing between Washington and Beijing, and a startup boom partly financed from China will test that equilibrism — especially in sensitive sectors such as data, semiconductors, and dual-use technology. The Act's designation of "strategic sectors" with extended incentives hints at where the state expects the payoff, and where scrutiny will concentrate.

## Conclusion: deregulation is only the first step

The Startup Enterprise Promotion Act is a sensible, if overdue, piece of housekeeping. It removes genuine obstacles and signals that Bangkok understands the innovation deficit is a policy problem, not a meteorological one. But laws do not mint unicorns. Thailand still needs patient domestic capital, a deeper pool of technical talent, and a cultural shift that rewards failure as a learning cost rather than a stain. The region's leaders are not standing still; Singapore tightens its lead, Vietnam climbs, Indonesia scales. Bangkok has loosened the bindings — whether Thai founders run with the freedom is the story the next five years will tell.

## Further reading

**Startup**: a young company built on an innovative model or technology and expected to grow fast, typically financed by venture capital rather than traditional bank loans.

**National Innovation Agency (NIA)**: Thailand's state agency for science, technology and innovation, responsible for startup certification, policy coordination and one-stop enterprise services.

**Two-speed economy**: a structural split in which tourism and traditional manufacturing stay strong while high-value, innovation-led sectors lag — a framing Thai policymakers use for their own economy.
