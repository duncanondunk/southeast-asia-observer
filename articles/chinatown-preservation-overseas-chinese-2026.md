# 从老街区到新地标：海外侨胞探寻唐人街的保护与更新

据中国侨网10月15日报道，中国新闻社记者吴侃探访了海外侨胞正在推进的唐人街保护更新。马德里乌塞拉区唐人街牌坊本月初揭幕，米兰华人街从批发商街变身为步行文化街区，马来西亚马六甲鸡场街则在旅游年中推出闽南文化周与「老街守护者」盖章打卡。三组案例串起一条清晰线索：唐人街早已不只是侨胞落脚谋生的旧街巷，而正在成为离散族群的身份地标、城市更新的试验场，也是中华文化「软实力」在海外最可见的载体之一。

## 从落脚点到城市名片

唐人街的诞生，几乎都与「下南洋」「闯欧美」的移民潮绑定。第一代移民在异国海岸落脚，先有餐馆、杂货铺、会馆，再慢慢聚成街。颜天禄把鸡场街的名字追溯到闽南语「街场街」，意为「繁忙的街道」——这正是华侨下南洋的重要落脚点。今天，这些老街区面临同一个命题：当老一辈渐次老去、城市更新不断推进，唐人街要如何既不丢掉记忆，又接得住新的人流与消费？

我的看法很直接：唐人街的价值不在「老」，而在「活」。一条只供怀旧的街道终会被城市甩在身后；一条能把乡情、商业与文化体验拧在一起的老街，反而能在全球化城市里站稳脚跟。马德里的牌坊、米兰的步行街、马六甲的盖章打卡，走的都是「活态传承」这条路。

## 马六甲鸡场街：闽南语里的「繁忙街道」

马六甲是马来西亚旅游年（2026）的重点。鸡场街工委会今年推出闽南文化周，用美术展、文化讲堂展示「送王船」等民俗；「老街守护者」活动则让游客手持「文化护照」，按地图寻访点位、盖章打卡。值得玩味的是，马来西亚的唐人街叙事始终绕着闽南语转——鸡场街、槟城姓周桥、吉隆坡茨厂街，背后都是福建裔为主的移民脉络。

这给观察者提了个醒：东南亚唐人街的「中国性」从来不是铁板一块，而是闽南、客家、广府、潮汕等方言群各自成网。理解唐人街，先要理解方言群。马来西亚把鸡场街放进旅游年，本质上是在用一条老街讲一个多元族群共存的故事——这比单纯「怀旧」更有竞争力。

## 欧洲样本：牌坊与步行街的焕新

报道里的两个欧洲案例同样有参考价值。马德里乌塞拉区牌坊把中国龙与马德里市徽的熊形象结合，陈恩官称之为「和谐共融」；米兰华人街早年「热闹拥堵」，华商推着小推车卖货，如今已是集餐饮、消费、文化体验于一体的步行街。中意商联成立十余年，推动商家委员会、优化垃圾与安保，还办春节舞龙舞狮联欢。

欧美的唐人街与东南亚有个关键差别：欧洲多是二十世纪末才成形的新移民商业带，东南亚则是数代沉淀的「老侨」核心区。但两者的困境相通——人流来了，治安、环境、业态升级的压力也来了。林杰说新生代为华人街带来品牌塑造与社媒传播，这恰恰点出了更新的真正抓手：不是政府单边规划，而是侨社自己长出运营能力。

## 新生代接棒：从批发档到品牌店

无论是米兰的中意商联，还是马六甲的工委会，更新背后的推手都是「华裔新生代」。他们更在意门店设计、消费体验与社交媒体传播，新餐饮、新零售不断涌现。陈恩官提到未来要推动店铺「精品化、特色化」，增设双语便民服务——这已经不是在守摊，而是在经营一个面向全城的地标。

这里有个容易被忽略的点：新生代接棒，意味着唐人街的主导语言与叙事权正在转移。当「龙」与「熊」能被放进同一座牌坊，当闽南语老街用「文化护照」对接国际游客，唐人街就不再只是某族群的「内部空间」，而成了城市公共生活的一部分。这是离散族群融入的成熟形态。

## 保护与更新的两难

当然，更新不是没有代价。牌坊立起来了、步行街热闹了，租金往往跟着涨，老租户可能被挤出；过度商业化又会把文化变成布景。马德里要「精品化」，米兰要「优化垃圾与安保」，马六甲要「保留老街风貌」——三地的表述都透露出同一种审慎：更新要快，记忆要慢。

我倾向于这样一个判断：唐人街保护的最高境界，是让原住民侨胞仍然是主角，而不是被文旅资本请下舞台。衡量一条唐人街成不成功，不该只看游客量与打卡数，更要看老街坊还在不在、会馆还开不开、方言还响不响。活的地标，首先得是活的人。

## 延伸阅读

**离散族群（Diaspora）**：因移民散居海外、仍与祖籍地保持文化经济联系的族群；唐人街是离散族群最集中的空间载体。

**送王船（Wangkang）**：闽南海洋民俗，送「王船」出海以求平安，2020年列入联合国教科文组织人类非物质文化遗产名录，常见于马六甲等东南亚闽南社群。

**马六甲（Malacca）**：马来西亚历史名城，扼马六甲海峡，鸡场街为世遗核心区，是华侨下南洋的早期重要落脚点之一。

# From Old Blocks to New Landmarks: Overseas Chinese Reimagine Chinatown Preservation

On 15 October, China Qiao Wang (China News Service) reported from Beijing on how overseas Chinese communities are pushing ahead with the preservation and renewal of their Chinatowns. A ceremonial archway unveiled this month in Madrid's Usera district has become a neighbourhood landmark; Milan's Chinese street has been reborn from a wholesale strip into a pedestrian cultural boulevard; and in Malacca, Malaysia, Jonker Street has rolled out a Hokkien culture week and a "street guardian" stamp-trail for visitors during the Malaysia Tourism Year. Three cases, one clear thread: the Chinatown is no longer merely the old alley where migrants once scraped a living. It is becoming a landmark of diaspora identity, a testing ground for urban renewal, and one of the most visible carriers of Chinese culture's soft power abroad.

## From landing pads to city landmarks

Chinatowns were born of migration — the voyages "to Nanyang" and across the Atlantic and Pacific. The first generation landed on foreign shores and built, first, restaurants, grocery shops and clan associations, then whole streets. Ganh Tian Loo, who chairs the Jonker Street working committee in Malacca, traces the name back to the Hokkien "keciang-ke" — a busy street — a reminder that this was a key landing point for Chinese migrants heading south. Today these old districts face the same question: as the pioneer generation ages and urban renewal presses in, how does a Chinatown keep its memory without losing the new flows of people and commerce?

My own view is blunt: a Chinatown's value lies not in being old, but in being alive. A street kept only for nostalgia will eventually be left behind by the city; an old street that weaves together hometown sentiment, commerce and cultural experience can hold its ground in a globalised city. Madrid's archway, Milan's pedestrian street and Malacca's stamp-trail are all walking the path of "living heritage."

## Malacca's Jonker Street: a "busy street" in Hokkien

Malacca is a centrepiece of Malaysia Tourism Year 2026. This year the Jonker Street committee launched a Hokkien Culture Week, using art exhibitions and public talks to showcase folk rituals such as "Wangkang" (sending the royal boat to sea); the "street guardian" activity hands visitors a "cultural passport" to follow a map, find the spots and collect stamps. What is telling is that Malaysia's Chinatown narrative keeps circling back to the Hokkien language — Jonker Street, Penang's Chew Jetty and Kuala Lumpur's Petaling Street all rest on migrant lineages that are predominantly Fujianese.

This is a reminder for any observer: the "Chineseness" of Southeast Asian Chinatowns was never a monolith. It is a web of separate dialect groups — Hokkien, Hakka, Cantonese, Teochew — each with its own networks. To understand a Chinatown, start with its dialect groups. By placing Jonker Street inside the tourism year, Malaysia is essentially telling a story of multi-ethnic coexistence through a single old street — a story far more competitive than plain nostalgia.

## European samples: arches and pedestrianised streets reborn

The two European cases in the report are instructive too. In Madrid's Usera district, the archway blends the Chinese dragon with the bear from Madrid's city coat of arms, which Chen Enguan calls "harmonious coexistence"; Milan's Chinese street, once "noisy and congested" with traders pushing carts of goods, is now a pedestrian street combining dining, retail and cultural experience. The China-Italy Business Alliance, active for over a decade, has pushed for a merchants' committee, better waste and security management, and a lunar-new-year dragon-lion gala.

There is a key difference between European and Southeast Asian Chinatowns: Europe's are mostly new-immigrant commercial belts formed only from the late twentieth century, while Southeast Asia's are the hardened cores of communities settled across generations. Yet their predicaments converge — more footfall brings more pressure on security, the environment and business upgrading. Lin Jie notes that the new generation brings branding and social-media outreach to the street, and that points precisely to the real lever of renewal: not one-sided government planning, but an operating capacity that the community grows for itself.

## A new generation takes over: from wholesale stalls to branded stores

Whether it is the China-Italy Business Alliance in Milan or the committee in Malacca, the driving force behind renewal is the "new generation of Chinese descent." They care more about store design, the consumer experience and social-media dissemination; new F&B and new-retail formats keep emerging. Chen Enguan mentions plans to make shops "boutique and distinctive" and add bilingual community services — this is no longer tending a stall, but running a city-wide landmark.

One easily missed point: as the new generation takes over, the dominant language and the narrative power of the Chinatown are shifting. When a "dragon" and a "bear" can sit in the same archway, when a Hokkien old street uses a "cultural passport" to meet international visitors, the Chinatown ceases to be merely an ethnic group's "internal space" and becomes part of the city's public life. That is the mature form of diaspora integration.

## The dilemma of preservation and renewal

Of course, renewal is not cost-free. Once the archway rises and the pedestrian street buzzes, rents tend to follow, and old tenants may be squeezed out; over-commercialisation turns culture into a stage set. Madrid wants "boutique" shops, Milan wants better waste and security, Malacca wants to "keep the old street's character" — all three betray the same prudence: renew fast, but remember slowly.

My own verdict: the highest form of Chinatown preservation is to keep the resident overseas Chinese as the protagonists, not to usher them off the stage for tourism capital. A Chinatown should not be measured only by visitor counts and photo-ops, but by whether the old neighbours remain, the clan halls stay open and the dialect still rings out. A living landmark must first be living people.

## Further reading

**Diaspora**: Communities dispersed abroad through migration that still maintain cultural and economic ties with their ancestral homelands; the Chinatown is the densest spatial carrier of diaspora life.

**Wangkang (送王船)**: A Hokkien maritime folk ritual of sending a "royal boat" out to sea for peace, inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2020 and common among Hokkien communities across Southeast Asia such as Malacca.

**Malacca**: A historic Malaysian city commanding the Strait of Malacca; its Jonker Street sits at the heart of a UNESCO World Heritage core zone and was an early, important landing point for Chinese migrants heading south.
