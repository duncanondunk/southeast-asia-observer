# 金边运动会背后的在柬华商"成年礼"

9月12日至19日，柬埔寨中国商会第一届运动节在金边落下帷幕。中国驻柬埔寨大使汪文斌、使馆公使衔参赞薛东，以及商会会长林士强、多位常务副会长悉数到场；约300名来自会员企业的代表、运动员、裁判与工作人员参与了这届为期八天的活动。赛事本身并不算大——36支队伍、230名运动员、72场比赛，含第六届羽毛球赛与首届乒乓球赛。但正是在这场看似平常的运动会里，藏着观察在柬华商群体如何完成"制度化成年"的一扇窗口。

## 一场运动会，半部在柬华商编年史

把时间轴拉开，柬埔寨中国商会早已不是初来乍到的互助小组。它今天要服务的，是数量庞大的在柬中资企业与人员——从基建、能源、金融到制造业、服务业，华商网络几乎覆盖了柬埔寨经济的每一根毛细血管。运动节这类活动，表面是"快乐健康办会"，实质是把分散在各行各业的在柬中国人，重新编织进一个可识别、可动员、有归属感的共同体。

林士强在开幕式上把商会称作在柬中资企业的"共同家园"，这话听着朴素，背后却是一套清晰的治理逻辑：当经营环境需要协调、当员工需要业余生活、当企业之间需要非正式的信任渠道，商会就扮演了那条"看不见的纽带"。本届运动节参赛队伍与运动员人数均创下同类活动新高，说明这条纽带已经织得够密、够结实。

## 从互助会到"共同家园"：商会的制度化

值得追问的是，为什么是"运动节"，而不是别的？答案藏在商会自身的演化轨迹里。早期海外华人商会多以解决生存性问题为主——签证、纠纷、突发事件的互助。而随着在柬中资规模扩张、人员结构从"闯荡者"变成"常住者"，商会的职能必然从"救急"走向"日常治理"：文体活动、交流平台、与使馆的常态化对接，都是这套治理的零件。

汪文斌在致辞中说，使馆支持商会举办更多相关活动，"更好地服务广大在柬中资企业和员工"。这句话的分量在于，它把商会从"企业自娱自乐"提升为"官方认可的侨务服务节点"。换言之，运动场上的哨声，与使馆的致辞，其实是同一套海外利益延伸机制的两种声音。

## 体育作纽带，也是在打"形象牌"

薛东在闭幕致辞里说，运动节"以体育活动为纽带，拉近了在柬中资企业同仁之间的距离"，并"展现健康向上、团结协作的精神风貌"。这类表述当然有套话成分，但不能只当套话听。在柬埔寨这样一个人均收入不高、地缘政治敏感度极高、且中国资本长期被西方叙事贴上"债务陷阱""影响力渗透"标签的国家，华商群体主动把自己呈现为"健康、向上、守法、团结"的形象，本身就是一种必要的声誉对冲。

更直白地说，当同一片土地上还时不时冒出"网诈园区""灰产"等负面联想时，正规商会用一场公开、透明、有使馆站台的运动会，把自己与那些灰色地带划清界限，是最朴素也最管用的公关。体育不解决任何经贸难题，但它能把"我们不是你们想象的那群人"这句话，用最不具攻击性的方式讲给东道国社会听。

## 为什么是柬埔寨：华商网络的特殊密度

把镜头推远，柬埔寨是观察中国海外商业离散网络的一块特殊样本。与新加坡的成熟华人社会、马来西亚的百年族群政治不同，柬埔寨的"中国因素"在近年来急速膨胀——中资是柬埔寨最大的外资来源，西哈努克港经济特区、金边的基础设施、能源与电信，几乎都打着中资的印记。这种密度的另一面，是极高的能见度与极高的被审视度：华商的一举一动，既被东道国百姓看在眼里，也被区域与全球媒体放大解读。

也正因为此，柬埔寨中国商会的"成年礼"就格外有指示意义。当一个海外华商群体已经大到需要自己的羽毛球赛、乒乓球赛，需要使馆大使亲自站台，需要把"共同家园"写进致辞，它就不再只是生意人的松散结社，而是一套平行于外交系统的民间基础设施。北京对此心知肚明：善用商会，比单纯依赖使馆，更能把影响力做进东道国的社会纹理里。

## 风险与边界：光环之下的审视

话要说回来，运动会的欢呼声里也该留一分冷静。商会的"共同家园"叙事，天然带着把异质性抹平的倾向——它倾向于呈现一个团结、成功、合规的华商整体形象，却可能掩盖个体企业在劳务、环保、合规上的参差。东道国社会对"中国资本"的复杂情绪，并不会因为一场运动会就烟消云散；相反，越是高调的共同体展示，越容易激起"你们到底代表谁"的质疑。

更现实的边界在于，体育终究替代不了硬约束。劳工权益、本地化雇佣、税务透明、与当地社区的收益共享，这些才是决定中柬务实合作能否"深入发展"（薛东语）的真正变量。运动节能凝聚人气，却不能替任何一家企业完成合规作业。对在柬华商而言，这场"成年礼"的真正考题，不在球场上，而在日常经营的每一个合规细节里。

# The coming-of-age of Chinese business in Cambodia, on a sports field

From 12 to 19 September, the first Sports Festival of the Chinese Chamber of Commerce in Cambodia closed in Phnom Penh. Wang Wenbin, China's ambassador to Cambodia, Xue Dong, the embassy's minister-counsellor, chamber president Lin Shiqiang and several standing deputy presidents all showed up. Roughly 300 representatives from member companies, athletes, referees and staff took part in the eight-day event. The competition itself was modest in scale — 36 teams, 230 athletes, 72 matches, comprising the sixth badminton tournament and the inaugural table-tennis tournament. Yet behind this seemingly ordinary festival lies a window into how the Chinese business community in Cambodia has completed its "institutional coming-of-age".

## A sports festival, half a chronicle of Chinese business in Cambodia

Stretch the timeline and the Chinese Chamber of Commerce in Cambodia is long past being a fledgling mutual-aid group. What it serves today is a vast population of Chinese-funded enterprises and personnel — spanning infrastructure, energy, finance, manufacturing and services, the Chinese business network runs through nearly every capillary of the Cambodian economy. Events like a sports festival look, on the surface, like a "fun and healthy" gathering; in substance they re-weave dispersed Chinese nationals across industries into an identifiable, mobilisable and belonging-bearing community.

At the opening ceremony Lin Shiqiang called the chamber a "common home" for Chinese-funded enterprises in Cambodia. The phrase sounds plain, yet behind it sits a clear governance logic: when the business environment needs coordination, when staff need leisure, when enterprises need informal channels of trust, the chamber acts as the invisible tie. This festival set new highs in both the number of teams and athletes, evidence that the tie has been woven densely and firmly enough.

## From mutual-aid club to "common home": the institutionalization of the chamber

One might ask why a "sports festival" rather than something else. The answer lies in the chamber's own evolutionary track. Early overseas Chinese chambers were preoccupied with survival problems — visas, disputes, emergency mutual aid. As the scale of Chinese investment in Cambodia expanded and the personnel profile shifted from "pioneers" to "residents", the chamber's function inevitably moved from crisis response to everyday governance: cultural and sports activities, exchange platforms, and routine liaison with the embassy are all cogs in that governance machine.

In his speech Wang Wenbin said the embassy supports the chamber in holding more such events, to "better serve the broad mass of Chinese-funded enterprises and staff in Cambodia". The weight of that line is that it elevates the chamber from "companies entertaining themselves" to an "officially recognised node of diaspora service". In other words, the whistle on the sports field and the ambassador's address are two voices of the same mechanism for projecting overseas interests.

## Sport as a tie — and as image management

At the closing ceremony Xue Dong said the festival used "sport as a tie" to bring colleagues from Chinese-funded enterprises closer, and to display a "healthy, upward-looking and united" spirit. Such phrasing is partly formulaic, but it should not be read as mere boilerplate. In a country like Cambodia — low per-capita income, extremely high geopolitical sensitivity, and where Chinese capital has long been tagged by Western narratives with "debt trap" and "influence infiltration" labels — the Chinese business community's voluntary presentation of itself as "healthy, upward, law-abiding and united" is a necessary reputational hedge.

To put it more bluntly: while the same soil still occasionally throws up negative associations such as "cyber-scam parks" and "grey industries", a properly run chamber uses a public, transparent festival — with the embassy on the podium — to draw a line between itself and those grey zones. It is the plainest and most effective public relations available. Sport solves no trade problem, but it lets the community say "we are not who you imagine" to the host society in the least confrontational way possible.

## Why Cambodia: the unusual density of the Chinese business network

Zoom out and Cambodia is a special specimen for observing China's overseas commercial diaspora. Unlike Singapore's mature ethnic-Chinese society or Malaysia's century-old communal politics, Cambodia's "China factor" has ballooned in recent years — Chinese capital is Cambodia's largest source of foreign investment, and the Sihanoukville Special Economic Zone, Phnom Penh's infrastructure, energy and telecoms all bear the mark of Chinese funding. The flip side of that density is extraordinarily high visibility and scrutiny: every move by Chinese business is watched by the host population and magnified by regional and global media.

For precisely this reason, the chamber's "coming-of-age" is especially telling. When an overseas Chinese business community has grown large enough to need its own badminton and table-tennis tournaments, important enough for the ambassador himself to appear, and self-confident enough to write "common home" into its speeches, it is no longer a loose association of merchants. It is a civil-infrastructure layer running parallel to the diplomatic system. Beijing knows this well: leveraging chambers works better than relying on embassies alone to weave influence into the social texture of the host country.

## Risks and limits: scrutiny beneath the halo

That said, a note of sobriety belongs in the cheering. The "common home" narrative carries a natural tendency to smooth over heterogeneity — it leans toward presenting a united, successful, compliant image of Chinese business while possibly obscuring the uneven record of individual firms on labour, environment and compliance. The host society's complex feelings toward "Chinese capital" will not dissolve because of one sports festival; on the contrary, the more high-profile the communal display, the more it can provoke the question of whom exactly such a community represents.

A more practical limit is that sport ultimately cannot substitute for hard constraints. Labour rights, local hiring, tax transparency and benefit-sharing with local communities are the real variables deciding whether China–Cambodia practical cooperation can "deepen" (in Xue Dong's words). A festival can rally morale, but it cannot complete the compliance homework of any single enterprise. For Chinese business in Cambodia, the true test of this "coming-of-age" lies not on the court, but in the compliance details of everyday operations.
