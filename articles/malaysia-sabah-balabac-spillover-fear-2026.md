# 沙巴为何让马来西亚对南海紧张"多看一眼"

南海从来都是一个拥挤的舞台。但在它的南缘，情况起了变化。据《南华早报》报道，马来西亚正以异乎寻常的注意力盯着沙巴州外海——并非吉隆坡主动挑起事端，而是担心被别人的冲突拖下水。导火索是一段很短的水道，以及水道对面一座快速扩张的军事基地。

## 隔壁的燃点

巴拉巴克岛是菲律宾最南端的岛屿，距沙巴州的邦吉岛仅约50公里，两者被宽约12公里的巴拉巴克海峡隔开。这条海峡连接苏禄海与南海，是一条关键水道。如今这条平静的海上走廊已成地缘热点。根据菲律宾2014年与华盛顿签署的《加强防务合作协议》（Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement, EDCA），美国获得了菲律宾九个军事基地的使用权，其中两个位于巴拉望，包括巴拉巴克岛上的纳西索·德尔罗萨里奥海军基地，那里正在修建一条长约3公里的军用跑道。截至2026年5月，EDCA基地扩建合同金额约1.25亿美元（约9亿元人民币）。美军不是在"访问"，而是在浇筑混凝土、建机库、铺输油管线。

## 吉隆坡的隐忧

马来西亚并未保持沉默。国防部长穆罕默德·哈立德·诺丁10月3日公开表示，如果在那个地理上狭小的区域发生什么，也可能波及本国。总理安瓦尔·易卜拉欣早在8月便表达关切，称作为受沙巴主权主张直接影响的邻国，马来西亚对日益增强的、美国支持的菲律宾军事存在感到不安——尽管与马尼拉的双边关系依然良好。安瓦尔直言，马来西亚无法匹敌巴拉巴克正在建设的设施规模，因此必须加强自身的基本防御能力。

## 沉睡的主张，醒着的焦虑

这正是让巴拉巴克格外敏感的历史情结所在。马来西亚对沙巴行使主权与有效管辖，但菲律宾从未完全放弃源自苏禄苏丹国的历史主权主张。这一问题在国与国层面大体休眠，却从未从两国的政治与历史意识中消失。这绝不意味着马尼拉在巴拉巴克的军事集结是针对马来西亚、或与其历史主张有关——并无证据证明这一点。但地理总以固执的方式提醒各国政府那些未决的历史。从吉隆坡的视角看，巴拉巴克海峡对面的军事设施不能只用菲中争端的透镜去读，而必须透过马来西亚自身的安全利益来评估。

## 安全困境的逻辑

这正是经典的"安全困境"（security dilemma）登场之处。哈立德防长谨慎地表示，吉隆坡并不把菲律宾、美国或中国单独视为具体威胁。他的担忧更宽泛：当更多军事资产在相对狭小的地理空间内运作，误解、误判与非预期升级的风险随之上升。一国因感到不安全而加强军力；邻国看到集结后感到威胁，于是也加强自身军力；前者又将后者的反应视为进一步军事化的理由。我们未必已进入军备竞赛，但区域安全困境的早期动态已清晰可见。

## 北京的看法

中国则有不同的解读。国防部新闻发言人张晓刚大校指出，域外国家不远万里来南海炫耀武力、蓄意拱火，才是制造紧张的乱源；马尼拉"倚外闹海"、兴风作浪，不断侵权挑衅，损害地区国家共同利益。北京偏好的手段被分析人士称为"灰色地带"（grey-zone）策略——具有强制性却低于武装冲突门槛，旨在施加压力而不给华盛顿提供条约触发点。结果是一种人人盯着人人的紧张均衡。

## 对东盟意味着什么

外溢风险恰好撞上区域外交的尴尬时刻。菲律宾是2026年东盟轮值主席国，这让马来西亚更难把议题摆上议程——东道主通常掌握议程设置权。同情吉隆坡的分析人士认为，马来西亚的最佳选项是倚重国际法（首要是《联合国海洋法公约》（United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, UNCLOS））以及东盟与双边渠道，推动透明度与建立信任——在增强能力与避免军备竞赛、不选边站之间保持平衡。说易行难。当一个声索国身后有超级大国撑腰、另一个决意抵抗时，中立中间斡旋的空间便被压缩。

沙巴的启示很清楚。南海早已不只是"谁拥有哪块礁石"的问题，而是一起争端中的军事集结如何外溢到那些在紧张开始很久之后才划定的边界。马来西亚并未自请成为前沿国家，但地理——以及如今停在距其海岸仅50公里处的大国博弈——让它别无选择地成了前沿。

## 延伸阅读

**《加强防务合作协议》（Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement, EDCA）**：菲律宾2014年与美国签署的框架，授予美军指定菲军事基地的使用权；至2026年由5处扩至9处，多靠近南海与台湾海峡。

**安全困境（security dilemma）**：国际关系中的一种情形，一国为增进安全而采取的措施引发他国反应，反而使其更不安全，因为对方会相应扩充自身军力。

**苏禄苏丹国主张（Sulu Sultanate claim）**：菲律宾对沙巴休眠主权主张的历史依据，沙巴原为苏禄苏丹国领土，1963年并入马来西亚。

# Why Sabah has Malaysia watching South China Sea tensions more closely

The South China Sea has always been a crowded stage. But something has changed on its southern rim. According to the South China Morning Post, Malaysia is watching the waters off its Sabah state with unusual attention — not because Kuala Lumpur has picked a fight, but because it fears being dragged into someone else's conflict. The trigger is a short stretch of water and a rapidly expanding military base on the other side of it.

## The flashpoint next door

Balabac is the southernmost island of the Philippines, sitting barely 50 kilometres north of Sabah's Banggi Island. The two are separated by the 12-kilometre-wide Balabac Strait, a chokepoint linking the Sulu Sea to the South China Sea. Today that quiet maritime corridor has become a geopolitical hotspot. Under the Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement (EDCA), signed with Washington in 2014, the United States has gained access to nine Philippine military sites. Two of them sit on Palawan, including the Narciso Del Rosario Naval Base on Balabac, where a roughly three-kilometre military runway is under construction. As of May 2026, expansion contracts for the EDCA sites were valued at about US$125 million. American forces are not merely visiting; they are pouring concrete, building hangars and laying fuel lines.

## Kuala Lumpur's quiet alarm

Malaysia has not stayed silent. Defence Minister Mohamed Khaled Nordin said publicly on 3 October that if something happened in that narrow geographic space, it could affect his country too. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim had already voiced concern in August, noting that as a neighbour directly affected by the Sabah sovereignty claim, Malaysia was uneasy about the growing US-backed Philippine military presence — even as bilateral ties with Manila remained good. Anwar conceded bluntly that Malaysia cannot match the scale of the facilities being built on Balabac and must therefore strengthen its own basic defence capacity.

## A dormant claim, an awake anxiety

This is what makes Balabac so historically sensitive. Malaysia administers Sabah and exercises sovereign and effective control over it, but the Philippines has never fully abandoned a historical claim rooted in the Sultanate of Sulu. The issue lies largely dormant at the state-to-state level, yet it has never disappeared from the political memory of either country. None of this means Manila's build-up on Balabac is aimed at Malaysia or connected to its historical claim — there is no evidence for that. But geography has a stubborn way of reminding governments of unresolved history. From Kuala Lumpur's vantage point, the military infrastructure across the Balabac Strait cannot be read only through the lens of the Philippines–China dispute; it must be assessed through Malaysia's own security interests.

## The logic of the security dilemma

This is where the classic security dilemma sets in. Defence Minister Khaled has been careful to say Kuala Lumpur does not single out the Philippines, the United States or China as a specific threat. His concern is broader: as more military assets operate in a relatively confined geographic space, the risks of misunderstanding, miscalculation and unintended escalation rise. One state builds up because it feels insecure; a neighbour, seeing the build-up, feels threatened and builds up in turn; the first then reads that reaction as further justification. We are not necessarily in an arms race yet, but the early dynamics of a regional security dilemma are visible.

## Beijing's read

China, for its part, frames the matter differently. Defence ministry spokesman Senior Colonel Zhang Xiaogang has argued that extra-regional countries coming all this way to flex military muscle in the South China Sea are the source of tension, while Manila's reliance on outside powers to "make waves" undermines the common interests of regional countries. Beijing's preferred instrument is what analysts call the grey-zone approach — coercive but below the threshold of armed conflict, designed to apply pressure without handing Washington a treaty trigger. The result is a nervous equilibrium in which everyone is watching everyone else.

## What it means for Asean

The spillover risk lands at an awkward moment for regional diplomacy. The Philippines chairs Asean in 2026, which makes it harder for Malaysia to put the issue on the agenda; the host usually sets it. Analysts sympathetic to Kuala Lumpur argue that Malaysia's best option is to lean on international law, above all the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), and on Asean and bilateral channels to promote transparency and confidence-building — building capacity without entering an arms race or taking sides. That is easier said than done. When one claimant is backed by a superpower and another is determined to resist, the space for neutral middle powers shrinks.

The lesson of Sabah is plain. The South China Sea is no longer just about who owns which rock or reef. It is about how the military build-up of one dispute can leak across borders that were drawn long after the tensions began. Malaysia did not ask to be a frontline state. But geography — and the great-power rivalry now parked 50 kilometres from its shore — has made it one anyway.

## Further reading

**Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement (EDCA)**: The 2014 Philippines–US framework granting American forces access to designated Philippine bases; expanded from five to nine sites by 2026, with several near the South China Sea and the Taiwan Strait.

**Security dilemma**: A situation in international relations in which one state's measures to increase its security cause reactions that make it less secure, as others respond by building up their own military capacity.

**Sulu Sultanate claim**: The historical basis of the Philippines' dormant sovereignty claim over Sabah, a former territory of the Sulu Sultanate that became part of Malaysia in 1963.
