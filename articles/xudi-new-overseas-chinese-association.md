# 从新中式餐厅到新一代侨团：一位法国新侨的"本色与创新"

2009年，徐𬱖（Xu Di）远赴法国里昂（Lyon）求学，从餐厅兼职到创办新式中餐厅，再到2026年3月成立法国奥罗阿中法青年协会，十余年间，她一直在寻找属于这一代新侨的"新答案"。近日，她接受中国侨网《华人故事》栏目采访，讲述的不仅是一家餐厅的创业史，更是一条新侨融入当地、重建组织纽带的路径。在观察者看来，这家名为"New China"的餐厅与这个平均年龄二十八至四十岁的青年侨团，恰好构成理解当代欧洲新侨群体的一对样本：创新不必以丢掉本色为代价，扎根也不等于囿于旧壳。

## 食物的名片：文化认同的直观载体

徐𬱖初到法国，靠在华人餐馆周末兼职支撑学业。她后来意识到，食物是一种最直观的文化名片——常用的食材、烹饪手法、菜品的呈现方式与通过嗅觉味觉传递的风味，都能让人直接感受背后的文化意蕴。这种来自一线的体感，让她在毕业后决心进入餐饮业。

但她的第一步并非中餐，而是日料。理由很现实：在当时法国的亚洲餐饮市场，日料、越南菜的接受度更高，中餐却多被固化为外卖店与酸甜口味的快餐印象，特色川菜虽地道，却以华侨华人为主要客群，重油重辣难以打开当地市场。徐𬱖的选择透露出新侨创业的一个基本判断：与其在已被日料占据的成熟赛道硬拼，不如回归自身饮食文化、做出差异化。

## 平衡之道：本色与创新的张力

2018年，她创办"New China"餐厅，以"中法融合"探索海外中餐的新解法。落地后真正的难题，是如何在"正宗中式口味"与"本地市场习惯"之间找到支点。为适应当地习惯，餐厅采用分餐制，却引来两边不满：侨胞嫌少了中餐围坐分享的热闹，法国食客又觉得与印象中的中餐差距太大。

徐𬱖的应对是改良而非迎合。她减少内脏、带骨占比高的菜式，选用鱼虾禽肉等欧洲常见食材，在降低油盐与勾芡的同时保留爆炒的锅气；用中法双语写菜单，为每道菜附上食材与口味说明。招牌"金丝扇贝虾球"把虾做成虾滑、中间嵌扇贝颗粒、外层裹酸甜酱与极细土豆丝；"紫米鹅肝塔"则用法国代表性的鹅肝搭配中餐常见的紫米，在丝滑中添嚼劲。融合的口感让餐厅同时赢得华侨华人与法国食客，也印证了一个朴素的道理：先让食客因熟悉的食材愿意尝试，再靠口味争取认可。

更难得的是"真诚"二字的落地。她把烤鸭的切片摆盘在桌边完整呈现，手把手教客人卷薄饼、配葱丝黄瓜与甜面酱；培训员工对老年顾客推荐更软的菜、对不熟悉中餐的顾客详解辣度。这些细节帮她结识了附近的音乐家、银行职员，甚至一位搬走后仍互发问候的瑞典老先生。饮食因此从生意变成人与人之间真实的连接。

## 从餐桌到侨团：新侨的组织实验

餐厅里的一次触动，催生了侨团。当国内英歌舞队到里昂演出反响热烈，徐𬱖却发现有年轻侨胞对此一无所知——这让她意识到，当地青年侨胞缺少一个分享与交流的平台。2026年3月，在中国驻里昂总领馆举办的"中国发展与世界机遇"交流活动中，法国奥罗阿中法青年协会公开亮相。

协会至今已举办龙舟体验、高山徒步、冰雪运动、知识讲座、技能培训、文化参访、经贸交流等活动，会员年龄集中在二十八至四十岁，行业覆盖经贸、文旅、体育、新能源、人工智能（AI）、医疗、物流、法律、金融、传媒等。这个新侨群体的画像本身就有别于传统侨团：他们职业多元、与主流社会融入度更高，也更有国际化视野。

## 新老之间：侨团工作的"侨魂"

徐𬱖把针对新侨的工作归结为"新"字——新形式的活动既丰富业余生活，也为成员的事业发展与个人成长赋能；新侨行业广泛、融入度高，能在中外交流与维护华人群体权益中发挥前沿作用。但她也反复强调与老一辈侨团侨领的交流："他们的无私奉献和责任担当，始终是海外侨团工作的'侨魂'。"

这番话点出了海外侨团演变的一条主线：新老侨团是创新与传承、开拓与扎根的关系，正如新式中餐脱胎于传统中餐却始终以中餐风味为本，青年侨团也要在活动形式创新的同时固守本心。从一家里昂小餐厅到跨行业的青年协会，徐𬱖的尝试或许规模不大，却提供了一个可观察的微观案例：当新侨不再满足于"老乡抱团"，而是以专业与兴趣为纽带、以文化为媒介主动嵌入当地社会，"侨"的意义正在被重新书写。

## 延伸阅读

**新侨（New Chinese migrants）**：指改革开放后、尤其近二十余年以留学、专业技术、投资等途径移居海外的中国公民及其后裔，职业结构与融入方式较传统老侨更为多元。

**法国奥罗阿大区（Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes）**：法国东南部大区，里昂为其首府，是法国重要的经济、教育与华人聚居中心之一。

**华人社团代际更替（generational succession of Chinese associations）**：指海外传统侨团由早期以同乡、同业为纽带的"老侨领"模式，逐步向以专业、兴趣、青年为主体的新侨组织演进的过程。

# From a New-Chinese Restaurant to a New Generation of Diaspora Associations

In 2009, Xu Di left for Lyon, France, to study. Over the next decade and more, she moved from waiting tables in a Chinese restaurant to founding a fusion eatery, and in March 2026 launched the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes China-France Youth Association. In a recent interview with China Qiao Wang's "Chinese Stories" series, she told a story that is less about one restaurant than about how a new generation of Chinese migrants in Europe is remaking its ties to both homeland and host society. Read closely, the "New China" restaurant and the young association — whose members average between 28 and 40 years old — form a revealing pair of specimens: innovation need not discard one's roots, and putting down roots need not mean staying inside an old shell.

## Food as a business card

When Xu first arrived in France, she supported her studies with weekend shifts in Chinese restaurants. What she learned there was that food is the most immediate cultural business card: the ingredients, the techniques, the plating, and the flavours carried through smell and taste let people feel a culture directly. That frontline intuition later pushed her into the restaurant business.

Her first step, tellingly, was not Chinese cuisine but Japanese. The reasoning was pragmatic: in France's Asian-food market, Japanese and Vietnamese food enjoyed higher acceptance, while Chinese food was often reduced to takeaway boxes and a sweet-and-sour cliché; authentic Sichuan dishes, though genuine, served mostly the Chinese community and their heavy oil and spice repelled local diners. Xu's choice reveals a basic calculus of new-migrant entrepreneurship — rather than fight in the mature lane already owned by Japanese food, return to your own culinary culture and differentiate.

## The balance: authenticity vs. local adaptation

In 2018 she opened "New China," pursuing a Sino-French fusion as a fresh formula for overseas Chinese dining. The real difficulty came after launch: how to balance "authentic Chinese taste" with "local habits." She adopted plated service to suit French custom, only to displease both sides — older compatriots missed the communal warmth of shared dishes, while French diners found it too far from what they expected of Chinese food.

Xu responded by refining rather than capitulating. She cut dishes heavy in offal and bones, used familiar European proteins like fish, shrimp and poultry, kept the wok hei of stir-frying while lowering oil, salt and starch, and wrote bilingual menus explaining each dish's ingredients and flavour. The signature "golden shrimp and scallop ball" blends shrimp mousse with scallop bits under a sweet-and-sour glaze and a fine jacket of shredded potato; the "purple-rice foie gras tower" pairs France's signature foie gras with China's common purple rice, adding chew to silkiness. The fusion won over both Chinese and French customers, confirming a simple truth: let diners try because the ingredients are familiar, then earn acceptance through taste.

What stands out is how literally she takes "sincerity." She carves and plates the Peking duck at the table, teaching guests to wrap it with pancakes, scallions, cucumber and sweet bean sauce; she trains staff to suggest softer dishes for elderly patrons and to explain spice levels to the uninitiated. These details turned the restaurant into real human connections — with a nearby musician, a banker, even a Swedish gentleman who moved away yet still exchanges greetings. Food, in her hands, became a bond between people.

## From the dining table to the association

A moment in the restaurant seeded the association. When a Yingge dance troupe from China performed to great acclaim in Lyon, Xu found young compatriots who had never heard of it — proof that local youth lacked a platform to share and connect. In March 2026, at a "China's Development and the World's Opportunities" event hosted by the Chinese consulate in Lyon, the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes China-France Youth Association was formally launched.

Since then it has run dragon-boat experiences, mountain hikes, winter sports, lectures, skills training, cultural visits and business exchanges. Members, mostly 28 to 40, span trade, tourism, sport, new energy, AI, healthcare, logistics, law, finance and media. The profile alone distinguishes it from traditional associations: professionally diverse, highly integrated into mainstream society, and internationally minded.

## Heritage and pioneering

Xu sums up new-migrant work in one word — "new." New-format activities enrich leisure and empower members' careers and growth; new migrants, broadly employed and well integrated, can play a frontline role in China-foreign exchange and in defending the community's interests. Yet she insists on learning from the older generation of leaders: "their selfless dedication and sense of duty are always the 'soul' of overseas diaspora work."

That sentence names the main thread of diaspora-organisation evolution: old and new associations relate as innovation to inheritance, as pioneering to rootedness — much as new-style Chinese food grows from tradition yet keeps Chinese flavour at its core, so youth associations must innovate in form while holding to their essence. From a small Lyon restaurant to a cross-sector youth group, Xu's experiment may be modest in scale, but it is an observable micro-case: when new migrants stop settling for "fellow provincials huddling together" and instead organise by profession and interest, using culture as a medium to embed themselves in local society, the very meaning of "qiao" — overseas Chinese — is being rewritten.

## Further reading

**New Chinese migrants**: Citizens who emigrated after China's reform and opening, especially in the last two decades via study, skilled work or investment; their occupational structure and integration differ markedly from earlier "old" migrants.

**Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes**: A southeastern French region whose capital is Lyon, a major centre of economy, education and Chinese settlement in France.

**Generational succession of Chinese associations**: The shift of overseas Chinese organisations from hometown- or trade-based "old leader" models toward youth- and profession-oriented new migrant groups.
