# 泰国普吉九皇斋节：当华人信仰长成一张旅游名片

## 一场延续百余年的节庆走向台前

据中国新闻网10月19日自普吉报道，今年九皇斋节于10月10日至18日举行。九天里，普吉各地华人庙宇陆续举行祭祀、巡游、过火、过桥以及送神等传统仪式，这个原本属于移民社区的节庆日历，如今吸引了来自不同国家和地区的游客。记者在一座庙宇看到的景象颇具代表性：白衣信众焚香祈福，红灯笼悬于檐下，朱红立柱上盘绕彩色巨龙，庙前耸立关公塑像——而举着镜头的游客，也越来越多了。

一个在同化压力下延续百余年的节庆，如今被写进了一个国家的旅游叙事。这件事值得认真对待，而不只是一篇游记素材。

## 岛屿之下的福建根系

普吉与中国的联系并非装饰性的，而是靠锡矿打底的。十九世纪起，来自福建的闽南移民陆续抵达普吉淘锡谋生，其后裔逐渐成为当地商业与社会的主干。普吉泰华博物馆所在建筑由早期华人移民建于1934年，馆内的历史照片与文献记录了采矿、商业、教育等活动对当地社会的影响，也记录了华人信仰、服饰、饮食和节庆同本土社会逐步交融的过程。

值得点明的是：正因如此，九皇斋节在普吉的意义，与一场「漂洋出海」的中国文化活动完全不同。它不是外来项目，而是本地遗产。有了这条福建根系，泰国国家旅游局把2026年九皇斋节作为「普吉深厚的福建华人文化底蕴」来推广时，听起来才不像宣传口号，而像陈述事实。

## 从社区祭祀到城市景观

移民社区的节庆通常只有两种命运：悄然式微，或被定格为民俗标本。普吉九皇斋节走出了第三条路——制度化地「被看见」。白衣斋戒与庙宇祭祀继续承担社区认同锚点的功能；街头巡游、特色斋食和过火这类极具画面感的仪式，则撑起了一整套节庆经济，让整座城市进入节日状态。

有人会问：信仰被做成景观，是否意味着意义在流失？或许有。但看看反面：从未被看见的遗产，往往连「活着」的机会都没有。对移民后裔而言，节庆被主流社会郑重对待，本身就是一种承认。

## 旅游局为何亲自下场

泰国国家旅游局将2026年普吉九皇斋节作为当地重要文化旅游体验进行推广，算盘并不难懂。普吉的海滨度假产品面临区域内激烈竞争，文化厚度才是差异化的筹码；而在旅游复苏的大背景下，华语客源市场举足轻重——一个既地道华人、又「泰味十足」的节庆，恰好两头都能打动。

对外国游客而言，「先在博物馆读懂历史、再走进节庆现场」的动线，提供了海滨日光浴无法复制的层次感。游客走进华人庙宇、观看传统仪式、品尝斋食，实际上是在读一部普吉华人社区的发展史。这也是泰国旅游当局推广的重点所在。

## 自发生长的文化样本

真正值得做的对比在这里。中国在对外文化项目上投入巨大——孔子学院、中华文化大乐园、各类语言博物馆，这些属于供给驱动，外界往往也以「供给」视之。普吉九皇斋节恰恰相反：需求驱动、社区内生、早已织入东道国社会。它展示了文化软实力最耐久的形式——不需要任何部门去组织，因为它本来就在那里生长。

对东南亚各地的华侨华人社区来说，这个样本有两面启示。融合没有抹掉他们的文化，反而给了它一个本地身份，最终还给了它一个市场。九皇斋节所呈现的，正是中华文化不再是「出口品」、而成为「街坊日常」之后的样子。这份从信仰到名片的路程，走了一百多年，比任何推广预算都更有说服力。

## 延伸阅读

- **九皇斋节（Nine Emperor Gods Festival）**：农历九月举行的连续九天节庆，是泰国、马来西亚、新加坡闽南裔社群的共同信仰实践；在普吉又称素食节，以白衣斋戒为标志。
- **福建移民（Hokkien diaspora）**：自闽南下南洋的移民群体，构成海岛东南亚华人社会的中坚，携带闽南语、庙宇与饮食文化在侨居地落地生根。
- **文化旅游（Cultural tourism）**：以体验目的地活态文化与遗产为动机的旅行方式，日益被各国旅游部门用作成熟海岛目的地的差异化抓手。

# Phuket's Nine Emperor Gods Festival: When Chinese Faith Becomes a Tourism Card

## A century-old rite steps into the spotlight

According to China News Service reporting from Phuket on October 19, this year's Nine Emperor Gods Festival ran from October 10 to 18. Over nine days, Chinese temples across the island held sacrificial rites, street processions, fire-walking, bridge-crossing ceremonies and the final send-off of the gods. A festival calendar that once belonged to a migrant community now draws visitors from many countries. The scene at one temple was telling: worshippers in white burning incense, red lanterns under the eaves, colorful dragons coiled around vermilion pillars, a statue of Guan Gong standing guard — and, increasingly, tourists with cameras.

A festival that survived a century of assimilation pressure is now being written into a nation's tourism narrative. That shift deserves serious attention, not just a travel-note treatment.

## The Fujian roots beneath the island

Phuket's connection to China is not decorative; it was built on tin. From the nineteenth century, Hokkien migrants from Fujian arrived to work the island's mines, and their descendants became the backbone of local commerce and society. The Phuket Thaihua Museum, housed in a building erected by early Chinese settlers in 1934, traces this arc: its photos and documents record how mining, trade and schooling shaped local society, and how Chinese beliefs, dress, food and festivals gradually interwove with the host community.

This is precisely why the festival reads differently from a Chinese cultural event staged abroad. It is not imported programming; it is local heritage. With those Fujian roots in place, when the Tourism Authority of Thailand promotes the 2026 festival as an expression of "Phuket's deep Hokkien Chinese cultural heritage", it sounds not like a slogan but a statement of fact.

## From neighborhood rite to urban spectacle

Festivals of migrant communities usually face two fates: quiet erosion, or preservation as a folkloric specimen. Phuket's Nine Emperor Gods Festival has taken a third path — institutionalized visibility. White-clad fasting and temple rites continue to anchor community identity, while street processions, vegetarian food and photogenic rituals such as fire-walking sustain a festival economy that puts the whole city into holiday mode.

Some will ask: does turning faith into spectacle mean losing its meaning? Perhaps partly. But consider the counterfactual: heritage that is never seen rarely gets the chance to stay alive at all. For descendants of migrants, having their festival taken seriously by the mainstream is itself a form of recognition.

## Why the tourism authority stepped in

Thailand's Tourism Authority promoted the 2026 festival as a key cultural tourism experience, and the calculus is easy to read. Phuket's beach product faces fierce regional competition, and cultural depth is the differentiator; amid the tourism recovery, Chinese-speaking source markets matter enormously. A festival that is authentically Chinese yet distinctly Thai speaks to both audiences at once.

For foreign visitors, the "understand the history first, then walk into the festival" route offers layers that a beach holiday cannot replicate. Visiting Chinese temples, watching traditional rites and tasting vegetarian food is, in effect, reading the history of Phuket's Chinese community. That is exactly what the tourism authority is selling.

## A home-grown model of cultural confidence

The contrast worth making is this one. Beijing invests heavily in outward cultural programs — Confucius Institutes, culture camps, language museums. These are supply-driven, and they are often received as such. Phuket's festival is the opposite: demand-driven, community-born, long woven into the host society. It displays the most durable form of cultural soft power — the kind no ministry has to organize, because it grew there.

For overseas Chinese communities across Southeast Asia, the lesson cuts both ways. Integration did not erase their culture; it gave the culture a local identity and, eventually, a market. The Nine Emperor Gods Festival shows what Chinese culture looks like once it stops being an export and becomes part of the neighborhood. The road from faith to tourism card took more than a century — and it is more persuasive than any promotional budget.

## Further reading

- **Nine Emperor Gods Festival (九皇斋节)**: A nine-day festival in the ninth lunar month, shared by Hokkien-descended communities in Thailand, Malaysia and Singapore; in Phuket it is known as the Vegetarian Festival, marked by white garments and meat-free diets.
- **Hokkien diaspora (福建移民)**: Migrants from southern Fujian who formed the backbone of Chinese communities in maritime Southeast Asia, carrying the Minnan language, temples and cuisine to their new homes.
- **Cultural tourism (文化旅游)**: Travel motivated by experiencing a destination's living culture and heritage, increasingly used by national tourism bodies to differentiate mature beach destinations.
