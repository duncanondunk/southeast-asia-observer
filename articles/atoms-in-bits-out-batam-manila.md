# 原子进来，比特出去：峇淡岛的工厂与马尼拉的工位

同一周的两条东南亚新闻，指向相反的方向。一条来自印尼峇淡岛：在美国关税战推动下，这座距新加坡40分钟船程的小岛正成为代工转移的新落点——东莞玩具厂商在此开设中国境外首座工厂，苹果的AirTag工厂供应全球约七成需求，甲骨文、基汇资本、Firmus与英伟达的数据中心项目相继落地，去年经济增速6.8%，高于全国的5.1%。另一条来自菲律宾：外包业员工在亲手训练AI之后被裁，一位从业15年的内容写手说，"感觉就像亲手挖了自己的坟墓"。

把两条新闻并排读，会看到一个被忽略的结构：关税把**实体生产**推进东南亚，生成式AI却把**认知型工作**从东南亚抽走。两者的委托方其实是同一个——欧美企业的成本决策。区域不是在单纯"获益"，而是在被重新分选。

更值得警惕的是三重不对称。其一，可逆性相反：工厂搬迁靠沉没成本固定下来，却可能被下一纸关税令逆转；AI替代不需要沉没成本，却几乎不可逆。其二，就业密度相反：菲律宾外包业以约190万个岗位换来约400亿美元收入，而360兆瓦的数据中心资本密集、用人极少。其三，分配效应相反：外包业曾是数百万菲律宾人无需精英学历即可进入中产的阶梯，峇淡岛的新投资并不提供同一部电梯。

因此，真正的问题不是"如何吸引产业转移"，而是"转移进来的产业，能否吸纳被自动化释放的人"。答案大概率是否定的——它们分属不同岛屿、不同技能、不同国家。GDP的算术可以很好看，而造就中产的引擎却可能同时熄火。

# Atoms In, Bits Out: Batam's Factories, Manila's Desks, and Southeast Asia's Two-Front Reordering

Two Southeast Asian stories surfaced in the same news cycle this week, and they point in opposite directions.

The first came from Batam, an Indonesian island forty minutes by ferry from Singapore, long overlooked by global investors. Under the pressure of Washington's escalating tariff regime, it has become a destination for relocated contract manufacturing. In July, a Dongguan-based plastics and hardware manufacturer — a licensed producer for Disney, Crayola and other international brands — opened its first plant outside China there, turning out more than thirty lines of Disney-related toys and gifts. Its executive was candid about the logic: clients demanded supply-chain diversification after last April's tariff measures, Chinese costs kept climbing, and after surveying several Southeast Asian options the firm chose Batam. "We had no choice but to diversify," he said. "If we stayed in China, we might lose the orders."

He is not alone on the island. Apple has built an AirTag facility there that reportedly supplies around seventy percent of global demand. Oracle and Hong Kong's Gaw Capital have committed to cloud and data-centre projects. Most recently the Australian firm Firmus Technologies announced a 360-megawatt data centre in partnership with Nvidia. Batam grew 6.8 percent last year against a national rate of 5.1 percent; Indonesia's investment ministry has opened a permanent office on the island, and President Prabowo Subianto met local officials last month to discuss expanding its international port. As one industrial-park developer put it, Trump may not realise he has engineered Batam's rise.

The second story came from the Philippines, and it is the mirror image. A content writer with fifteen years' experience described being assigned to edit AI-generated articles so the system could learn her employer's house style — and then being dismissed a month before her probation ended. "It felt like digging my own grave," she said. A colleague reported the same arc: AI output riddled with errors, more hours spent verifying than writing, and then redundancy anyway.

The Philippine business-process outsourcing sector — the "call centre capital of the world" — employs roughly 1.9 million people, generates about US$40 billion a year, and accounts for close to a tenth of the national economy. The International Labour Organization estimates that some 12.7 million Filipino workers hold jobs exposed to generative AI: more than a quarter of employment, the highest share in Southeast Asia.

Read side by side, these are not two unrelated items. They are two columns of the same ledger.

## One principal, two agents

The conventional framing treats tariff-driven relocation as Southeast Asia's windfall and AI displacement as Southeast Asia's misfortune, as though the region were simply lucky in one domain and unlucky in another. That framing misses the common cause.

Both movements originate in the same place: cost decisions made by firms selling into North American and European markets. When Washington raises the price of Chinese-origin goods, those firms move assembly to Batam. When the same firms face margin pressure and a plausible substitution technology, they thin out the offshore desks in Manila. The principal is identical; only the instrument differs. Southeast Asia is not two economies experiencing two shocks. It is one region being re-sorted by a single set of external balance sheets.

This matters because it changes what "diversification" means. Batam's boom is not evidence that the region has secured a more robust position in global production. It is evidence that the region remains highly responsive to decisions taken elsewhere — which is precisely the condition that also makes Manila's BPO seats removable at short notice. Responsiveness is an asset in an upswing and a liability in a reallocation.

## Three asymmetries worth naming

If the cause is shared, the consequences are not symmetric. Three differences deserve attention.

**Reversibility runs the wrong way.** Factory relocation involves sunk capital — land, permits, tooling, trained supervisors — which makes it sticky in the short run but not permanent. A change in the tariff schedule, a bilateral deal, a shift in the political calculus in Washington, and the arithmetic that made Batam attractive can be undone. The relocation is real but contingent. AI displacement carries the opposite profile: it requires little sunk investment, and once a workflow is automated it is not restored when demand recovers. The gain, in other words, is revocable; the loss is structural. Any regional planner who nets the two against each other is making a category error.

**Job density runs the wrong way.** The BPO sector's virtue was never its margin — it was its headcount. Roughly 1.9 million jobs for US$40 billion in revenue is a labour-intensive ratio by any standard. Batam's incoming investment is stratified. Toy moulding and AirTag assembly do employ people, though at modest wages and in narrow skill bands. But the data-centre projects — a 360-megawatt facility above all — belong to a different economic species entirely: enormously capital-intensive, land- and power-hungry, and employing a few hundred technicians where a BPO campus of comparable capital value would seat thousands. The island's attractions, as reported, include gas-fired power for more than eighty percent of its supply, a younger workforce, tax incentives and proximity to Singapore's shipping hub. Those are inputs that suit compute infrastructure at least as well as they suit labour-absorbing industry. Batam may well win more investment than Manila loses, measured in dollars, while generating far fewer jobs.

**The distributional engine runs the wrong way.** This is the consequential one. Philippine outsourcing was not merely an employer; it was a ladder. It offered entry-level, English-language work to hundreds of thousands of young people without elite credentials or family capital, and it moved them into the middle class within a few years. Very little else in the Philippine economy performs that function at scale. Batam's new investment does not offer the same escalator — different island, different country, different skills, different entry requirements. The aggregate figures for Southeast Asia can look perfectly healthy while the specific mechanism that manufactured a middle class quietly seizes up.

## The Nanyang circuit, under a new name

There is a longer history embedded in the Batam story that is easy to miss. Consider the shape of the transaction: capital and management from South China, land and labour in the archipelago, market access and logistics through Singapore. That is not a new configuration. It is the commercial geography that ethnic Chinese trading networks operated across the Riau–Singapore corridor for more than a century — the Nanyang circuit, rebuilt with containerised shipping and a Disney licence.

The Dongguan manufacturer opening in Batam is, in this reading, less a novelty than a resumption. What the tariff war has done is make an old route economically rational again. The observation is not romantic; it has an analytical payoff. Networks of this kind carry advantages that greenfield foreign investment does not — familiarity with local partners, tolerance for institutional ambiguity, faster decision cycles. They also carry a familiar political vulnerability. Concentrated ethnic Chinese commercial presence in Indonesia has historically been an object of periodic resentment, and a visible wave of China-origin factories arriving on Indonesian soil is not a politically neutral fact. Whether Batam's boom is absorbed as national success or coded as foreign encroachment will depend on how broadly its gains are distributed locally — which returns us, again, to job density.

## A necessary caveat on attribution

Restraint is warranted on the Manila side of the ledger. Industry and academic voices quoted in the reporting flag a real measurement problem. The Philippine IT and Business Process Association notes that while more than two-thirds of member firms have piloted AI and some roles are genuinely automatable, most affected staff are redeployed rather than dismissed. Larger operators insist AI handles routine tasks and that workers are being retrained for more complex work. Scholars add a sharper point: outsourcing firms facing client cost cuts and soft demand have an incentive to package ordinary retrenchment as "AI transformation," because the technological framing sounds like strategy rather than weakness.

This does not dissolve the problem — the ILO exposure estimate stands on its own, and the individual accounts are credible — but it should discipline the analysis. Not every AI-attributed layoff is caused by AI. The honest formulation is that generative AI has arrived as both a genuine substitution technology and a convenient narrative, and separating the two will take better data than currently exists. Manila's government has pledged reskilling for more than 300,000 BPO workers and support for domestic AI firms; officials have acknowledged, unusually frankly, that the country must move faster or watch an industry that lifted millions into the middle class leave part of its workforce behind.

## What follows

The policy question, stated plainly, is not "how do we attract relocating industry." Southeast Asia is already good at that, and Batam demonstrates it. The question is whether the industry arriving can absorb the people that automation is releasing.

On present evidence the answer is no, for reasons that are geographic before they are economic. The factories are landing in the Riau Islands; the redundancies are occurring in Metro Manila and Cebu. The skills do not map; the labour markets do not clear across national borders; ASEAN has no mechanism that would make them do so. Two member states are experiencing a gain and a loss that look complementary on a regional spreadsheet and are entirely disconnected in practice.

The modest conclusion is this. Southeast Asia's current moment is usually narrated as a windfall — the "China plus one" dividend, the region as the great beneficiary of great-power friction. Batam supports that reading. Manila complicates it. The region is receiving atoms and shedding bits, and the exchange is neither balanced nor within its control. Recognising that is not pessimism. It is the precondition for asking a better question than "how much investment did we attract this quarter."
