# 泰国（Thailand）清算「代持买房」，马来西亚（Malaysia）开门迎客：一场争夺富裕移民的区域竞赛

## 一夜之间，房子不再属于你

据《南华早报》（South China Morning Post）报道，泰国对非法外国人持有房产的打击已持续一年，一场原本让人安心的交易，变成了法律噩梦。一些外国人以为自己买下了别墅，如今却发现从未合法拥有过它。没收、逮捕、起诉接踵而至。英国人布莱恩·拉姆斯登（Brian Ramsden）长期在社交媒体上以克制著称的讲解回应此类疑问，他告诉该报：「外面有很多困惑、恐惧和失眠。」一些人被迫卖掉房产。

这不是土地登记系统的一次技术故障，而是对一种安排的主动拆解——过去数十年，正是这种安排悄悄支撑着外国资本涌入泰国度假地产。问题不在于这场打击是否合法：它当然合法。问题在于，为什么是现在，以及谁从风险的重新分配中获益。

## 灰色路径如何长成：49%的天花板

泰国《土地法》与《外国商业法》（1999年）原则上禁止外国人拥有永久产权土地，但允许其在49%的外资配额内购买公寓，或注册30年租赁。数十年来，想绕过土地限制的外国买家形成了一套固定操作：设立泰国有限公司，由泰国籍股东挂名持有大部分股份却不实际出资，控制权则牢牢握在外方手中。房产转售时，土地本身不过户，只是更换那49%的外资股东名字。

在芭堤雅，数以万计的外国人持有公寓。业内观察人士警告，即便是遵守49%上限的楼盘，如今也可能面临更严格的审查。这套灰色安排之所以能长期盛行，是因为它同时满足了三方：买家拿到资产，中介拿到佣金，地方拿到投资。而代价，被推迟到执法到来的那一天。

## 从抽查到全国清算

2026年，泰国从零星检查转向全国协调执法。商业发展厅要求泰国籍共同投资人证明资金来源合法；总理阿努廷（Anutin Charnvirakul）在10月启动三管齐下行动，要求官员在12月前优先调查高风险对象。政府数据显示，此轮打击涉及逾11000家公司、约850亿泰铢（约合175亿元人民币）的房地产资产。

在普吉、甲米、春武里和华欣等地，突击行动已查获数十处价值数十亿泰铢的豪华别墅项目。刑事处罚最高可达3年监禁，罚款最高100万泰铢（约合21万元人民币）。点评一句：当「合规」变成可以追溯追究的标准，法律风险就从一项可计算交易成本，变成了随时可能被没收的风险——而这恰恰是资本最不愿定价的东西。

## 不只泰国：巴厘岛与越南（Vietnam）

区域范围内同步收紧的并非只有泰国。在印尼（Indonesia）巴厘岛，当局开始针对用于规避外国人永久产权禁令的代持安排采取行动：2月停止农地转换，当地媒体当月报道约10500块土地以这类非法安排持有。岛上的土地主管部门负责人4月承认，代持安排通常只有在产权纠纷出现时才会浮出水面。在越南，一份住房法草案拟允许外国人按现有楼宇配额继续购买公寓，却禁止其购买别墅、联排等独立土地房屋，意在消除监管灰色地带。

越南第一太平戴维斯（Savills Vietnam）住宅销售与私人客户业务负责人黄氏香（Huong Do）建议外国买家在付款或签约前完成全面的法律与财务尽职调查。她进一步提醒：「从泰国及其他国际市场得到的更广泛教训是，投资者不应依赖非正式的所有权结构、代持安排，或是以为法律的解释与执行会保持不变。」

这轮区域同步收紧并非巧合。疫情之后外资大量涌入，住房成为本地年轻人的心头之痛，外国投资者的身份不再自动换来豁免。

## 马来西亚的算盘：永久产权与7650份申请

泰国收紧的同时，邻国马来西亚准备获益。与泰国、印尼不同，马来西亚允许外国人以自己的名义购买住宅房产，包括永久产权土地，只需满足最低价格门槛并获得各州批准，且独立于长期运行的「马来西亚第二家园计划」（Malaysia My Second Home）。马来西亚第二家园顾问协会主席安东尼·刘（Anthony Liew）说，这让代持问题在马来西亚「不那么成立」，因为限制主要针对农地和马来人保留地。他提到，马来西亚的国际学校被视为孩子将来出国深造的「跳板」。

马来西亚旅游艺术文化部副部长张庆信（Chiew Choon Man）9月表示，该计划在2024年10月至2026年8月间收到7650份申请，创造88.5亿林吉特（约合156亿元人民币）的经济价值，其中逾50亿林吉特（约合88亿元人民币）来自购房。申请者主要来自中国大陆、台湾、香港和新加坡，美国紧随其后。

马来西亚出售的不只是房子，而是一种「产权可预期性」。在一个执法充满不确定性的区域，确定性本身就是溢价。

## 门槛抬高之后，谁还被欢迎

不过，马来西亚的门也并非向所有人敞开。2024年修订的规则要求申请者持有价值60万林吉特（约14.7万美元，约合106万元人民币）的房产，方可获得五年可续签的银级签证；白金级则需200万林吉特（约合353万元人民币），对应20年可续签签证。

2014年申请第二家园计划的美国退休者安德鲁·泰勒（Andrew Taylor）说，早年该计划的门槛较低、马来西亚生活成本也低，让他得以在60岁退休。但如今，高额定期存款——最高相当于100万美元（约合720万元人民币）——加上不菲的中介费，已把中等收入的外籍人士挡在门外。「我现在根本负担不起加入这个计划，」他说，「他们似乎只想要富裕的申请者，并不考虑没那么富裕的人同样为马来西亚经济作贡献。」

一个打着「欢迎投资者」旗号的计划，最终按资本厚度筛选人群。这既是财政理性，也暴露了移民政策中的阶层偏向。而在申请人构成中，来自中国大陆、台湾、香港的海内外华人占据主力，这条移民链条的延续性值得持续观察。

## 套利终结之后

这场区域竞赛的真相，不在于谁更「开放」，而在于谁能在资本流入与社会容忍之间重新定价。泰国的清算、马来西亚的门槛、印尼和越南的收紧，共同宣告了「监管套利」（regulatory arbitrage）时代的结束。过去，外国资本依靠灰色结构绕过限制；如今，各国用价格门槛把不确定性转化为筛选工具。

对华人与海外投资者而言，教训很直接：产权的可执行性，比手里那张产权证更重要。而对东南亚（Southeast Asia）国家来说，考题才刚刚开始——当「欢迎外资」悄悄变成「只欢迎富有的外资」，增长的合法性，又要由谁来买单？

## 延伸阅读

**代持安排（Nominee ownership）**：由本地居民挂名持有资产、实际权益归外国投资者的安排，常见于限制外国人持有土地的经济体，游走于合规与违法之间。

**第二家园计划（Malaysia My Second Home）**：马来西亚面向外国退休者家庭的长期居留签证项目，把购房与居留资格绑定，是该国招揽外部资本的重要工具。

**监管套利（Regulatory arbitrage）**：利用不同司法辖区或法律解释之间的差异降低合规成本的行为，其收益往往建立在执法的不确定性之上。

# Thailand Purges Nominee Homes, Malaysia Opens the Gate: A Regional Contest for Wealthy Migrants

## Overnight, the house was no longer yours

According to the South China Morning Post, a year-long crackdown on illegal foreign property ownership in Thailand has turned a comfortable assumption into a legal nightmare. Foreigners who believed they owned a villa are discovering they never legally did. Confiscations, arrests and prosecutions have followed. "There's a lot of confusion, fear and sleepless nights going on out there," Brian Ramsden, a Briton who has become a trusted voice on social media for his measured explainers on the subject, told the paper. Some people are having to sell their properties.

This is not a technical glitch in the land registry. It is the deliberate unwinding of an arrangement that quietly underwrote decades of foreign investment in Thai resorts. The question is not whether the crackdown is lawful — it plainly is. The question is why it is happening now, and who benefits from the redistribution of risk.

## How the grey path grew: the 49 per cent ceiling

Thailand's Land Code and Foreign Business Act of 1999 bar foreigners from owning land freehold in principle, while allowing them to buy condominium units within a 49 per cent foreign ownership quota or register 30-year leases. For decades, foreign buyers who wanted around the land restrictions settled into a familiar routine: set up a Thai limited company, let Thai shareholders hold the majority of shares on paper without contributing capital, and keep real control in foreign hands. When the property was resold, the land often did not change hands at all — the company simply swapped the name of its 49 per cent foreign shareholder.

Tens of thousands of foreign nationals own condominiums in Pattaya. Industry observers warn that even buildings complying with the 49 per cent limit could now face stricter checks. The grey structure endured because it served three parties at once: buyers secured an asset, agents earned commissions and localities attracted investment. The cost was deferred to the day enforcement arrived.

## From spot checks to a nationwide reckoning

In 2026, Thailand shifted from sporadic checks to a coordinated nationwide enforcement mandate. The Department of Business Development required Thai co-investors to prove the legitimate source of their funds, and Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul launched a three-part campaign in October directing officials to prioritise high-risk investigations by December. Government data indicate the campaign spans more than 11,000 companies and roughly 85 billion baht (about US$2.4 billion) in real-estate assets.

High-profile raids in Phuket, Krabi, Chonburi and Hua Hin have seized dozens of luxury villa developments worth billions of baht, with criminal penalties carrying up to three years in prison and fines reaching one million baht (about US$28,500). Consider the implications: once compliance becomes retrospective, legal risk ceases to be a calculable transaction cost and becomes an ever-present threat of confiscation — precisely the risk capital is least willing to price.

## Not just Thailand: Bali and Vietnam

Thailand is not alone in tightening. In Bali, authorities have begun moving against nominee arrangements used to bypass Indonesia's ban on foreign freehold ownership. In February the local government stopped conversions of agricultural land under a rule targeting nominee holdings, and local media reported that roughly 10,500 parcels were held through such illegal arrangements. The island's land agency chief acknowledged in April that nominee holdings typically surface only when ownership disputes arise. In Vietnam, a draft housing law would let foreigners keep buying condominiums under existing building quotas but bar them from purchasing individual landed houses such as villas and townhouses — a tightening aimed at eliminating regulatory grey areas.

Huong Do, head of residential sales and private office at Savills Vietnam, advised foreign buyers to complete comprehensive legal and financial due diligence before paying a deposit or signing any commitment. "A broader lesson from Thailand and other international markets," she added, "is that investors should not rely on informal ownership structures, nominee arrangements or the assumption that the interpretation and enforcement of legislation will remain unchanged."

The regional synchrony is no coincidence. In the wake of the post-pandemic influx of foreign capital, housing has become a raw nerve for local youth, and the foreign investor's status no longer commands an automatic exemption.

## Malaysia's calculus: freehold titles and 7,650 applications

As Thailand tightens, neighbouring Malaysia stands to gain. Unlike Thailand and Indonesia, Malaysia lets foreigners buy residential property — including freehold land — in their own names, subject to minimum price thresholds and approval from individual state authorities, independent of the long-running Malaysia My Second Home (MM2H) residency programme. Anthony Liew, president of the MM2H Consultants Association, says this makes nominee ownership less of an issue, since restrictions largely cover only agricultural land and Malay reserve land. He points to Malaysia's international schools as "a stepping stone" towards further studies overseas.

Chiew Choon Man, the country's deputy tourism, arts and culture minister, said in September that the programme had received 7,650 applications between October 2024 and August 2026, generating 8.85 billion ringgit (US$2.17 billion) in economic value — more than 5 billion ringgit (about US$1.23 billion) of it in property purchases. Most applicants came from mainland China, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore, with the United States close behind.

What Malaysia is selling is not merely property but predictability of title. In a region where enforcement is uncertain, certainty itself commands a premium.

## After the threshold rose, who is still welcome

The gate, however, is not open to everyone. Revised rules introduced in 2024 require applicants to hold property worth 600,000 ringgit (US$147,200) for the entry-level silver tier, which offers a five-year renewable visa, and 2 million ringgit (about US$490,000) for the platinum tier, which comes with a 20-year renewable visa.

Andrew Taylor, an American retiree who applied for MM2H in 2014, says the scheme's earlier affordability and Malaysia's lower cost of living allowed him to retire at 60. Today, fixed deposits ranging up to the equivalent of US$1 million and steep agent fees have priced out middle-income expatriates. "I could not afford to enter the programme now," he said. "They only seem to want wealthy applicants and don't consider how even less wealthy applicants contribute to the Malaysian economy."

A programme billed as welcoming investors ends up sorting people by the thickness of their wallets. That is fiscal rationality, but it also exposes the class bias embedded in migration policy. And because applicants from mainland China, Taiwan and Hong Kong form a large share of the pool, the continuity of this Chinese diaspora chain deserves sustained attention.

## After the arbitrage ends

The truth of this regional contest lies not in who is more "open", but in who can re-price the balance between incoming capital and social tolerance. Thailand's reckoning, Malaysia's thresholds, and the tightening in Indonesia and Vietnam together mark the end of the era of regulatory arbitrage. Foreign capital once routed around restrictions through grey structures; now states are converting uncertainty into a screening tool via price thresholds.

For overseas Chinese and investors, the lesson is blunt: the enforceability of a title matters more than the certificate itself. For Southeast Asian governments, the harder question is only beginning — when "welcome foreign capital" quietly becomes "welcome only rich foreign capital", who ultimately pays for the legitimacy of growth?

## Further reading

**Nominee ownership**: An arrangement in which local residents hold assets on paper while the beneficial interest belongs to a foreign investor; common in economies that restrict foreign land ownership, it straddles the line between compliance and illegality.

**Malaysia My Second Home**: Malaysia's long-stay residency visa for foreign retirees and families, tying property purchase to residency rights and serving as a key tool for attracting external capital.

**Regulatory arbitrage**: The practice of exploiting differences between jurisdictions or legal interpretations to lower compliance costs, with gains that often rest on uncertainty in enforcement.
