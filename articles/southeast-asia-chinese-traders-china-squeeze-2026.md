# 被母国"抢生意"：东南亚华人小商贩遭遇中国工厂直销挤压

据《南华早报》（South China Morning Post）近日报道，从印度尼西亚（Indonesia）到菲律宾（Philippines），一些经营日用小商品、服饰和电子配件的华人小商贩开始抱怨：过去老老实实给他们供货的中国工厂，如今不再满足于躲在幕后当"代工厂"，而是自己开起网店、做直播、搞跨境直邮，把货直接卖给终端消费者。一句话——他们的"供货商"变成了"竞争对手"。

这则看似平常的商界花絮，戳中的是东南亚（Southeast Asia）华侨华人一个延续百余年的经济角色：做中国货与本地市场之间的"中间商"。如今这个角色正被母国的数字化直销浪潮一点点掏空。

## 历史的角色：侨商曾是"中国货"的代名词

在东南亚，华人移民长期扮演着"买办"与批发商的角色。从十九世纪的"下南洋"到战后重建，印尼雅加达（Jakarta）、菲律宾马尼拉（Manila）、泰国（Thailand）曼谷的唐人街，华人商铺既是本地人认识"中国制造"的第一窗口，也是中国商品辐射群岛与内陆的集散节点。他们懂两边的语言、吃得透两边的规矩，靠信息差和信任网络赚取代差利润。

这个中间商生态，是华侨华人在东南亚扎根的经济根基之一。它不性感，却足够稳健——只要"中国生产、东南亚消费"的格局不变，侨商的位子就稳。

## 转折：工厂绕过中间商，直接卖给消费者

但格局正在变。中国制造业经过数十年积累，供应链极致内卷，工厂利润薄到以"分"计。为了多挣一点，越来越多工厂不再满足于接外贸订单，而是利用短视频平台（TikTok）、跨境平台（Temu、Shein、AliExpress）和直邮物流，把货直接送到东南亚消费者手里。

过去，"中国工厂 → 华人进口商 → 本地批发 → 零售小贩 → 消费者"是一条长长的链；如今工厂恨不得把链条砍到只剩"工厂 → 消费者"两环。中间每一道被砍掉的环节，都是侨商世代经营的生意。

## 数字：被侵蚀的中间利润

这背后的数字很能说明问题。中国与东盟（ASEAN）贸易额已连续多年站上约1万亿美元（约7.2万亿元人民币）的规模，其中跨境电商是增长最快的板块之一。据行业估算，东南亚社交电商与直播带货的年增速常年保持在两位数，平台补贴和免税额度进一步压低了终端价格。

对消费者当然是好事：同样的商品更便宜了。但对那些靠"加价转售"维生的小商贩而言，这意味着两头受压——进货价被工厂直销击穿，售价又被平台补贴压低，中间利润几乎归零。一位马尼拉的华人零售商对媒体直言，工厂给终端消费者的到手价，比他的进货价还低。

## 印尼与菲律宾：两个切面

在印尼，华人商贸网络深度嵌入批发与零售，从纺织品到日用杂货，传统的"商贩—批发商"层级分明。当中国厂家绕过这些层级直接上线，受影响的不只是个体户，还有一整套依赖层级加价的生计体系。

在菲律宾，大量华人家庭经营社区小店与集市摊位，靠稳定客源和长期赊销维持。跨境直邮的平价冲击，最先动摇的恰恰是他们最看重的"价格优势"和"邻里信任"——当邻居发现网上更便宜，街角小店的生意就难做了。

## 不止是生意：侨商角色的重构

这件事的意义，远不止"一些小商人日子难过"。它触及一个更深的问题：当母国从"产品输出"走向"渠道输出"，海外侨商的传统价值在哪里？

过去，侨商的价值在于"连接"——把中国的产能对接本地需求。如今，平台把"连接"这件事自动化、去人格化了。侨商若只会做搬运和加价，空间只会越来越小；但若能把本地化服务、售后、信任和社区关系做成平台替代不了的东西，反而可能在新格局里找到位置。

事实上，已经有华人商家开始转型：做选品、做品牌代理、做面向本地的内容电商，甚至反过来把东南亚好物卖回中国。角色在变，但"桥"的功能没有消失。

## 悖论：母国越强，侨商越难

这则新闻最耐人寻味的，是一个经济学上的悖论：中国越强、出海越猛，部分东南亚华人小商贩反而越难。当年华人靠"近水楼台"吃到的红利，如今正被母国自己的数字化巨轮碾过。

对华侨华人研究而言，这是一个值得长期跟踪的议题：侨商的经济角色正在被重塑，而重塑的动力不在东道国，而在海峡对岸的故乡。当"侨"不再是天然的渠道优势，离散族群与母国的关系，也到了重新定义的时候。

## 延伸阅读

**跨境电商（Cross-border e-commerce）**：指商家或平台通过互联网将商品直接销售给海外消费者，绕过传统进口商与批发商，是挤压东南亚华人小商贩的核心机制。

**侨商（Overseas Chinese merchant）**：指定居海外的华人所经营的商业网络，历史上长期充当中国商品进入东南亚市场的中间商与集散节点。

**下南洋（Migration to Nanyang）**：十九至二十世纪中国东南沿海民众向东南亚大规模移民的统称，奠定了当地华人商贸网络的人口与商业基础。

# Caught in the squeeze: Southeast Asia's ethnic Chinese traders and the China shock from home

A recent South China Morning Post report describes a quiet grievance among small ethnic Chinese retailers in Indonesia and the Philippines: the Chinese factories that once supplied them are now their toughest competitors. Those factories no longer hide behind the "OEM" label. They run their own online stores, livestream their products, and ship cross-border directly to consumers. In the words of one vendor, the "supplier" has become the "rival."

Behind this mundane business anecdote lies a structural shift in the economic role of Southeast Asia's overseas Chinese — a middleman niche built over more than a century, now being hollowed out by the homeland's digital direct-sales wave.

## The historical role: overseas Chinese as the face of "Made in China"

In Southeast Asia, ethnic Chinese immigrants long served as compradors and wholesalers. From the nineteenth-century "Nanyang" migration to the postwar reconstruction, Chinatowns in Jakarta, Manila, and Bangkok were both the local consumer's first window onto Chinese goods and the distribution hubs radiating Chinese products across archipelagos and hinterlands. They spoke both languages, understood both rulebooks, and earned a margin on the gap in information and trust.

That intermediary ecosystem was one of the economic foundations of the diaspora's rootedness in the region. It was unglamorous but stable — as long as the "China produces, Southeast Asia consumes" pattern held, the overseas Chinese merchant's seat was safe.

## The turn: factories bypass the middleman

The pattern is changing. After decades of accumulation, Chinese manufacturing supply chains have turned brutally competitive, with factory margins thinned to the level of fractions of a cent. To keep a little more, a growing number of factories no longer settle for export orders. Using short-video platforms such as TikTok, cross-border platforms such as Temu, Shein, and AliExpress, and direct-mail logistics, they now deliver goods straight to Southeast Asian consumers.

The old chain — "Chinese factory → ethnic Chinese importer → local wholesaler → retail peddler → consumer" — was long and layered. Today the factory wants to cut it to two links: "factory → consumer." Every link removed is a business that overseas Chinese families ran for generations.

## The numbers: the eroded middle margin

The numbers tell the story. China–ASEAN trade has held above roughly US$1 trillion for years, and cross-border e-commerce is among its fastest-growing segments. Industry estimates put the annual growth of Southeast Asia's social and livestream commerce in the double digits, while platform subsidies and duty-free thresholds further depress end prices.

For consumers this is unambiguously good: the same goods are cheaper. But for small vendors who lived on markups, it means pressure from both sides — their purchase price is undercut by factory-direct sales, and their retail price is suppressed by platform subsidies, leaving the middle margin near zero. A Manila-based ethnic Chinese retailer told the paper that the factory's delivered price to end consumers was lower than his own cost.

## Indonesia and the Philippines: two slices

In Indonesia, ethnic Chinese commercial networks are deeply embedded in wholesale and retail; from textiles to daily sundries, the traditional "peddler–wholesaler" hierarchy is clear. When Chinese manufacturers bypass those layers and go online, what is hit is not only individual traders but an entire livelihood system built on layered markups.

In the Philippines, many ethnic Chinese families run neighbourhood shops and market stalls, sustained by steady clientele and long-term credit. The flat-price shock of cross-border direct mail first erodes precisely what they prized most — price advantage and neighbourhood trust. When a neighbour discovers cheaper goods online, the corner store's business suffers.

## Beyond business: reinventing the diaspora role

The significance reaches well beyond "some small merchants having a hard time." It touches a deeper question: as the homeland moves from exporting products to exporting channels, where does the traditional value of the overseas Chinese merchant lie?

Historically, that value was connection — linking Chinese capacity to local demand. Today platforms have automated and de-personalised that connection. If the merchant only moves goods and adds a markup, the space shrinks; but if they turn localised service, after-sales, trust, and community ties into something platforms cannot replace, they may yet find a place in the new order.

Indeed, some ethnic Chinese businesses are already pivoting — into curation, brand agency, localised content commerce, even selling Southeast Asian goods back to China. The role is changing, but the function of the "bridge" has not disappeared.

## The paradox: a stronger homeland, a harder life for its diaspora

What makes the report most thought-provoking is an economic paradox: the stronger China grows and the more aggressively it goes global, the harder life becomes for some ethnic Chinese peddlers in Southeast Asia. The dividend those communities once captured by virtue of proximity is now being run over by the homeland's own digital juggernaut.

For those who study the overseas Chinese, this is a topic worth tracking over the long term: the economic role of the qiaoshang is being reshaped, and the driving force is not the host country but the ancestral home across the strait. When "overseas Chinese" is no longer a natural channel advantage, the relationship between the diaspora and the motherland must be redefined.

## Further reading

**Cross-border e-commerce**: The sale of goods to overseas consumers via the internet, bypassing traditional importers and wholesalers — the core mechanism squeezing Southeast Asia's ethnic Chinese peddlers.

**Overseas Chinese merchant (qiaoshang)**: Commercial networks run by ethnic Chinese settled abroad, historically the middlemen and distribution nodes through which Chinese goods entered Southeast Asian markets.

**Migration to Nanyang (下南洋)**: The collective term for the large-scale migration of people from China's southeast coast to Southeast Asia in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, laying the demographic and commercial foundations of the region's Chinese business networks.
