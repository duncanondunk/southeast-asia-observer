# 关税墙重建：301条款把东南亚（Southeast Asia）六国放上同一张税率表

据《日经亚洲》（Nikkei Asia）报道，美国贸易代表办公室11月25日依据《1974年贸易法》第301条款，对14个经济体作出「结构性产能过剩」认定并提出加征关税方案。六个东南亚经济体同时上榜：越南（Vietnam）最高35%、新加坡最高20%，泰国（Thailand）、马来西亚（Malaysia）、印度尼西亚（Indonesia）与柬埔寨均为18%。这不是一场全新的贸易战，而是最高法院推翻「对等关税」之后，华盛顿换一根法律支柱把旧税率重新立起来。真正值得问的是：当「产能过剩」成为普遍标签，东南亚究竟是出口平台，还是被预先定价的「中国邻近产能」？

## 一张税率表，六个东南亚名字

11月25日的认定清单里，东南亚占了六席。除中国台湾地区、孟加拉国与印度外，越南、新加坡、泰国、马来西亚、印度尼西亚、柬埔寨都在其中。最初的调查在3月11日启动时覆盖16个经济体，墨西哥和挪威此轮暂未被认定。

美方给出的拟议税率并不整齐：越南最高35%，但若与美国达成「符合美方要求」的互惠贸易协定，可调整为20%；新加坡最高20%，达成协议则可考虑降至10%；泰国、马来西亚、印度尼西亚、柬埔寨和孟加拉国均为18%。中国为20%，印度最高25%（达成协议为17%），欧盟、日本、韩国、瑞士及中国台湾地区为15%。

这里有个容易被误读的技术细节。这些百分比是「扣除既有301关税后」的拟议税率，并非在现有关税上简单相加；部分商品被列入附件获得豁免，已受国家安全关税约束的钢铁、铝和汽车也不重复计征。税率表看着吓人，实际落到一个集装箱上，仍需看商品编码、原产地与谈判进度。据《日经亚洲》报道，这一点恰恰是美方留下弹性的地方——关税尚未生效，先亮出数字。

## 从最高法院到301条款：换法条不换税率

要理解这份方案，得先回到今年2月。美国最高法院以6比3裁定，总统不能依据国际紧急经济权力法（IEEPA）征收「对等关税」。此后华盛顿先用《贸易法》第122条实施为期150天的10%全球关税，再转向301条款重建体系。耶鲁预算实验室（Yale Budget Lab）11月26日测算：若新方案落地，美国平均关税率将从目前的11.2%升至13.9%，接近最高法院裁决前的13.8%。

换句话说，这不是从低税率走向高税率，而是把被判违法的税率换一个法律外壳搬回来。该实验室还估计，即便不计新方案，现行关税政策已使美国消费者价格水平最终上升约0.6%，相当于每个家庭每年多付约900美元（约6480元人民币）。关税的账单从来不是出口国单独承担，这一点在美国国内政治中越来越难回避。

值得玩味的是，曾被美方拉着一起批评别国「产能过剩」的盟友，这次也被同一把尺子量了。欧盟发言人明确拒绝「欧洲是结构性产能过剩根源」的前提；日本官房长官表示遗憾，并强调日本不存在结构性产能过剩。昨日之矛，今成今日之盾，这套话语的说服力由此可见。

## 越南与新加坡：两个浮动税率

六个东南亚名字里，越南和新加坡最特殊——它们拿到的是「区间价」。越南35%的拟议上限是14个经济体中最高的，但如果达成互惠贸易协定，税率可降到20%；新加坡从20%降到10%的通道同样写在文本里。

这不是优惠，而是标价。美方等于告诉两地：税率取决于你在谈判桌上愿意给什么。对越南而言，35%不是一个抽象数字，而是纺织服装、鞋类、造船、水泥和信息通信设备等被点名行业的报价单；对新加坡而言，被点名的是电子、半导体、医疗和石化，几乎覆盖其出口结构的核心。

浮动税率还改变了谈判的性质。过去谈的是市场准入和关税减让，现在谈的是「被认定后的减免」——起点是惩罚，终点是回到原先承诺的水平。这种结构让协议不再是互利的交换，而更像缴纳保险费换取不触发条款。

## 18%那一档：出口平台的共同困境

泰国、马来西亚、印度尼西亚和柬埔寨同处18%一档，表面上是同一待遇，实际痛点各不相同。

美方对泰国的报告点了电子、汽车、机械和电气设备，认定其产业与出口规划、市场准入壁垒、优惠贷款和劳工保护不足共同构成不公平做法。马来西亚被点名的是信息通信技术与汽车；印度尼西亚是纺织服装、矿产金属与可再生能源，并特别提到「下游化」政策；柬埔寨则是服装、太阳能和轮胎。

把这些行业放在一起看，图景就清楚了：它们正是东南亚过去十年承接产业转移、吸引外国直接投资的支柱。美方口中的「产能过剩」，在很多情况下就是东南亚引以为傲的工业化成果——工业园、优惠税制、基础设施配套、劳动力动员。当这些政策被统一翻译成「扭曲」，区域发展战略本身就被纳入了审查范围。

## 「产能过剩」如何写进法律

美方的逻辑链条并不复杂：产业政策、金融压制、劳工保护不足、非市场经济体投入，导致美国制造业岗位与投资流失，因而构成对美国商业的负担。贸易代表格里尔称，多国领导人多年来都承认产能过剩的威胁，只是声明没有带来改变。

问题在于，「产能过剩」从来是相对概念。对美国是过剩，对东南亚可能只是产能不足的补位；对某个行业是过剩，对下游用户却可能是降本的条件。把一种经济判断写进单边关税依据，再用它去要求别国修改产业政策，等于把美国的市场结构当作全球标准。中国商务部已明确表示产能过剩是伪命题，反对以此为借口搞政治操弄；这种分歧不是措辞之争，而是发展权之争。

更要紧的是举证责任。美方发布了国别报告，调查阶段收到约1070份书面意见、近140名证人作证，程序看似充分；但最终税率如何按商品调整、哪些产品进入豁免附件，仍由行政程序决定。程序完整不等于结论必然公允。

## 转口与原产地：被点名的第三国投入

这份方案里有一个对东南亚格外敏感的措辞：未能解决来自「非市场经济体」的进口与外国直接投资所造成的产能过剩。越南被明确列出鼓励或未能处理转口、来自其他非市场经济体的投资与进口；其他经济体报告中也反复出现同类表述。

这意味着美方审查的不再只是「谁出口」，而是「谁在里面」。一家在越南设厂的华商企业，即便注册地、纳税地和用工都在当地，仍可能因设备、原料或股东背景被贴上第三国投入的标签。对新加坡这样的转口枢纽，压力更大：货物是否「实际进入」该国，与最终用户是谁，本来就不是同一套记录。

原产地规则因此从技术性海关问题升级为战略问题。企业要面对的不只是税率高低，还有能否拿出经得起核验的成本构成、采购链条和增值比例证明。对长期依赖「中国零件、东盟（ASEAN）组装、美国销售」模式的厂商，这是一次被迫的账本重整。

## 从2027年1月5日开始的窗口

方案目前只是拟议措施。利益相关方须在2027年1月5日前提交书面意见和听证申请，听证会定于1月25日举行并视情况延续；最终税率与生效日期尚未确定，美方官员称最早明年1月，市场分析则多指向2月至3月。

这个窗口有两个含义。其一，税率不是既成事实，仍有游说与谈判空间，尤其对能承诺采购、投资或政策调整的经济体。其二，不确定性本身就是成本。订单周期长的电子与机械行业，可能在新税率落地前就推迟排产、重算库存和改签合同。关税还没收，供应链已经先动。

对东南亚政府而言，时间压力并不对称。越南和新加坡有明确的「降价通道」，其他国家拿到的更像固定报价。谁能在听证前拿出美方认可的承诺，谁就有机会把18%变成更低的数字；拿不出的，只能承受既成事实。

## 华商：在税率表之外做账的人

这场关税重估对华侨华人社群不是抽象议题。东南亚制造业中，华商长期扮演投资人、供应商和中间人的角色，许多工厂的边际利润本就单薄。18%与35%之间的差距，足以决定一条生产线留在本国还是再搬一次。

但把华商网络自动读成「转口通道」是危险的。华商之所以能扎根，靠的是本地雇员、本地税务与本地客户关系，而不是护照上的祖籍。美方若以族裔或血缘作为风险代理指标，不仅会误伤合规经营者，也会把最了解区域供应链的一群人推到对立面。对中国而言，更聪明的做法不是替华商喊冤，而是帮助他们把原产地、采购和财务记录做到可核验——合规能力本身就是竞争力。

反过来，华商也不应把新税率当作简单涨价的机会。若把关税成本转嫁给品牌方与消费者，最终削弱的是东南亚作为制造目的地的性价比。真正的护城河，是透明而非模糊。

## 三问定成色

第一，浮动税率会不会变成「让步换减免」的明码标价？若20%和10%成为谈判奖励，关税就不再是贸易救济，而是外交筹码。第二，豁免附件和原产地认定能否公开、可预期？如果企业只能靠猜测商品编码做投资决策，成本会长期高于税率本身。第三，东盟是集体回应还是各自谈？六个经济体被放在同一张表上，却拿到不同价格，这种差别待遇最考验区域凝聚力。

据《日经亚洲》报道，美方把这套方案描述为「恢复公平」。但从东南亚看，它更像把旧关税墙换了砖：墙高差不多，门却握在别人手里。短期看，这是定价问题；长期看，是东南亚能否证明自己的工业化不是任何大国的附属品。答案不在11月25日的文件里，而在2027年1月的听证席上，以及此后每一份报关单上。

## 延伸阅读

**关税墙（Tariff Wall）**：通过高关税系统性限制进口的保护主义安排，此处指美国在司法挫败后以新法律依据重建高税率体系。

**原产地规则（Rules of Origin）**：判定商品「国籍」的海关标准，决定适用哪档税率；在转口与第三国投资审查中尤为关键。

**结构性产能过剩（Structural Excess Capacity）**：美方此次调查的核心概念，指政府产业扶持被认为造成超出需求的制造能力，但其界定与举证长期存在争议。

# Rebuilding the tariff wall: what Section 301 means for Southeast Asia

Nikkei Asia reports that the Office of the United States Trade Representative determined on November 25, under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, that 14 economies engage in unreasonable practices related to "structural excess capacity" and proposed new tariffs. Six Southeast Asian economies appear on the same rate card: Vietnam up to 35 percent, Singapore up to 20 percent, and Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia and Cambodia at 18 percent. This is not a new trade war so much as the restoration, under a different legal pillar, of rates struck down by the Supreme Court. The question that matters is this: when "overcapacity" becomes a universal label, is Southeast Asia being treated as an export platform, or priced in advance as China-adjacent capacity?

## One rate card, six Southeast Asian names

The November 25 list gives Southeast Asia six seats. Alongside Taiwan, Bangladesh and India, Vietnam, Singapore, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia and Cambodia are all named. The investigations had begun on March 11 covering 16 economies; Mexico and Norway were not determined this time.

The proposed rates are far from uniform. Vietnam faces up to 35 percent, reducible to 20 percent if it reaches a reciprocal trade agreement "responsive" to US concerns. Singapore faces up to 20 percent, potentially adjustable to 10 percent under a deal. Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, Cambodia and Bangladesh are each at 18 percent. China is at 20 percent, India up to 25 percent (17 percent with an agreement), and the EU, Japan, Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan at 15 percent.

A technical point is easily misread. These percentages are net of existing Section 301 duties, not simple additions on top of them; some goods are exempted in annexes, and products already covered by national-security tariffs — steel, aluminium and automobiles — are not double-counted. The headline numbers are alarming, but what lands on a container depends on classification, origin and the state of negotiations. According to Nikkei Asia, that flexibility is precisely the point: Washington is showing the numbers before the tariffs take effect.

## From the Supreme Court to Section 301: new statute, same rates

To understand the proposal, start in February, when the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that the president could not impose "reciprocal tariffs" under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. Washington then used Section 122 of the Trade Act for a 150-day, 10 percent global tariff before turning to Section 301 to rebuild the system. The Yale Budget Lab estimated on November 26 that if the new plan takes effect, the average US tariff rate would rise from 11.2 percent to 13.9 percent — close to the 13.8 percent in force before the court ruling.

In other words, this is not a move from low tariffs to high tariffs. It is the old wall carried back in under a new legal shell. The lab also estimated that even without the new plan, current policies would ultimately raise US consumer prices by about 0.6 percent, costing roughly US$900 per household per year. The bill for tariffs has never been paid by exporters alone, and that is becoming harder to ignore in American domestic politics.

There is an irony here. Allies once enlisted by Washington to criticise others' "overcapacity" are now measured by the same ruler. The European Commission rejected the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity; Japan's chief cabinet secretary expressed regret and insisted Japan has no such problem. Yesterday's spear has become today's shield, which says something about the credibility of the framing.

## Vietnam and Singapore: two floating rates

Of the six Southeast Asian names, Vietnam and Singapore are special because they are given ranges. Vietnam's 35 percent ceiling is the highest among the 14 economies, but falls to 20 percent if a reciprocal trade agreement is reached. Singapore's 20 percent can be considered for reduction to 10 percent, also written into the text.

That is not a discount; it is a price tag. Washington is effectively telling both governments that the rate depends on what they bring to the table. For Vietnam, 35 percent is not an abstraction but a quotation for named sectors: textiles and apparel, footwear, shipbuilding, cement and information and communications technology. For Singapore, the named sectors are electronics, semiconductors, health and petrochemicals — close to the core of its export structure.

A floating rate changes the nature of bargaining. Talks used to be about market access and tariff cuts; now they are about relief from a finding of guilt. The starting point is punishment and the endpoint is a return to previously promised levels. Such a structure turns an agreement from a mutual exchange into something closer to an insurance premium against triggering a clause.

## The 18 percent tier: the export-platform dilemma

Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia and Cambodia sit together at 18 percent. The headline treatment looks identical, but the pressures differ.

The US report on Thailand names electronics, automobiles, machinery and electrical equipment, arguing that industrial planning, market-access barriers, preferential lending and inadequate labour protections combine to burden US commerce. Malaysia is named for information and communications technology and automobiles; Indonesia for textiles and apparel, mining and metals, and renewable energy, with "downstreaming" singled out; Cambodia for apparel, solar and tyres.

Put those sectors side by side and the picture sharpens. They are exactly the pillars of Southeast Asia's industrial upgrading over the past decade — the parks, incentive regimes, infrastructure packages and labour mobilisation that attracted foreign direct investment. What Washington calls "overcapacity" is, in many cases, the region's industrialisation success story. Once those policies are translated uniformly into "distortion", regional development strategy itself becomes subject to review.

## How "overcapacity" became a legal finding

The administration's chain of reasoning is simple enough: industrial policy, financial repression, weak labour protection and non-market-economy inputs erode US manufacturing output, jobs and investment, and therefore burden US commerce. Ambassador Jamieson Greer says leaders from Donald Trump to Xi Jinping have long recognised the threat, but statements at the G7, G20 and WTO have not changed behaviour.

The trouble is that "overcapacity" is a relational concept. What is surplus to the United States may be a necessary complement elsewhere; what is excess in one sector may be a cost reduction for downstream users. Writing an economic judgement into a unilateral tariff authority, then using it to demand changes in other countries' industrial policy, treats the US market structure as a global standard. China's Ministry of Commerce has called overcapacity a false proposition and rejected its political use. That disagreement is not about wording; it is about the right to develop.

The burden of proof matters just as much. The USTR published country reports and says it received about 1,070 comments and testimony from nearly 140 witnesses during the investigations. The process looks substantial. But how rates are adjusted by product, and which goods enter exemption annexes, remain administrative decisions. Procedural completeness does not guarantee a fair conclusion.

## Transshipment and origin: third-country inputs named

One phrase in the plan is especially sensitive in Southeast Asia: the failure to address overcapacity arising from imports and foreign direct investment from non-market economies. Vietnam is explicitly cited for encouraging, or failing to address, transshipment and investment and imports from other non-market economies. Similar language recurs across the other country reports.

This means Washington is no longer asking only who exports, but who is inside the factory. A Chinese-owned manufacturer in Vietnam may be registered, taxed and employing workers locally, yet still be tagged for third-country inputs because of equipment, materials or shareholders. For Singapore as a transshipment hub, the problem is larger: whether goods physically entered the country and who ultimately uses them are different records.

Rules of origin have therefore moved from a technical customs matter to a strategic one. Companies face not just the height of the tariff but whether they can produce verifiable cost structures, procurement trails and value-added ratios. For firms long accustomed to "Chinese components, ASEAN assembly, American sales", this is a forced rewrite of the ledger.

## The window opening on January 5, 2027

The plan is still proposed action. Interested parties must submit written comments and hearing requests by January 5, 2027; hearings are set for January 25 and may continue as needed. Final rates and effective dates are undecided. US officials have suggested January as the earliest, while market analysts mostly point to February or March.

That window has two implications. First, the rates are not yet facts; there is room for lobbying and negotiation, especially for economies able to offer purchases, investment or policy changes. Second, uncertainty itself is a cost. In electronics and machinery, where order cycles are long, firms may delay production schedules, recalculate inventory and rewrite contracts before a single new duty is collected. The supply chain moves before the tariff does.

For Southeast Asian governments, the time pressure is uneven. Vietnam and Singapore have explicit pathways to lower rates; the others have what looks like a fixed quote. Whoever produces commitments acceptable to Washington before the hearings can try to turn 18 percent into a smaller number. Whoever cannot will simply have to live with the outcome.

## Chinese businesses: doing the math outside the rate card

This tariff reassessment is not an abstract issue for overseas Chinese communities. Ethnic Chinese entrepreneurs have long been investors, suppliers and intermediaries in Southeast Asian manufacturing, and many plants operate on thin margins. The gap between 18 and 35 percent is enough to decide whether a production line stays put or moves again.

But reading diaspora networks automatically as transshipment channels is dangerous. Chinese businesses took root because of local employees, local taxes and local customer relationships, not because of ancestry on a passport. If Washington treats ethnicity or kinship as a risk proxy, it will not only hurt compliant operators; it will push the people who know regional supply chains best into opposition. For China, the smarter response is not to plead on their behalf but to help them make origin, procurement and financial records auditable — compliance capability is itself a competitive advantage.

Conversely, Chinese firms should not treat the new tariffs as a simple excuse to raise prices. Passing costs to brand owners and consumers ultimately weakens Southeast Asia's value proposition as a manufacturing destination. The real moat is transparency, not opacity.

## Three questions that will settle it

First, will the floating rates become an explicit price for concessions? If 20 and 10 percent become negotiated rewards, tariffs stop being trade remedies and become diplomatic leverage. Second, can exemption annexes and origin determinations be made public and predictable? If firms must guess tariff classifications to make investment decisions, the cost will exceed the rate itself over time. Third, will ASEAN respond collectively or negotiate separately? Six economies are placed on one table but given different prices, and that differential treatment is a real test of regional cohesion.

Nikkei Asia describes the plan as restoring fairness. From Southeast Asia, it looks more like the old wall rebuilt with different bricks: roughly the same height, but with the door held by someone else. In the short term this is a pricing problem. In the long term it is a test of whether Southeast Asia can prove that its industrialisation is not an adjunct of any great power. The answer is not in the November 25 document. It is in the hearing room in January 2027, and on every customs declaration after that.

## Further reading

**Tariff wall**: A system of high duties designed to restrict imports systematically; here, the rebuilding of high US rates under a new legal authority after a court setback.

**Rules of origin**: The customs test that determines a good's "nationality" and therefore its tariff rate; crucial when transshipment and third-country investment are under scrutiny.

**Structural excess capacity**: The concept at the centre of the US investigation — government support alleged to create manufacturing capacity beyond demand — whose definition and evidentiary basis remain contested.
