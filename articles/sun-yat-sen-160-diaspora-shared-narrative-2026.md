# 孙中山诞辰160周年：海外华侨为什么仍需要一个共同叙事

今年是孙中山先生诞辰160周年。中国侨网转载的一篇评论提出了一个看似简单、实则不易回答的问题：一百多年前，他凭什么让散居世界各地的华侨华人相信，中国的命运与自己有关？

这个问题值得认真对待。纪念活动年年都有，但真正有分量的纪念，不是把雕像再擦亮一遍，而是重问当年那个组织难题：一群被籍贯、语言、阶层和政治立场切开的人，怎样才愿意把彼此当成同一个事业的同伴？

## 纪念背后的真问题

那篇评论的作者戴錡长期在美国北加州参与侨团活动，他给出的答案很直接：孙中山讲明白了一个道理——中国的前途，关系到海外中国人自己的前途。1894年，兴中会在檀香山成立；1905年，中国同盟会在东京成立。把这两个地点放进同一张地图，就能看出早期革命的组织重心从来不在国内的政治中心，而在海外。

为什么是海外？评论的解读是，早年漂洋过海的中国人经商、做工、办社团，屡遭歧视与排斥，比许多人更早懂得：一个国家的强弱，会直接写成一个普通人在海外的尊严。这话并不新鲜，但放在今天仍有锋芒。很多人谈侨务，第一反应是汇款、投资、人才引进；可如果侨胞只被当成可以提取的资源，关系的存续就全靠行情。孙中山当年做的，恰恰不是提取，而是连接。

## 从檀香山到南洋：信任的地理学

不同海外节点在革命中承担了不同角色。檀香山是组织起点，日本是思想与人物汇聚之地，北美有既存多年的传统华人社团。而南洋的位置尤其特殊：华侨众多、商业网络成熟、离中国又近，既能筹款，也能办报、发展组织，还能为国内起义提供支持。

这里有个容易被忽略的细节。孙中山并没有把旧式会馆、同乡组织统统推倒重来，而是借助已有的人脉、信用与义气，把原本用于同乡互助、生活自保的组织，慢慢转成服务于“振兴中华”的力量。说白了，革命不是从零搭建一套新机构，而是给旧网络换了一个更大的目标。这一点放到今天依然成立：侨社的动员能力，从来不取决于口号有多响，而取决于既有信任能不能被善用。反过来，若把侨社当成空白画布随意涂写，投入再多，也画不出稳定的共同体。

## “革命之母”不能只算钱

“华侨为革命之母”是常被引用的一句话。评论作者提醒，不能把它窄化成华侨捐了多少钱。商号与住宅成为活动场所，一家人的生活与革命连在一起，这些都不是开支票能解释的。真正的变化是身份感的改写：许多人不再只认自己是某个县、某个省的人，而开始以“中国人”来理解自己。

但也要看到，海外华人社会从来不是意见一致的社会。籍贯不同、经历不同、政治观念不同，争论有时相当激烈。孙中山的本事，不是消灭分歧，而是在民族共同利益面前找到最大公约数。作者以自己1996年在旧金山参与保钓活动的经历作证：平日里各执一词的人，在共同议题面前可以先把分歧放下。这个例子比任何宏大叙事都更能说明问题——共识不是天然存在的，它是在一次次具体协作中被验证出来的。

## 三道考题，第三道最难

评论为今日海外华侨华人列出三件事：维护民族共同利益，珍惜两岸和平，以文化重新连接被政治分开的人。前两件听起来堂堂正正，做起来并不轻松；最难的是第三件。

维护共同利益，牵涉主权与尊严，立场问题相对清楚；珍惜和平，则要求把普通人的安稳生活放在口水输赢之上，尤其涉及中国台湾问题，容不得激情用事。文化这一项却没有捷径。一首歌、一场演出、一次节庆、一段共同历史，确实比政治语言更容易让人理解彼此，但前提是这些东西必须真实、自然、由社群自己愿意传承。文化如果被当作宣传的替身，就会失去它最宝贵的性质：让人自愿走近，而不是被要求表态。这算得上一条硬标准，华侨华人工作做得多热闹，最终都要回到这里检验。

## 东南亚该读出什么

对东南亚华人社会而言，这篇评论的意义不在于提醒他们纪念谁，而在于重新确认他们在历史中的位置。南洋不是革命的边角料，而是组织、资金、舆论与人员流动的关键节点。会馆、华校、华文报纸和侨批网络，构成了一套跨越海洋的民间基础设施。今天谈中国与东盟的人员往来、华文教育和华商网络，使用的仍是这套基础设施的当代版本。

问题在于，连接方式会老化。老一代侨胞的社团经验无法自动传给新生代；华校如果只剩语言技能而失去社区依托，也很难承担文化记忆的传递；侨批档案若能数字化却无人讲述其背后的家庭史，就只是漂亮的图片。东南亚华人需要的不是被反复动员，而是被认真支持：支持他们办好自己的学校、社团、媒体与学术机构，允许他们同时做忠诚的当地公民和文化上的中国人。这两件事并不冲突，把它们对立起来，才是真正的误判。

## 从情绪到责任：纪念的落点

“天下为公”与“振兴中华”放在一起读，含义很清楚：一个强大、自信、有文化根基的中国，不是为了与世界拉开距离，而是更有能力与世界和平相处。评论作者说得好，海外华侨华人长久的力量是文化、是信任，是能不能成为中国与世界之间的一座桥。爱国不能只是一句口号，也不能只是一种情绪，它应该是责任、理性与行动。

孙中山留下的那几个问题今天仍在：人在海外，文化的根在哪里；面对分歧，是放大它还是寻找共同点；中国与世界之间出现误解时，是旁观还是出面沟通。160周年最好的纪念，不是把答案背下来，而是让每一代侨胞用自己的方式重新回答一遍。回答得诚实，这座桥才立得住。

## 延伸阅读

**南洋（Nanyang）**：中文语境中对东南亚的历史称谓，也是近代华侨迁徙、经商与革命活动的地理概念。

**侨社（overseas Chinese community）**：由会馆、宗亲会、学校、媒体等构成的海外华人组织网络，其信任结构往往比单一事件更持久。

**振兴中华（rejuvenation of China）**：孙中山提出的历史性口号，曾为分散的海外华人提供超越籍贯与阶层的共同目标。

# Sun Yat-sen at 160: why the diaspora still needs a shared story

This year marks the 160th anniversary of Sun Yat-sen's birth. A commentary carried by China Qiao Wang poses a seemingly simple question that is anything but: a century ago, what convinced overseas Chinese scattered across the world that China's fate was their own?

The question deserves to be taken seriously. Commemorations happen every year, but the ones that matter do more than polish a statue. They return to the original organisational puzzle: how do people divided by native place, language, class and political conviction come to regard one another as partners in a common undertaking?

## The real question behind the commemoration

The commentary's author, Dai Qi, has long been active in Chinese community organisations in northern California. His answer is blunt: Sun made one proposition clear — the future of China was bound up with the future of Chinese people abroad. The Revive China Society was founded in Honolulu in 1894; the Tongmenghui, or Alliance Society, followed in Tokyo in 1905. Put the two places on the same map and it becomes evident that the early revolutionary movement was organised not at China's political centre but beyond its borders.

Why abroad? Dai's reading is that early emigrants — traders, labourers, organisers of associations — met discrimination and exclusion overseas, and understood earlier than many at home that the strength of a country is written into the dignity of an ordinary Chinese person abroad. That is not a new observation, but it still bites. Discuss diaspora work today and the reflex is to count remittances, investment and talent. Yet when overseas Chinese are treated mainly as a resource to extract, the relationship lasts only as long as the returns do. What Sun built was not extraction but connection.

## From Honolulu to Nanyang: a geography of trust

Different overseas nodes served different functions. Honolulu provided the first organisational base; Japan gathered ideas and activists; North America already had long-established Chinese associations. Nanyang — the historical Chinese term for Southeast Asia — mattered most perhaps because it combined dense Chinese settlement, mature commercial networks and proximity to China. It could raise money, run newspapers, build organisations and support uprisings at home.

There is an easily missed detail here. Sun did not sweep away the old clan halls and native-place associations and start from scratch. He worked through existing ties, credit and obligations of loyalty, gradually turning bodies built for mutual aid and everyday survival towards the larger goal of "rejuvenating China". In other words, the revolution did not invent new institutions; it gave old networks a bigger purpose. The same logic holds today. A community's capacity to act is never a function of how loud its slogans are, but of whether existing trust can be put to use. Treat a diaspora community as a blank canvas and no amount of effort will produce a durable commonwealth.

## The "mother of the revolution" was more than a chequebook

"Overseas Chinese were the mother of the revolution" is a much-quoted line. Dai warns against reducing it to the sum of their donations. Shops and homes became meeting places; entire households tied their daily lives to the revolutionary cause. None of that is explained by writing a cheque. What changed was the sense of self: people who had identified first with a county or a province began to understand themselves as Chinese.

Yet overseas Chinese society has never been of one mind. Differences of origin, experience and political conviction can produce fierce argument. Sun's skill was not to abolish disagreement but to find the widest common ground when a shared national interest was at stake. Dai cites his own role in the 1996 San Francisco mobilisation over the Diaoyu Islands: people who normally disagreed on almost everything set their differences aside for a common cause. That example does more work than any grand narrative. Consensus is not a natural endowment; it is verified through concrete cooperation, again and again.

## Three tasks, and the hardest is the third

Dai sets out three tasks for overseas Chinese today: defend shared national interests, cherish peace across the Taiwan Strait, and use culture to reconnect people divided by politics. The first two sound unimpeachable and are not easy to carry out; the third is the hardest.

Defending common interests involves sovereignty and dignity, where the line is relatively clear. Cherishing peace demands that ordinary people's security matter more than winning an argument — particularly on issues concerning Taiwan, China, where passion is a poor counsellor. Culture offers no shortcut. A song, a performance, a festival or a shared history can indeed communicate what political language cannot, but only if these things are authentic and sustained by the community itself. When culture is made to stand in for propaganda it loses its most valuable property: the ability to draw people in voluntarily rather than demand a declaration of loyalty. That is a hard test, and however busy diaspora work appears, it ultimately has to meet it.

## What Southeast Asia should read into it

For Southeast Asian Chinese communities, the commentary matters less as a reminder of whom to commemorate than as a restoration of where they stand in history. Nanyang was not a footnote to the revolution; it was a critical node of organisation, finance, public opinion and movement. Clan halls, Chinese schools, Chinese-language newspapers and the qiaopi remittance-letter network formed a civil infrastructure across the sea. When China–ASEAN exchanges, Chinese-language education and overseas Chinese business networks are discussed today, it is this infrastructure's contemporary version that is being used.

The trouble is that connections age. The organisational experience of an older generation does not transfer automatically to a younger one. A Chinese school that teaches only language skills without a community anchor can hardly carry cultural memory. Qiaopi archives that are digitised but never narrated become handsome pictures rather than family history. What Southeast Asian Chinese need is not repeated mobilisation but sustained support: support for their schools, associations, media and academic institutions, and room to be both loyal local citizens and culturally Chinese. The two are not in conflict. Treating them as a zero-sum choice is the real miscalculation.

## From sentiment to responsibility

Read together, "a public world for all" and "rejuvenation of China" point in a clear direction: a strong, confident China with cultural roots is not drawing away from the world but is better able to live peaceably within it. Dai's line is well chosen — the durable strength of overseas Chinese lies in culture, in trust, in whether they can be a bridge between China and the world. Patriotism cannot be a slogan or a passing emotion; it has to be responsibility, reason and action.

Sun's questions remain: where do the roots lie when a life is lived abroad; when differences arise, do we magnify them or look for common ground; when misunderstanding opens between China and the world, do we stand aside or try to explain? The best way to mark 160 years is not to memorise the answers but to let each generation answer afresh. Only honest answers make the bridge stand.

## Further reading

**Nanyang (南洋)**: the historical Chinese name for Southeast Asia, and a geographic concept tied to modern Chinese migration, commerce and revolutionary activity.

**Overseas Chinese community (侨社)**: the network of clan halls, associations, schools and media abroad, whose structures of trust outlast any single event.

**Rejuvenation of China (振兴中华)**: Sun Yat-sen's historic rallying call, which offered dispersed Chinese an identity beyond county, province and class.
