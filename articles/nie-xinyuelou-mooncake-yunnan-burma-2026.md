# 一枚月饼，载着缅籍华人家族四代人的团圆回乡路

## 一块跨越国界的团圆滋味

每年中秋，全球华侨华人都会拿起一枚月饼。但在滇缅边境，有一种月饼讲述的故事，是海外华人生活的常规叙事常常忽略的：一家诞生于异乡、在异国打磨成型、如今又归返故土的家庭生意。2026年9月25日，中新社从云南瑞丽沿边产业园畹町片区发回报道，新月楼饼庄的第四代传承人正对着直播镜头，讲述一款近七十年来以这样或那样的方式跨越国界的月饼。

## 滇缅融合风味：云腿、缅甸蜂蜜与坚果

新月楼的招牌，是一款“滇缅融合风味”月饼。它的灵魂是云南传统的云腿月饼——以酥皮裹腌火腿的内馅经典；而配方里又揉进了缅甸蜂蜜与当地坚果。正如报道所言，这种甜咸交织的口感“成为备受中缅食客喜爱的风味”。这并非为融合而融合。它其实是一个夹在两个国家之间生活的社群的味觉沉淀：火腿指向祖辈的云南来处，蜂蜜与坚果则指向这个家族真正安身立命的那片土地。细读之下，这块月饼是一份小小的文献，记录了离散身份是如何被组装起来的——不是靠抽象的理念，而是靠人们选择端上餐桌的东西。

## 从腊戍到畹町：四代人的回归

创始人聂子敬，祖籍云南保山，1958年在缅甸腊戍创立新月楼。这段渊源颇能说明问题：他身在异乡，思念故土的月饼，便动手试制。当地华人邻里一同帮忙，调试配方、攻克饼型难定型、饼皮易渗油等早期难题。最终成型的是双馅、大尺寸的特色月饼，成了缅甸华人大家庭团聚桌上的佳品。2025年，第四代传承人聂佳佳带着祖辈的心愿，将品牌带回中缅边境的云南瑞丽畹町，新建工厂。她告诉记者，漂泊在外的华侨华人，总有回到故乡的念想。我们不必过快地把这层情感浪漫化：在云南边境产业园设厂，本身也是对内地开放与“边境经济区”政策的一次精明下注。但情感的语法是真实的，而恰恰是这一部分，是干巴巴的贸易统计永远捕捉不到的。

## 月饼如何成为离散社群的共同记忆

这里有一个值得驻足的细节。数十年来，新月楼的月饼经由边民互市等方式进入云南，成为报道中所说的“缅甸华人社群与中缅边境民众中秋团圆的共同印记”。请想想这意味着什么：一款在外国生产的食物，竟也成为边界这一侧人们过节的一部分。换言之，即便在人员跨境日益困难的年代，边界对文化却是渗透的。在一个各国比以往更严密地把守边境的时代，一种共同滋味的绵延，是对“国界线能干净利落地切割文化”这一观念的安静反驳。月饼做到了外交有时做不到的事：它让一个社群对自己保持可辨识。

## 直播间、缅籍员工与全年产线的生意经

这门生意，也是观察边境经济如何真实运转的一扇小窗。自今年9月上线直播带货以来，聂记月饼单日最高销售额突破5万元人民币；绝对数字虽不大，但对一个试探数字化渠道的老字号而言，是颇具意义的信号。其11个口味中，已有8种在畹町实现生产；普洱、临沧、西双版纳等云南沿边州市乃至贵州，都有顾客专程前来订购。畹町工厂里，深耕月饼手艺多年的缅籍员工接近百名；今年中秋，传承人将月饼送给中缅员工及周遭工厂的缅籍工友。关键的是，畹町的四条月饼生产线正从季节性生产向全年生产转型，国庆之后还将增设蛋糕生产线，新品亦将同步供应缅甸市场。缅甸方面的生产仍在持续，产品销往泰国曼谷、清迈和马来西亚等海外华人聚居地。这不是被供进博物馆的怀旧，而是一条情感与物流本就是同一件事的跨境供应链。

## 一枚小故事，一个大图案

把视角拉远，图案便清晰起来。新月楼的回归，是云南与中南半岛边境被重新编织这一更大进程的其中一个节点——与“侨乡”工程、跨境旅游走廊、澜湄合作框架背后的动力同源。令人瞩目的是，这一重新编织中最柔软、最私密的一层——一个家族的味觉记忆——或许比那些硬基础设施更为持久。昆明与北京的决策者谈论“边境经济区”与“命运共同体”；而畹町的一位第四代传承人，正以一枚枚月饼的方式身体力行。风险当然在于，“侨味”被压扁成一场品牌营销。但眼下，正如传承人坚持的那样，承载中缅食客共同记忆的月饼内核，始终未变。

## 延伸阅读

**离散群体（Diaspora）**：东南亚的华侨华人社群，以及食物、语言与节庆实践如何在国境之上维系跨地域的身份认同。

**边民互市（Border household trade）**：中国边境沿线的小额跨境交换体系，既是经济通道，也充当着文化传导的媒介。

**缅甸腊戍华人社区（Lashio Chinese communities）**：上缅甸历史悠久的华人聚居地，长期以亲缘、商贸与饮食与云南相连。

# A Border Town's Mooncake Carries a Burmese-Chinese Family's Four Generations Home

## A taste of reunion that crosses borders

Every Mid-Autumn Festival, the Chinese diaspora reaches for a mooncake. But along the Yunnan–Burma border, one particular pastry tells a story that the standard accounts of overseas Chinese life tend to miss: the story of a family business born in exile, perfected in a foreign land, and now returned home. On 25 September 2026, China News Service reported from Wanding, a border-industry park in Ruili, Yunnan, where the fourth-generation heir of the Xinyuelou (New Moon Tower) bakery was live-streaming mooncakes that have, one way or another, crossed the frontier for nearly seventy years.

## The Yunnan–Burma fusion on a single plate

The signature product is a "Yunnan–Burma fusion" mooncake. Its soul is the Yunnan-style cured-ham (yun-tui) mooncake — the flaky, savoury classic from the hinterland — but the recipe folds in Myanmar honey and local nuts. The result, as the report puts it, is "a flavour loved by both Chinese and Burmese diners." This is not fusion for its own sake. It is the culinary residue of a community that lived between two countries: the ham speaks to the ancestors' Yunnan origin, the honey and nuts to the land where the family actually built its livelihood. Read closely, the mooncake is a small document of how diaspora identity is assembled — not from abstract ideas, but from what people chose to put on the table.

## From Lashio to Wanding: four generations returning

The founder, Nie Zijing, a Burmese Chinese from Baoshan, Yunnan, opened Xinyuelou in Lashio in 1958. The legend is telling: he missed the mooncakes of his homeland, so he tried his hand at making them. Neighbours in the local Chinese community helped troubleshoot the early failures — the dough that would not hold its shape, the pastry that leaked oil. What emerged was a distinctive double-filling, oversized mooncake that became a staple of Burmese-Chinese family reunions. In 2025, Nie Jiajia, the fourth-generation heir, fulfilled her ancestors' wish and brought the brand back across the border to Wanding, building a new factory. She told reporters that those who drift abroad never lose the longing to return. One should not romanticise this too quickly: a factory in a Yunnan border park is also a calculated bet on China's inland openness and its "border economic zone" policy. But the emotional grammar is real, and it is precisely the part that dry trade statistics never capture.

## How a pastry became a shared diaspora memory

Here is the detail worth pausing on. For decades, Xinyuelou's mooncakes reached Yunnan through border household trade and became, in the report's words, "a common imprint of Mid-Autumn reunion for both the Burmese-Chinese community and the border populace of China and Myanmar." Think about what that means. A food produced in a foreign country became part of how people on this side of the border also marked the festival. The border, in other words, was porous to culture even when it was hard to cross in person. In an era when states police frontiers more tightly than ever, the persistence of a shared taste is a quiet rebuttal to the idea that national boundaries cleanly sort cultures. The mooncake did what diplomacy sometimes cannot: it kept a community legible to itself.

## Live-streaming, Myanmar workers, and the year-round wager

The business is also a small window into how border economies actually work. Since launching live-stream sales in September, Xinyuelou has recorded a single-day peak of more than 50,000 yuan (roughly US$7,000) — modest in absolute terms, but a meaningful signal for a heritage brand testing digital channels. Of its eleven flavours, eight are now produced in Wanding; customers travel from Pu'er, Lincang, Xishuangbanna and even Guizhou to order. The Wanding plant employs nearly a hundred Myanmar workers, many long practised in the craft; this Mid-Autumn, the heir shared mooncakes with them and with workers at neighbouring factories. Crucially, the four Wanding lines are shifting from seasonal to year-round production, with a cake line to follow after the National Day holiday, and new products will also supply the Myanmar market. Production in Myanmar continues, and products are shipped to Chinese communities in Bangkok, Chiang Mai and Malaysia. This is not nostalgia kept in a museum; it is a cross-border supply chain in which sentiment and logistics are the same thing.

## A small story, a large pattern

Scale it up and the pattern is clear. The return of Xinyuelou is one node in a much larger reweaving of Yunnan's border with mainland Southeast Asia — the same dynamic behind the "Qiaoxiang" (ancestral-home) programmes, the cross-border tourism corridors, and the Lancang–Mekong cooperation framework. What is striking is that the softest, most intimate layer of that reweaving — a family's taste memory — may prove more durable than the hard infrastructure. Policymakers in Kunming and Beijing talk about "border economic zones" and a "community of shared future"; a fourth-generation heir in Wanding is living it, one mooncake at a time. The risk, of course, is that the "Qiao flavour" gets flattened into a branding exercise. But for now, as the heir insists, the kernel that carries the shared memory of Chinese and Burmese diners remains unchanged.
