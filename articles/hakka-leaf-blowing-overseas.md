# 一片绿叶吹向世界：福建客家树叶吹奏技艺的"出海"之路

8月12日晚，邱少春带领的21人福建树叶吹奏代表队从韩国首尔返抵福州。这支年龄跨度从12岁到65岁的队伍，刚在"2026世界叶笛节"上揽获多项大奖。一片来自闽西山野的榕树叶，如何吹响国际舞台？答案藏在千年的客家乡音与当代的文化自信之中。

## 从山野到舞台：一门千年技艺的当代亮相

树叶吹奏（树叶吹奏）在中国已有上千年历史，是闽西（west Fujian）最具特色的客家（Hakka）传统技艺之一。这门技艺向外传播后，在日本、韩国等地被当地民众称作"草笛"（grass flute）。邱少春是福建省歌舞剧院一级演奏员、福建省级非物质文化遗产代表性项目树叶吹奏技艺的省级代表性传承人。此次赴韩，他带领21人的队伍，用密封保鲜盒从福州、厦门两地精心保存了逾两百张新鲜榕树叶带往首尔，在舞台上接连奏响《家乡有座风雨桥》《客家山歌传万代》《月光光》等饱含乡土情感的曲目。

## 代际接力：用年龄跨度证明传承梯队

参赛选手的年龄从12岁覆盖到65岁，老、中、青、少四个年龄段齐整登场。邱少春表示，这样的代际搭配，是为了向世界展现这项千年非遗在福建的完整传承梯队，凸显其"代代接续的鲜活生命力"。一支队伍的结构本身，就是一封写给未来的传承宣言。

## 小众非遗的"出海"考题

邱少春直言，树叶吹奏作为相对小众的文化，在中国国内的传承发展中也遇到不少现实困难，因此他格外看重这次"出海"交流的机会。在他看来，登上海外赛场，既是对技艺最好的考验，也是面向世界最生动的一次非遗宣传。随团选手邱子樱说得朴素而恳切："对我们来说，最重要的是让来自中国的绿叶在国际舞台吹响，让听众理解藏在音符里的乡土情感。"

## 一面镜子：日韩同行的共同困境

值得玩味的是，邱少春在观察中还提到，如今日韩两地的树叶吹奏传承，正面临老艺人难出新作、年轻群体不愿接班的共同困境。这恰恰成为中国传承者需要提前警醒、做好长远规划的重要参照——非遗的生机，终究要靠一代代人把叶子接过去。

## 从一片叶子看文化"出海"的深层逻辑

跳出一场比赛，民间技艺的"出海"远不止于表演本身。当一片榕树叶在首尔响起，它承载的是中华文化以自身语汇与世界对话的尝试。对海外华侨华人（overseas Chinese）社群而言，这类乡土艺术既是连接原乡的情感纽带，也是在不同社会中自我表述的文化资本。一片绿叶，由此成为文化软实力（cultural soft power）中一个微小却真切的注脚。

## 延伸阅读
**客家（Hakka）**：中国汉族的重要民系之一，历史上因迁徙形成以粤东、闽西、赣南为中心，并广泛分布于东南亚的离散族群。
**非物质文化遗产（Intangible cultural heritage）**：以口头传统、表演艺术、手工技艺等为代表、靠代际传承维系的文化实践，需主动保护以免断层。
**草笛（Leaf flute / grass flute）**：树叶吹奏在日韩等地的别称，说明该技艺曾作为东亚共有的民间音乐传统向外传播。

# A Single Leaf, Blown to the World: The "Going Global" of Fujian Hakka Leaf-Playing

On the evening of August 12, the 21-strong Fujian leaf-playing delegation led by Qiu Shaochun returned to Fuzhou from Seoul. With members aged 12 to 65, the team had just won multiple awards at the 2026 World Leaf Flute Festival. How did a banyan leaf from the hills of west Fujian (闽西) sound on an international stage? The answer lies in a millennium of Hakka (客家) village melodies and a contemporary cultural confidence.

## From hillside to stage: a thousand-year art on present display

Leaf-playing (树叶吹奏) has a history of over a thousand years in China and is among the most distinctive Hakka traditions of west Fujian. In Japan and Korea it is known as the "grass flute" (草笛). Qiu Shaochun, a first-grade performer at the Fujian Provincial Song and Dance Theatre and provincial representative inheritor of the art, led a 21-member team carrying more than two hundred fresh banyan leaves from Fuzhou and Xiamen in sealed containers. On the Seoul stage they performed pieces such as "There Is a Wind-and-Rain Bridge in My Hometown," "Hakka Mountain Songs for Generations," and "Moonlight."

## A generational baton: proving the inheritance ladder

The participants spanned ages 12 to 65 across four generations — a deliberate choice to show the art's complete transmission ladder. Qiu emphasised that such cross-generational pairing reveals the "living vitality" of uninterrupted succession. The composition of a single troupe, in itself, is a declaration of continuity addressed to the future.

## The "going global" test for a niche heritage

Qiu noted plainly that, as a relatively niche culture, leaf-playing faces real difficulties in domestic transmission, which is precisely why he values this overseas exchange. The foreign stage is both the best trial of the craft and the most vivid promotion of intangible heritage to the world. Fellow performer Qiu Ziying put it simply yet earnestly: "The most important thing is to let Chinese green leaves sound on the international stage, and let listeners understand the native-land emotion carried in the notes."

## A mirror: the shared predicament of Japan and Korea

Tellingly, Qiu observed that leaf-playing in Japan and Korea faces the same troubles — aging artists who struggle to produce new works, and youth reluctant to take over. He sees this as an early warning for China's own long-term planning of inheritance: the vitality of intangible heritage ultimately depends on one generation after another picking up the leaf.

## The deeper logic of cultural "going global"

Beyond a single competition, the overseas journey of folk art is far more than performance. When a banyan leaf sounds in Seoul, it carries an attempt by Chinese culture to converse with the world in its own idiom. For overseas Chinese communities, such vernacular art is both an emotional bond with the ancestral homeland and cultural capital for self-representation in host societies. A single leaf thus becomes a small but telling instrument of cultural soft power.

## Further reading
**Hakka**: A major branch of the Han Chinese who, through historical migration, formed communities centred on eastern Guangdong, west Fujian and southern Jiangxi, with a wide diaspora across Southeast Asia.
**Intangible cultural heritage**: Cultural practices — oral traditions, performing arts, craft skills — sustained through generational transmission and requiring active protection against rupture.
**Grass flute (leaf flute)**: The name given to leaf-playing in Japan and Korea, evidence that the art once spread as a shared East Asian folk-music tradition.
