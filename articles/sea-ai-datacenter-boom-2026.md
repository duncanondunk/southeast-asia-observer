# 东南亚AI数据中心淘金热：正在重写区域科技版图

过去两年，东南亚已悄然成为全球增长最快的人工智能算力基础设施竞技场之一。据《日经亚洲》援引的市场情报，仅马来西亚一国如今就占了五个主要东南亚市场所有在建数据中心容量的逾一半；而与新加坡一水之隔的柔佛州，更从早年的"溢出市场"成长为区域人工智能工作负载的首要扩容地。数字令人咋舌：印尼、马来西亚、新加坡、泰国、越南与菲律宾目前合计运营超过两千座数据中心，另有数百座在建、逾千座处于规划阶段。这绝非一则房地产新闻，而是一场地缘政治叙事。

## 新加坡的选择：先约束，再统御

新加坡确立了区域先发优势，但土地与电力的稀缺迫使它按下暂停键：事实上的新建数据中心禁令从2019年延续至2022年；即便重新开放，城邦在最新一轮配额中也仅放出约200兆瓦新增容量，且附带严苛的能效门槛——能源使用效率须低于1.25。结果是有意为之的。新加坡不再试图承载最重的AI训练负载，而是把自己定位为区域的战略指挥节点：区域总部、对时延敏感的运用，以及一套东盟其余国家纷纷参照的成熟监管制度。微软2026年初承诺投入55亿美元（约390亿元人民币），亚马逊云科技早前承诺90亿美元（约639亿元人民币），都印证了这一逻辑：投资控制与编排，而非堆砌原始兆瓦。

## 马来西亚的跃升：溢出效应变成国家战略

一墙之隔的柔佛承接了新加坡容纳不下的产能。驱动因素平淡却关键——充足土地、较低电价，以及紧邻新加坡的网络互联骨干。马来西亚把地理上的偶然变成了产业政策。2026年2月，总理确认冻结所有与人工智能无关的新数据中心审批；2026年预算更拨出约4.9亿美元（约33亿元人民币），即21亿马来西亚林吉特（约33亿元人民币），用于主权人工智能云，旨在把训练数据与模型权重留在国境之内。马来西亚整体开发管道估计约3.4吉瓦，约占区域全部规划容量的六成。YTL电力获英伟达背书的液冷园区、AirTrunk的超大规模设施，以及微软规划的云区域，全部聚集在同一道柔佛走廊上。

## 第二条赛道：主权人工智能云

最耐人寻味的转变是结构性的。这一轮建设已裂分为两条平行赛道：一边是美国巨头（亚马逊云科技、微软、谷歌、甲骨文——合计承诺到2028年在区域云资本开支超过180亿美元，约1280亿元人民币）的超大规模园区；另一边是各国主权人工智能云。印尼在2026年投运首座主权人工智能数据中心；马来西亚把主权拨款以规模写入国家预算；新加坡则推出全球首个"智能体人工智能框架"。"谁的算力"与"谁的主权"之间的界线，如今已成为区域性的政策议题，而非供应商偏好。地缘政治利害在此变得直白：谁掌控模型、权重与数据，与谁浇筑混凝土机房的利害同样重大。

## 中美技术栈如今并肩运行

东南亚是少数美国与中国技术栈直接相邻运行的地区之一。在泰国，字节跳动已承诺在东部经济走廊投入88亿美元（约625亿元人民币），华为、阿里巴巴与腾讯也在与西方运营商并肩扩张。中国云服务商同样在马来西亚与印尼加码。这种共存并非偶然，它折射出区域刻意的不结盟姿态，以及借力博弈的胃口。但这也意味着，基础设施层正悄然成为美中科技竞争的静默前线。区域各国正在学会套利这场角力——两边都请进来，两边都不绑死，规则（数据本地化、可信走廊）由自己书写。

## 真正的瓶颈是电力，不是土地

然而热潮撞上了物理极限。据国际能源署估算，单座人工智能数据中心的耗电量可抵十万户家庭。马来西亚约81%的电力来自化石燃料，可再生能源占比仅约2%。为喂养这股热潮，吉隆坡计划到2030年新增多达8吉瓦燃气发电容量，并重启核电计划、锁定2031年投产目标。印尼以煤电为主、可再生审批缓慢的电网承受着类似压力。2026年2月，马来西亚甚至出现了首次数据中心抗议——柔佛州振林山（Gelang Patah）居民因扬尘、污染与用水问题集会。脱碳承诺与数据中心雄心，眼下正驶入一条尚未被任何人彻底化解的碰撞轨道。

## 这对区域意味着什么

三点判断随之而来。其一，东南亚不再只是世界的工厂车间，它正成为算力车间——人工智能时代无形基础设施被物理落地的场所。其二，美中角力正在本地化：主权而非仅制裁，成为新竞技场，东盟国家正在亲手撰写规则手册。其三，能源账单将最终限定这场淘金热能跑多远。区域的数据中心故事，归根结底是关于"电力、地缘与战略"的故事，以及由谁来决定这笔电力如何被花费。

## 延伸阅读

**主权人工智能云（Sovereign AI Cloud）**：把训练数据、模型与权重留在国境之内的国家级人工智能基础设施，将"超大规模厂商算力"与"国家算力"区分开来。

**柔佛走廊（Johor Corridor）**：马来西亚柔佛州紧邻新加坡的数据中心集群，如今是区域人工智能工作负载最大的扩容地。

**能源使用效率（Power Usage Effectiveness, PUE）**：衡量数据中心能效的指标；新加坡要求新建设施须低于1.25。

# Southeast Asia's AI data-center gold rush is redrawing the region's tech map

Over the past two years Southeast Asia has quietly become one of the world's fastest-growing arenas for AI computing infrastructure. According to market intelligence cited by Nikkei Asia, Malaysia alone now accounts for over half of all data-center capacity under construction across five key Southeast Asian markets, and Johor — a state just across the causeway from Singapore — has grown from a spillover market into the region's primary scale-out location for AI workloads. The numbers are staggering: more than 2,000 operational data centers now sit across Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam and the Philippines, with hundreds more under construction and over a thousand in planning. This is not merely a real-estate story. It is a geopolitical one.

## Singapore's choice: constrain, then command

Singapore built the region's first-mover advantage. But land and power scarcity forced a hard pause: a de facto moratorium on new data-center developments ran from 2019 to 2022, and even after reopening, the city-state released only about 200 megawatts of additional capacity in its latest allocation round, with strict efficiency rules — power usage effectiveness below 1.25. The result is deliberate. Singapore is no longer trying to host the heaviest AI training loads. Instead it positions itself as the region's strategic command node: home to regional headquarters, latency-sensitive applications, and a mature regulatory regime that the rest of ASEAN now references. Microsoft's US$5.5 billion commitment in early 2026 and Amazon Web Services' earlier US$9 billion pledge confirm the logic: invest in control and orchestration, not in raw megawatts.

## Malaysia's surge: spillover turned state strategy

Just across the border, Johor absorbed what Singapore could not hold. The drivers are mundane but decisive — available land, lower power costs, and direct proximity to Singapore's connectivity backbone. Yet Malaysia turned an accident of geography into industrial policy. In February 2026 the prime minister confirmed a freeze on all new data-center approvals unrelated to AI, and the 2026 budget earmarked about US$490 million — roughly 2.1 billion Malaysian ringgit (~US$470 million) — for a sovereign AI cloud designed to keep training data and model weights inside national borders. Malaysia's total development pipeline is estimated at roughly 3.4 gigawatts, around 60 percent of all proposed regional capacity. YTL Power's Nvidia-backed liquid-cooled campus, AirTrunk's hyperscale facility, and Microsoft's planned cloud region all cluster in the same Johor corridor.

## A second track: the sovereign AI cloud

The most interesting shift is structural. The build-out has split into two parallel lanes: the hyperscaler campuses of US giants (AWS, Microsoft, Google, Oracle — together committed to more than US$18 billion in regional cloud capex through 2028) on one side, and national sovereign AI clouds on the other. Indonesia commissioned its first sovereign AI data center in 2026; Malaysia put sovereign allocation into the national budget at scale; Singapore launched the world's first Agentic AI Framework. The line between "whose compute" and "whose sovereignty" is now a regional policy question, not a vendor preference. This is where the geopolitical stakes become explicit: who controls the models, the weights, and the data matters as much as who pours the concrete halls.

## The US and Chinese stacks now run side by side

Southeast Asia is one of the few places where American and Chinese technology stacks operate in direct proximity. In Thailand, ByteDance has pledged US$8.8 billion in the Eastern Economic Corridor, while Huawei, Alibaba and Tencent expand alongside Western operators. Chinese cloud providers are also scaling in Malaysia and Indonesia. This coexistence is not accidental — it reflects the region's studied non-alignment and its appetite for leverage. But it also means the infrastructure layer is becoming a quiet front in the broader US–China technology contest. The region's governments are learning to arbitrage that rivalry: inviting both, binding neither, and writing the rules — data localization, trusted corridors — themselves.

## The real bottleneck: power, not land

Yet the boom collides with physical limits. A single AI data center can consume as much electricity as 100,000 households, by the International Energy Agency's estimate. Malaysia generates about 81 percent of its electricity from fossil fuels, with only about 2 percent from renewables. To feed the surge, Kuala Lumpur plans up to 8 gigawatts of new gas-fired capacity by 2030 and has revived a nuclear programme with a 2031 target. Indonesia's coal-heavy grid and slow renewable approvals pose similar strains. Malaysia even saw its first data-center protest in February 2026, when residents in Gelang Patah, Johor, rallied over dust, pollution and water concerns. The decarbonization promises and the data-center ambitions are, for now, on a collision course that no one has fully resolved.

## What it means for the region

Three readings follow. First, Southeast Asia is no longer just a factory floor for the world; it is becoming a compute floor — a place where the intangible infrastructure of the AI era is physically built. Second, the US–China contest is being localized: sovereignty, not just sanctions, is the new arena, and ASEAN states are writing the rulebook. Third, the energy bill will be the ultimate constraint on how far this gold rush runs. The region's data-center story is, in the end, a story about power — electric, geopolitical, and strategic — and about who gets to decide how it is spent.

## Further reading

**Sovereign AI cloud**: National AI infrastructure that keeps training data, models and weights within a country's borders, separating "hyperscaler compute" from "national compute."

**Johor corridor**: The data-center cluster in Malaysia's Johor state, adjacent to Singapore, now the region's largest scale-out location for AI workloads.

**Power usage effectiveness (PUE)**: A metric for data-center energy efficiency; Singapore requires new facilities to stay below 1.25.
