# 马来西亚国立大学百余名中国留学生陷"学历危机"

逾百名在马来西亚（Malaysia）国立大学修读工商管理硕士的中国留学生，在完成一年制课程后突然被告知入学英语证书涉嫌造假，成绩遭扣押、无法如期毕业。一些人10月8日赶往雪兰莪州加影警区总部报案，并求助于中国驻马大使馆。这场看似个别的"证书风波"，实则戳中了马来西亚留学热背后的结构性软肋。

## 事件：毕业前夕的"英语证书造假"指控

据《星洲日报》报道，马来西亚国立大学（UKM，简称马国大）10月27日向媒体证实，校方早前接获投报，指部分外国学生在申请入学时提交了虚假的英语资格证书。校方商学院随即对2024/2025学年第二学期及2025/2026学年第一学期外国学生的入学英语资格展开全面审查。

受影响的多为2025年3月及10月入学的中国学生。他们在完成为期一年的两个学期课程后，今年3月查询成绩时发现无法通过校内系统查看，多次电邮校方均石沉大海；直到7月才陆续接获通知，被指入学文件涉嫌造假，要求重考"高等教育英语测试（HEET）"。更引发争议的是，校方一度要求学生签署一份承认入学文件涉嫌造假的文件，遭学生拒绝。

学生质问："如果我们的入学文件有问题，校方当初不是应该先审核吗？为何录了我们又秋后算账？"校方则称，审查系因一名遭开除学生的举报而起。截至事发，超过30名受影响学生已报警，部分已从中国飞返马来西亚处理，并向使馆求助。

## 数字：逾百名学生与一场招生扩张

这起事件之所以牵动舆论，是因为它发生在"中国学生赴马留学"高速扩张的背景之下。马来西亚教育全球服务中心（EMGS）数据显示，2024年在马注册国际学生达13.7万余人，其中中国留学生5.6万余人，占比约41%，稳居第一大生源国。2025年新申请中，中国以3.18万余份继续领跑；而从2020年到2024年，中国学生的申请量由8876份攀升至3.3万余份，五年增幅高达274%。

换句话说，马来西亚正成为中国学生避开英美不确定性（签证收紧、成本攀升、安全顾虑）的"高性价比备胎"。马来亚大学等公立学府的世界大学排名（QS）上行，更强化了"以更低成本换不错学历"的叙事。扩张越快，链条越长，漏洞自然越多——百余名中国学生的遭遇，正是这条高速扩张链上的一次"爆雷"。

## 症结：宽松录取与"秋后算账"

把责任简单推给学生，并不公平。一个绕不开的疑问是：若入学文件确有问题，校方为何在录取、注册、授课一整年后才"发现"？更合理的解释是——在留学热驱动下，部分高校的录取审核本就宽松，对中介机构代交的英语证书未认真核验；等到学生临毕业、要发证时，才以"打假"之名集中清算。

校方给出的补救方案（重考高等教育英语测试、达标者重新审议毕业、未达标者可再考或转考雅思（IELTS）／托福（TOEFL））看似温和，却难掩流程之失：审查的触发源于一名被开除学生的举报，而非制度性的入学复核。宽松与严苛之间的巨大落差，最终由学生买单。

## 中介、代办与跨国教育链的风险

这起风波背后，站着一条横跨两国的留学服务产业链。从选校、代交材料到"保录"，国内留学中介机构深度参与中国学生的马来西亚升学。当一部分机构为提升录取率而"包装"甚至伪造语言证明时，风险便被悄悄埋下。

问题不在于个别学生"想走捷径"，而在于整条链路的诚信水位被稀释：高校睁一只眼闭一只眼地收生，中介为成交而冒险造假，监管在扩张期集体缺位。等到东窗事发，最弱势、信息最不对称的留学生成了唯一被追责的对象。这提醒我们，跨境教育不是"谁便宜谁有理"，而是需要两端共同把守的学术诚信防线。

## 教育枢纽的软肋：诚信即品牌

马来西亚近年力推"区域教育枢纽"战略，希望以英语授课、多元文化、相对低廉的成本，承接全球（尤其是中国）的外溢生源。但教育枢纽的竞争，归根结底是信任的竞争——文凭含金量与招生诚信，就是最核心的品牌资产。

一次百人级的证书风波，对个体是前途悬于一线，对马来西亚的留学品牌却是一次系统性警示：若不补齐入学核验与过程监管，今日"爆雷"的只是英语证书，明日可能动摇的是整个"留学马来西亚"的口碑。对急于把教育做成产业的国度而言，诚信短板比排名短板更致命。

## 使馆、维权与海外侨胞保护

事件中，受影响学生向中国驻马大使馆求助，使馆的领事保护角色再次被推到台前。近年来，从电信诈骗到留学纠纷，海外中国公民的权益保护日益成为侨务工作的重中之重。使馆介入、协助沟通与维权，既是职责所在，也折射出"以侨为桥"的民心相通逻辑——教育合作本是一带一路（Belt and Road Initiative）框架下人文交流的重要一环。

但使馆能提供的，终究是救济而非根治。要真正护住这批"流动中的侨胞"，既要靠学生与家长的审慎选择，也要靠中外院校把招生诚信做实，更要靠对违规中介的清肃。留学的门越开越大，守门的栅栏也得同步立起来。

## 延伸阅读

**马来西亚国立大学（UKM）**：马来西亚五所研究型国立大学之一，成立于1970年，主校区位于雪兰莪州万宜，是该国公立高等教育的旗舰学府之一。

**高等教育英语测试（HEET）**：马来西亚公立大学为外语授课研究生设置的英语能力门槛考试，未达标者须补考或改考雅思、托福后方能注册或毕业。

**跨境教育（Cross-border Education）**：泛指学生、课程或教育机构跨越国界提供的教育服务，是服务贸易与软实力输出的双重载体。

# Over a Hundred Chinese Students at Malaysia's National University Caught in a "Credential Crisis"

More than a hundred Chinese students enrolled in the MBA programme at Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia (UKM) were told, after finishing a one-year course, that their admission English certificates were allegedly fake. Their grades were withheld and they could not graduate on time. On October 8, over thirty of them lodged police reports at the Kajang district police headquarters in Selangor and sought help from the Chinese Embassy. What looks like an isolated "certificate scandal" actually exposes a structural soft spot beneath Malaysia's study-abroad boom.

## The incident: fraud allegations at the graduation gate

According to Sin Chew Daily, UKM confirmed to the media on October 27 that it had received reports that some foreign students had submitted fraudulent English proficiency certificates when applying. The university's business school then launched a full review of the English qualifications of foreign students admitted in Semester 2 of 2024/2025 and Semester 1 of 2025/2026.

Most of those affected were Chinese students who enrolled in March and October 2025. After completing the two-semester, one-year programme, they found in March this year that they could not view their results through the university's internal system; emails to the administration went unanswered. Only in July did they receive notices alleging their admission documents were suspect and demanding they retake the Higher Education English Test (HEET). More controversially, the university at one point asked them to sign a document admitting their admission files were "suspected of fraud" — which they refused.

"Why did the university admit us in the first place if our documents were problematic?" one student asked. "Why settle accounts only now?" UKM says the review was triggered by a report from a dismissed student. More than thirty affected students have filed police reports; some have flown back from China to handle the matter and have turned to the embassy for help.

## The numbers: over a hundred students and an enrolment boom

The case resonates because it unfolds against a steep rise in Chinese enrolment in Malaysia. Data from Education Malaysia Global Services (EMGS) show that in 2024, of more than 137,000 international students registered in Malaysia, over 56,000 were from China — about 41 per cent, comfortably the largest source country. In 2025 new applications, China again led with more than 31,800. Between 2020 and 2024, Chinese applications surged from 8,876 to over 33,000 — a five-year increase of 274 per cent.

In short, Malaysia has become the "cost-effective backup" for Chinese students seeking to avoid the uncertainties of Britain and the United States — tightening visas, rising costs, and safety concerns. Rising QS rankings at public universities such as the University of Malaya have reinforced a narrative of "decent degrees at lower cost." The faster the expansion, the longer the chain, the more the cracks — and the plight of over a hundred students is one such crack surfacing.

## The crux: lax admission and retrospective reckoning

It is unfair to lay all the blame on the students. An unavoidable question: if the admission documents were indeed problematic, why did the university "discover" it only after a full year of enrolment, teaching, and registration? The more plausible explanation is that, driven by the enrolment frenzy, some institutions screened applications loosely and never properly verified the English certificates submitted by agents — and only launched a concentrated "crackdown" at graduation when degrees were about to be conferred.

The remedy offered — retake the HEET, have graduation reconsidered if the score reaches the threshold, or sit IELTS/TOEFL otherwise — appears mild, yet it cannot hide the procedural failure: the review was triggered by a report from a dismissed student, not by any institutional admission audit. The huge gap between leniency at entry and severity at exit is ultimately paid for by the students.

## Agents, brokers and the risks of a cross-border education chain

Behind this storm stands a two-country study-abroad service industry. From school selection and document submission to "guaranteed admission," domestic agencies are deeply embedded in Chinese students' Malaysian advancement. When some agencies "package" or even forge language proof to lift admission odds, the risk is quietly buried.

The problem is not a few students "cutting corners," but the dilution of integrity across the whole chain: universities admit with one eye closed, agencies gamble on forgery to close deals, and regulators look away during the boom. When the scandal breaks, the most vulnerable and least informed — the students — become the only ones held accountable. This is a reminder that cross-border education is not "cheapest wins," but an academic-integrity line that both ends must guard together.

## The soft underbelly of an education hub: integrity is the brand

Malaysia has in recent years pushed a "regional education hub" strategy, hoping to capture globally mobile — especially Chinese — enrolment with English-medium teaching, multicultural settings, and relatively low cost. But competition among education hubs is, at its core, a competition for trust: the value of the diploma and the integrity of admission are the most important brand assets.

A hundred-strong certificate scandal is, for the individuals, a future hanging by a thread; for Malaysia's study-abroad brand, it is a systemic warning. Without shoring up admission verification and process oversight, today's "explosion" is only about English certificates; tomorrow it could shake the very reputation of "Study in Malaysia." For a country eager to turn education into an industry, an integrity deficit is more fatal than a ranking deficit.

## The embassy, rights defence and protection of overseas Chinese

In this case, the affected students turned to the Chinese Embassy in Malaysia, once again bringing the embassy's consular-protection role to the fore. In recent years, from telecom fraud to study-abroad disputes, protecting the rights of Chinese citizens abroad has become a central task of overseas-Chinese affairs. The embassy's intervention, facilitation and rights advocacy are both its duty and a reflection of the logic of people-to-people bonds — educational cooperation being a key strand of people-to-people exchange under the Belt and Road framework.

But what the embassy can offer is relief, not a cure. To truly protect this "mobile diaspora," students and parents must choose prudently, Chinese and Malaysian institutions must make admission integrity real, and unscrupulous agencies must be cleaned up. As the door to studying abroad opens wider, the fence at the gate must rise in step.
