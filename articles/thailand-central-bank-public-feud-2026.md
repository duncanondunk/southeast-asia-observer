# 泰国（Thailand）央行高层罕见"公开开撕"：独立性这道防火墙，究竟挡的是谁

本来该在闭门会议里消化掉的分歧，这一次被摆上了台面。向来低调、习惯用共识和谨慎来维持体面的泰国中央银行，罕见地爆发了董事会主席与行长的公开内讧。董事会主席颂猜·萨乍蓬上周末发声明，直指央行管理不善、改革停滞；行长维泰·拉达纳功先在社交媒体发文反击，随后在所有副行长陪同下召开记者会，逐条回敬。据彭博社报道，前货币政策委员会委员颂猜·集素冲评价说，行长与董事会主席之间从未有过如此公开的冲突，"这可能是历史上第一次，绝对非常不寻常"。

这不是一场关于利率的争吵，而是一场关于"谁来定义央行独立性"的争吵。它发生在泰国经济最需要货币政策与财政政策协同的时刻——家庭债务高企、中小企业信贷萎缩、泰铢波动加剧。 观察人士会说这是治理层面的技术分歧，但把人事、法律与政党线索串起来看，一场关于权力边界的角力才算真正浮出水面。

## 修法之争：绩效指标与"解职权"

争议的落点，是对2008年《泰国银行法》的修订。颂猜主张制定更清晰的行长绩效考核标准，引入量化指标，并明确界定绩效欠佳是否构成免职理由。他否认自己试图借此赋予董事会直接解雇行长的权力，但话里话外，指向的正是行长职位的"可问责性"。

维泰的回应寸步不让。他重申央行内部工作组已得出结论：现行法律足够完善，无须修改；并警告把行长绩效与解职挂钩，可能为政治干预大开方便之门。他给出的判断相当绝对——全球没有任何一个国家采取这种做法。副首相兼财政部长埃尼提·尼提探巴帕则选择中立，称是否修法应由央行内部自行决定，"我相信央行的独立性，这意味着政府不应该干预央行的运作"。

把这三个人的话并排读，分歧就清楚了：颂猜担心的是"没人能问责一个不作为的行长"，维泰担心的是"一旦开了问责的口子，政治之手就能伸进来"。双方争夺的其实是同一件东西——对"独立性"的解释权。独立性究竟是保护央行免受政治操纵的防火墙，还是保护管理层免受问责的挡箭牌？这个问题，比修法本身更尖锐。

## 两份成绩单：18个项目对17个季度

这场争吵之所以难分高下，是因为双方各自握着一份能自圆其说的成绩单。

维泰今年56岁，自2025年10月出任行长以来，主张央行突破传统的货币政策框架，更多介入家庭债务、金融排斥和非法资金等结构性问题。他列举的履职成果包括：两度下调基准利率，从1.5%降到1.0%；推出18个项目应对四大结构性难题；重组超过18万个零售不良账户，将信用卡最低还款比例维持在8%，惠及约200万个账户；通过中小企业信贷提升计划发放超过800亿泰铢（约165亿元人民币）贷款；取消或下调19项金融服务费，预计每年为民众和企业节省80亿泰铢（约16.5亿元人民币）。在打击灰产方面，他称大额异常现金提取下降57%，可疑黄金交易量暴跌85%，关停了2500多个涉赌涉诈的"钱骡账户"。

颂猜的反驳同样有数据支撑。他援引普伊·翁帕功经济研究所今年8月发布的研究指出，在效率最高的30%企业中，只有约五分之一的小微企业拿到了商业银行贷款，大型企业这一比例超过60%；2024年的数据库中，约70万家企业有超过80%没有商业银行贷款记录；2025年超过75%的商业贷款流向约1.5万家大型企业。他尤其强调，商业银行对小微企业的贷款已连续近17个季度收缩，并把这称为东盟（ASEAN）内独一无二的现象。他批评央行的举措"多属短期治标或表面数据"，在三大结构性痛点上毫无进展。

两份成绩单的说服力，取决于你更相信"动作的数量"还是"结果的方向"。维泰强调"动手做"而非"数据公关"，颂猜则追问：如果做了这么多，为什么中小企业信贷还在连续收缩？这是一个难以回避的问题。

## 独立性的边界：谁来定义"干预"

真正让这场内讧超出人事纠纷的，是它对央行独立性的重新定义。

颂猜的逻辑里，对政治压力的担忧"反映了对议会制度的信心不足"——换言之，过度强调独立，可能变成对民选政府的不信任投票。他认为，法律的现代化应该纳入财政部、法制办公室、国家经济社会发展委员会等外部机构共同研究，而不是由央行管理层自己组成的工作组"既当运动员又当裁判"。这种质疑，把程序问题上升到了代表性问题：谁来参与制定规则，谁就能定义独立性的边界。

维泰的逻辑则更接近主流央行的正统立场：独立性是货币政策有效性的前提，一旦绩效与解职挂钩，政府就能以"绩效不佳"为由更换行长，独立性将名存实亡。前货币政策委员会委员也指出，颂猜作为董事会主席，本身并不在货币政策委员会、金融机构政策委员会和支付系统委员会中拥有政策制定权——这些委员会都由行长主持。也就是说，批评者可以批评，却不能直接改变政策。

这套安排设计的初衷，是让"监督"与"操作"分离。但当监督者认为操作者失职、操作者认为监督者越界时，制度并没有提供一条顺畅的裁决通道。于是分歧外溢到了媒体。独立学者、前最高法院资深法官瓦·丁萨米批评说，把组织内部分歧拿到媒体上公开指责并不妥当，"良好治理应通过组织机制解决问题，而非以媒体为指责舞台"。这话说得在理，但也恰恰说明：当组织机制失灵，媒体就会成为唯一的出口。

## 政党博弈的暗线

把人物履历摊开，这场内讧还有一层政党政治的底色。

颂猜出身财政部，最高做到财政部次长；维泰则出自政府储蓄银行。两人都是从体制内成长起来的技术官僚，但立足点不同。更值得注意的是任命时序：维泰是在为泰党政府时期上任的，而现任政府由自豪党领导。前朝任命的行长，遇上新朝的董事会主席，修法争议就很难被纯粹当作技术讨论。

颂猜自2026年6月起担任董事会主席，上任约一年半便公开表达"失望"。他要求央行加快法律修订、加强对信贷审批流程的监管，甚至建议对拒贷率异常高的银行进行抽查。这些主张看似专业，但落在政治坐标上，很容易被解读为新政府对货币政策方向施加影响的前奏。财政部长埃尼提反复强调政府不干预，恰恰说明"干预"的猜测已经到了需要反复澄清的程度。

不过，受访经济师普遍认为，这场风波尚不至于危及泰国的货币政策方向，因为争议主要集中在内部治理层面，而非要求调整利率的政策压力。这个判断可能是冷静的，但它低估了一件事：制度边界一旦被公开拉扯，市场就会重新定价"央行独立性"这个原本被视为理所当然的资产。

## 谁在替中小企业说话

回到中小企业信贷这条主线，问题的分量其实超出了曼谷的会议室。

泰国的经济增长近年来持续跑输区域同侪，家庭债务与中小企业融资难是两大结构性拖累。颂猜引用的数据揭示了一个残酷事实：效率最高的企业反而拿不到贷款，资源高度集中在少数大型企业手里。这不是银行"惜贷"那么简单，而是信贷配置机制本身出现了扭曲——当银行更倾向于向有抵押、有关系的头部客户放款，生产率更高的中小微企业就被挤出。

但解决方案的分歧同样明显。颂猜寄望于修法、加强监管和外部评估；维泰则强调结构性问题无法靠单一项目或单一机构解决，必须"持续重复推进"。前者要制度重构，后者要执行积累。两种路径并非互斥，问题在于谁掌握推动的权力和节奏。当董事会主席与行长连"是否有矛盾"都要在记者会上公开否认时，外界很难相信他们能在同一份改革方案上达成一致。

对泰国而言，真正的风险不是这一次修法与否，而是治理内耗拖慢了应对结构性问题的速度。央行的独立性是一项公共资产，它的价值不在于让管理层免于批评，而在于让货币政策免受选举周期的绑架。若把独立性异化为拒绝问责的盾牌，反而会侵蚀它赖以存在的公信力。这场公开内讧的收获，或许正是把这个问题逼到了必须回答的位置。

## 延伸阅读

**央行独立性（Central Bank Independence）**：指央行在制定和执行货币政策时免受政府短期政治目标干预的制度安排，通常通过法定任期、任命程序和资金自主来保障；其代价是问责链条变长，需要绩效评估与透明沟通来补足。

**绩效问责（Performance Accountability）**：把机构负责人的留任与可量化的目标挂钩的治理机制。在央行场景下，难点在于货币政策的成效往往滞后且受外部冲击影响，简单指标容易诱导短视行为。

**中小企业信贷缺口（SME Credit Gap）**：指中小企业实际获得的信贷与其创造就业、产值所需资金之间的差额。缺口长期存在会压制生产率与就业增长，是许多中等收入经济体共同面临的结构性难题。

# Thailand's central bank airs its dirty laundry: independence is a firewall — but against whom?

A disagreement that would normally be digested behind closed doors was laid out in public this time. The Bank of Thailand, long low-key and habituated to managing differences through consensus and discretion, has rarely seen such an open feud between its board chairman and its governor. Board chairman Somchai Sajjapongse issued a statement over the weekend accusing the central bank of mismanagement and stalled reform. Governor Vitai Ratanakorn hit back on social media, then convened a press conference flanked by all his deputies to answer point by point. According to Bloomberg, former Monetary Policy Committee member Somchai Jitsuchon said there had never been such a public clash between a governor and a board chairman: "This may be the first time in history. It is absolutely extraordinary."

This is not a quarrel about interest rates. It is a quarrel about who gets to define central bank independence, and it erupts at a moment when Thailand badly needs monetary and fiscal policy to pull in the same direction — household debt is high, small-business credit is shrinking and the baht is volatile. Observers will call it a technical dispute over governance. But string together the personnel, the legal text and the party-political thread, and what emerges is a genuine contest over the boundaries of power.

## The legal fight: performance metrics and the power to dismiss

The dispute lands on amendments to the Bank of Thailand Act of 2008. Somchai wants clearer performance standards for the governor, including quantitative metrics and an explicit definition of whether poor performance constitutes grounds for removal. He denies seeking to give the board a direct power to fire the governor, but the subtext is precisely the accountability of the governor's office.

Vitai gives no ground. He reiterates that an internal working group concluded the existing law is adequate and needs no revision, and warns that tying a governor's performance to dismissal could open the door wide to political interference. His judgment is categorical: no other country in the world does this. Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister Ekniti Nitithanprapas chose neutrality, saying the decision on amendments should rest with the central bank itself. "I believe in the central bank's independence," he said, "which means the government should not intervene in its operations."

Read the three statements side by side and the split becomes clear. Somchai worries that no one can hold an inactive governor to account; Vitai worries that once the door to accountability is opened, a political hand can reach in. What the two men are fighting over is the same thing — the right to interpret "independence." Is independence a firewall protecting the bank from political manipulation, or a shield protecting management from accountability? That question is sharper than the amendment itself.

## Two scorecards: 18 projects versus 17 quarters

The row is hard to adjudicate because each side holds a scorecard that makes sense on its own terms.

Vitai, 56, took office in October 2025 and has argued that the bank should break out of a narrow monetary-policy frame and intervene more directly in structural problems: household debt, financial exclusion and illicit flows. His listed achievements include two rate cuts, from 1.5 percent to 1.0 percent; 18 programmes addressing four structural problems; the restructuring of more than 180,000 non-performing retail accounts and a credit-card minimum repayment ratio held at 8 percent, benefiting some two million accounts; more than 80 billion baht (about US$2.3 billion) in loans under an SME credit-boost scheme; and the removal or reduction of 19 financial service fees, expected to save the public and businesses some 8 billion baht (about US$230 million) a year. On grey-market activity, he says abnormally large cash withdrawals fell 57 percent, suspicious gold trading collapsed 85 percent, and more than 2,500 "money mule" accounts linked to gambling and fraud were shut.

Somchai's rebuttal is equally data-heavy. Citing a study published in August by the Puey Ungphakorn Institute for Economic Research, he notes that among the most efficient 30 percent of firms, only about one in five small and micro enterprises obtained a commercial bank loan, versus more than 60 percent of large firms. In the 2024 dataset, more than 80 percent of roughly 700,000 firms had no recorded commercial bank loan; in 2025, over 75 percent of commercial lending went to about 15,000 large firms. He stresses that bank lending to small and micro firms has contracted for nearly 17 consecutive quarters, calling it a phenomenon unique within ASEAN. He dismisses the central bank's measures as largely "short-term fixes or surface-level numbers" that have made no progress on the three core structural pain points.

Which scorecard persuades depends on whether you trust the volume of actions or the direction of outcomes. Vitai insists on "actually doing" rather than "data public relations"; Somchai asks the awkward question: if so much has been done, why is small-business credit still contracting quarter after quarter?

## The limits of independence: who defines interference

What lifts the feud above a personnel dispute is its redefinition of independence itself.

In Somchai's logic, anxiety over political pressure "reflects a lack of confidence in the parliamentary system" — in other words, an overemphasis on independence can amount to a vote of no confidence in elected government. He argues that modernising the law should involve outside bodies such as the Finance Ministry, the Office of the Council of State and the National Economic and Social Development Council, rather than a working group composed of bank management that "runs the race and judges it." That reframes a procedural question as one of representation: who helps write the rules gets to define the limits of independence.

Vitai's logic stays closer to central-banking orthodoxy: independence is a precondition for effective monetary policy, and once performance is tied to dismissal, a government could replace a governor for "poor performance" and independence becomes nominal. A former MPC member also notes that as board chairman, Somchai holds no policy-making power within the Monetary Policy Committee, the Financial Institutions Policy Committee or the Payment Systems Committee — all chaired by the governor. Critics, in short, can criticise but cannot directly change policy.

The design intent was to separate oversight from operations. But when the overseer believes the operator is failing and the operator believes the overseer is overstepping, the system offers no clean channel for adjudication. So the disagreement spills into the media. Independent scholar and former senior Supreme Court justice Wat Ting Samitr criticised the public airing, saying internal disputes should be resolved through organisational mechanisms, "not with the media as a stage for accusation." Fair enough — but it also shows that when internal mechanisms fail, the media becomes the only exit.

## The undercurrent of party politics

Lay out the two résumés and the feud acquires a partisan hue. Somchai came from the Finance Ministry, rising to permanent secretary; Vitai came from the Government Savings Bank. Both are technocrats formed inside the system, but from different bases. More telling is the sequence of appointments: Vitai took office under a Pheu Thai government, while the current government is led by the Bhumjaithai party. A governor appointed by the previous administration facing a board chairman of the new one makes it hard to treat the amendment fight as purely technical.

Somchai became board chairman in June 2026 and voiced "disappointment" roughly a year and a half later. His calls to accelerate legal revision, tighten oversight of credit approval and even audit banks with abnormally high rejection rates sound professional, yet mapped onto the political coordinates they are easily read as a prelude to influencing the direction of monetary policy. Finance Minister Ekniti's repeated insistence that the government will not intervene is itself evidence that suspicions of intervention have reached a level requiring repeated clarification.

Still, economists interviewed generally believe the row will not derail Thailand's monetary policy direction, because the dispute is centred on internal governance rather than pressure to change rates. That may be a cool-headed reading, but it understates one thing: once institutional boundaries are publicly tugged at, markets reprice "central bank independence" — an asset previously taken for granted.

## Who speaks for small business

Return to the main thread of SME credit and the stakes reach well beyond a meeting room in Bangkok.

Thailand's growth has lagged regional peers, with household debt and difficult financing for small firms as two structural drags. Somchai's figures reveal a harsh fact: the most efficient firms are the ones that cannot get loans, and credit is concentrated in a handful of large companies. This is not simply banks being reluctant to lend; it is a distortion in the allocation mechanism itself. When banks prefer collateral-rich, well-connected head clients, more productive small and micro firms are crowded out.

The remedies, however, diverge just as sharply. Somchai looks to legislation, tighter supervision and outside evaluation; Vitai insists structural problems cannot be solved by any single programme or institution and must be "repeatedly pushed forward." One wants institutional reconstruction, the other accumulating execution. The two paths are not mutually exclusive; the problem is who holds the power and the pace to drive them. When a board chairman and a governor must publicly deny, at a press conference, that they have a conflict, the outside world has little reason to believe they can agree on a single reform plan.

For Thailand, the real risk is not whether this amendment passes, but that governance infighting slows the response to structural problems. Central bank independence is a public asset; its value lies not in exempting management from criticism but in keeping monetary policy free from the capture of election cycles. If independence is repurposed as a shield against accountability, it erodes the credibility on which it rests. Perhaps the gain from this public feud is that it forces the question to a point where it can no longer be avoided.

## Further reading

**Central bank independence**: The institutional arrangement under which a central bank conducts monetary policy free from short-term political objectives, usually secured by fixed terms, appointment procedures and financial autonomy. Its cost is a longer accountability chain, which must be offset by performance evaluation and transparent communication.

**Performance accountability**: A governance mechanism that ties an office-holder's tenure to measurable goals. In a central bank, the difficulty is that policy results are delayed and exposed to external shocks, so crude indicators can induce short-sighted behaviour.

**SME credit gap**: The difference between the credit small and medium enterprises actually receive and what they need to sustain employment and output. A persistent gap suppresses productivity and job growth, a structural challenge shared by many middle-income economies.
