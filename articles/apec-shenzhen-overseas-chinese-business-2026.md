# 深圳亚太经合组织会议上的华商：从牵线搭桥到搭建市场基础设施

## 一块榴莲引出的对接题

2026年亚太经合组织工商领导人峰会11月17日至18日在深圳举行。据中国侨网（China Qiao Wang）转引中新社报道，美中电商贸易协会执行会长王明明在会上提到一个细节：专题研讨会上，榴莲已经进入蛋糕、冰淇淋和咖啡等消费场景。她由此想到，如果美国烘焙品牌想开发榴莲产品，完全可以通过商家对商家（B2B）电商平台对接泰国（Thailand）原料供应商。

这不只是关于一种气味浓烈的水果的趣闻，而是一次对离散华商功能的精准描述。跨境贸易最难的部分，往往不是找不到商品，而是找不到可靠的交易对手、合规的通道和双方都认可的标准。一个既了解一方市场口味、又熟悉另一方供给条件的网络，实际上就是在提供市场基础设施。在这个榴莲例子里，东南亚（Southeast Asia）不是可有可无的背景板，而是让整条链条产生价值的起点。

由此看深圳这场会议，讨论“互联互通”并不稀奇，真正值得追问的是：谁在完成连接，依据什么规则完成连接。

## 从餐馆货架到数字平台

同一篇报道还引述韩国中华总商会执行会长郑朝辉的判断：过去在韩华商主要从事传统外贸、餐饮、零售和旅游服务，如今已扩展到跨境电商、供应链物流、医疗健康、科技服务、新能源和专业商务服务。

行业构成的变化，改变的是竞争力的来源。餐馆和进出口柜台靠的是熟人信任与家庭劳动，数字贸易和物流靠的是数据、合规以及满足对方技术要求的能力。过去定义侨商生意的“熟悉”，今天已不足以单独构成优势，它必须被转化为制度化的能力。

东南亚华商正在经历相近的转向，只是各自带有本地烙印。这一区域语言、支付系统和监管节奏差异很大，不存在一套可以照搬的区域打法。恰恰是这种难度，让同时熟悉两边规则的中介有了用武之地。但这也意味着老本行会迅速贬值：不投资技能、标准和数字能力的网络，终将被愿意做这些投资的平台取代。

## 合规与在地化：新的门槛

郑朝辉最有分量的话不是说机会，而是说约束。他指出，华商参与地区经贸往来更加注重合规经营与在地化发展，如何在符合各经济体法律法规和监管要求的前提下推动行业标准衔接，已成为眼下华商关切。

这是相当坦率的承认。关于海外华商的传统叙事总是赞美灵活性——靠非正式关系快速穿行于不同市场。但在数据规则、产品标准、劳动法和反欺诈执法共同塑造的环境里，没有合规约束的灵活性反而会成为负债。新的护城河不是认识谁，而是能拿出什么可以被审查的文件。

对东南亚来说，这不是抽象警告。区域市场向中国平台和资本开放的速度，快过各国规则协调的速度。当标准不能随货物一起跨境，风险就由中介承担。一家华商企业如果能向一国的监管者解释产品，再把监管要求反馈给另一国的供应商，它提供的服务是“文化亲近”无法替代的。

## 双向流动：华商与住在国发展同频

报道有意把视野拉到中国周边之外。全墨西哥华人华侨总会副会长牛董菲说，墨西哥承接北美生产需求、辐射拉美消费市场，传统工业基础完善、转型升级需求旺盛，华商赴墨投资建厂契合当地发展战略；俄罗斯浙江华侨华人联合总会常务副会长蔡一航描述了从批发贸易到在当地投资建厂、开展跨境金融服务，从向俄出口到帮助俄罗斯产品销往其他市场的转变；澳大利亚维多利亚州华人社团联合会副主席南京京则提到，年轻华商正进入科技创新、数字经济、新能源和文化传媒等领域，扎根当地的华商能在市场调研、项目评估和伙伴筛选上支持中国企业出海。

把这些案例放在一起，可以看到侨商生意的方向变了。旧模式是单向导管：资本、商品和经验从中国流向外部。新模式是双向嵌入：中介扎根住在国经济，对当地条件负责，也可能把第三国产品带回中国或区域市场。这使网络更有用，也使它更须承担责任。一个在当地扎根的企业，不能一边宣称代表社群，一边无视东道国的利益。

东南亚长期是这种嵌入的试验场。不同的是今天的规模和审视强度。区域供应链重组之际，那些降低搜寻成本的侨商网络，同时被要求证明透明度、本地增加值和对并非由自己制定的规则的遵守。

## 东南亚的位置：黏合剂也有边界

从深圳峰会很容易得出一个结论：海外华商网络是区域合作天然的黏合剂。这话说过头了。一个榴莲对接平台可以解决商业问题，却解决不了原产地规则、数据跨境和标准互认。那些是公共决策，由政府作出，不能外包给商会。

网络能做的是降低尝试的成本：找到供应商、试出一种交易格式、拼起一个小联合体，再向监管者展示实践中什么行得通。这对亚太经合组织所关心的供应链韧性、人工智能赋能实体经济和中小企业参与，确是实实在在的贡献。但这是执行层面的贡献，不是规则制定层面的贡献。

真正的风险是一种叙事通胀：把每一次成功对接都包装成“侨力”的胜利。更准确的说法是，海外华商已成为区域市场基础设施的一部分——它的价值恰在于专业，它的局限也恰在于专业，无法替代制度。对东南亚而言，商业一体化与规则一体化之间的落差仍然很大，这个区分应当塑造我们如何谈论侨胞的“桥梁”角色。桥好不好走，取决于两端接上的路。

## 延伸阅读

**供应链韧性（supply chain resilience）**：跨境生产网络在冲击下维持运转的能力，既是亚太经合组织的核心议题，也是对华商中介能力的现实检验。

**在地化经营（localisation）**：外资企业通过本地雇用、本地采购、合规经营和参与当地行业标准，嵌入东道国经济的过程。

**跨境电子商务（cross-border e-commerce）**：把企业与企业而非企业与消费者连接起来的数字平台，正越来越多地把东南亚农产品供应商与远方市场的买家对接起来。

# Overseas Chinese Business at Shenzhen APEC: From Bridge Builders to Market Infrastructure

## The question a durian raises

At the 2026 APEC CEO Summit in Shenzhen on 17–18 November, one of the more revealing conversations did not come from a ministerial podium. According to China Qiao Wang, citing China News Service, Wang Mingming, executive chair of the US–China E-commerce Trade Association, recalled a seminar session in which durian had found its way into cakes, ice cream and coffee. Her conclusion was practical: if an American bakery group wanted to develop durian products, a business-to-business platform could match it with Thai raw-material suppliers.

This is more than a curiosity about a fragrant fruit. It is a compact description of what overseas Chinese business networks increasingly do: they convert dispersed information into workable transactions. The hard part of cross-border trade is rarely finding a product; it is finding a reliable counterpart, a compliant route and a standard both sides accept. A network that knows the tastes of one market and the supply conditions of another is, in effect, market infrastructure. In the durian example, Southeast Asia is not a decorative backdrop — it is the point of origin that gives the chain its value.

That is the useful lens through which to read this year's Shenzhen gathering. Talk of "connectivity" is cheap. The interesting question is who actually performs the connection, and under what rules. A dinner-panel anecdote about fruit can tell us more about the mechanics of regional integration than a dozen communiques.

## From shops and restaurants to digital platforms

The same report quotes Zheng Chaohui, executive chair of the Korea Chinese Chamber of Commerce, on the changing composition of Chinese business in South Korea. In the past, he said, Korean Chinese entrepreneurs concentrated on traditional trade, catering, retail and tourism services. Today they have moved into cross-border e-commerce, supply-chain logistics, medical and health services, technology services, new energy and professional business services.

The sectoral shift matters because it changes the source of competitive advantage. Restaurants and import-export desks run on personal trust and family labour. Digital trade and logistics run on data, compliance and the ability to meet a partner's technical requirements. The familiarity that once defined diaspora commerce is no longer sufficient by itself; it has to be converted into institutional competence. Otherwise it is a sentiment, not a capability.

Southeast Asian Chinese businesses have been navigating a similar transition, though with their own local inflection. The region's diversity — different languages, payment systems and regulatory speeds — makes a single regional playbook impossible. That difficulty is precisely where intermediaries with dual familiarity can earn their keep. But it also means the old advantage is perishable: a network that does not invest in skills, standards and digital capability will eventually be replaced by a platform that does. Nostalgia is not a business model.

## Compliance and localisation: the new threshold

Zheng's most telling remark was not about opportunity but about constraint. He noted that Chinese businesses now pay more attention to compliant operation and local development, and that aligning industry standards across economies has become a central concern for them.

That is a candid admission. The traditional story about overseas Chinese business celebrates flexibility — the ability to move quickly through informal relationships. In a regulatory environment shaped by data rules, product standards, labour law and anti-fraud enforcement, flexibility without compliance becomes a liability. The new moat is not who you know but what you can document.

For Southeast Asia this is not an abstract warning. Markets in the region are opening to Chinese platforms and Chinese capital at a speed that outpaces the harmonisation of their rules. When standards do not travel with goods, the intermediary absorbs the risk. A diaspora firm that can explain a product to a regulator in one country and explain that regulator's requirements back to a supplier in another is providing a service that cultural affinity alone cannot substitute. The phrase "localisation" is often used as decoration in business speeches; in practice it is a cost centre and a discipline.

## Two-way flows: diaspora business and host-country development

The report deliberately widens the frame beyond China and its neighbours. Niu Dongfei, vice-chair of the All-Mexico Chinese Community Association, described Chinese investment in Mexican manufacturing as responding both to North American production demand and to Mexico's own industrial upgrading goals. Cai Yihang, executive vice-chair of the Zhejiang Chinese Federation in Russia, described a shift from wholesale trade to local manufacturing and cross-border financial services — and from exporting to Russia to helping Russian products reach other markets. Nanjing Jing, vice-chair of the Victorian Chinese Community Federation in Australia, argued that younger Chinese entrepreneurs are moving into technology, the digital economy, new energy and professional services, and that locally embedded Chinese firms can help companies going global with market research, project assessment and partner selection.

Read together, these cases describe a change in the direction of diaspora business. The older model was a one-way conduit: capital, goods and know-how moved outward from China. The newer model is two-way embedding: the intermediary is rooted in the host economy, answers to local conditions and may route third-country products back into Chinese or regional markets. That makes the network more useful, but also more accountable. A business embedded locally cannot claim to speak for a community while ignoring the interests of the place that hosts it.

Southeast Asia has long been a laboratory for this kind of embedding. What has changed is the scale and the scrutiny. As regional supply chains are reorganised, the same diaspora networks that lower search costs are being asked to demonstrate transparency, local value creation and compliance with rules they did not write. That is a fairer bargain than the old one, even if it is less comfortable for incumbents.

## Where Southeast Asia fits — and where the glue stops

It would be easy to conclude from the Shenzhen summit that overseas Chinese networks are the natural glue of regional cooperation. That would overstate the case. A durian matching platform can solve a commercial problem; it cannot settle rules of origin, data transfer or mutual recognition of standards. Those are public decisions, made by governments, and they cannot be outsourced to a chamber of commerce, however well connected it may be.

What the networks can do is reduce the cost of trying. They can identify a supplier, test a format, assemble a small consortium and show regulators what works in practice. That is a genuine contribution to APEC's stated concerns — supply-chain resilience, the real-economy use of artificial intelligence and the participation of small and medium enterprises. But it is a contribution to implementation, not to rule-making. Conflating the two is how a useful intermediary starts to look like a pseudo-government.

The risk is a kind of narrative inflation: calling every successful match a triumph of "diaspora power". The more useful formulation is that overseas Chinese business has become a piece of regional market infrastructure — valuable precisely because it is specialised, and limited precisely because it is not a substitute for institutions. For Southeast Asia, where the gap between commercial integration and regulatory integration remains wide, that distinction should shape how we talk about the bridge role of the Chinese diaspora. A bridge is only as good as the roads on both sides.

## Further reading

**Supply chain resilience**: the capacity of a cross-border production network to absorb shocks without breaking; a central APEC agenda item and a practical test for diaspora intermediaries.

**Localisation**: the process by which a foreign-invested firm embeds itself in a host economy through local hiring, sourcing, compliance and participation in local industry standards.

**Cross-border e-commerce**: digital platforms that match firms with firms rather than with final consumers, increasingly used to link Southeast Asian agricultural suppliers with buyers in distant markets.
