# 以"成年"为门槛：东南亚集体收紧社交平台的儿童保护监管

当印尼一位雅加达作家的14岁儿子轻点"我已年满18岁"，他同时拥有了照片墙（Instagram）、抖音国际版（TikTok）、我的世界（Minecraft）与罗布乐思（Roblox）的账号。这并非个例。据《南华早报》报道，自今年3月印尼实施东南亚最严的未成年人社交媒体禁令以来，孩子们用谎报年龄绕过门槛已是家常便饭。一道行政命令，关不掉一个被好奇心填满的账号。这恰恰揭示了东南亚"数字家长"实验的核心矛盾：想用法律把巨头关进笼子，却发现最先被关住的，往往是监管的想象力。

## 印尼开了第一枪：16岁以下禁入

印尼今年3月以政令形式，禁止16岁以下未成年人使用被认定为"高风险"的社交与游戏平台，范围涵盖照片墙、抖音国际版、我的世界与罗布乐思。雅加达的算盘很清楚：把保护责任从家庭推向平台。通信部长甚至亲赴纽约，与元宇宙公司（Meta）全球公共政策与安全团队面谈，要求加速下架未成年账号。但正如观察者所言，儿童保护难以公开反对，平台也就乐得在舆论成本高企时做做样子——禁令落地容易，年龄核验却始终是个技术窟窿。

## 马来西亚的罚单逻辑：不合规就重罚

马来西亚走的是"重罚加强制验证"路线。自今年6月起，16岁以下不得开设或持有社交账号，平台须核验用户年龄；违规企业最高可处1000万林吉特（约1800万元人民币）罚款，拒不执行监管指令还将叠加处罚。这笔罚单的分量，足以让任何一家社交巨头在吉隆坡派驻合规团队。但问题也随之而来：罚款能否真正倒逼"安全设计"，还是只换来一套应付检查的流程？

## 越南的"只读"草案与菲律宾的碎片化打法

越南正在审议一项法令草案，拟将16岁以下用户限制为社交媒体的"只读"模式——不能发帖、评论、转发或互动，账号须以家长身份注册，并对游戏时长设限。菲律宾则更为碎片化：既有法律强制网络服务商屏蔽儿童性虐待材料，又借监管压力从平台挤出直接让步——今年8月，元宇宙公司与罗布乐思在马尼拉就加强年龄核验、家长控制与内容下架达成妥协；多起校园枪击事件后，政府更勒令红迪（Reddit）与Discord指派本地代表，前者一度被短暂屏蔽。

## 新加坡：立法先行者

新加坡是区域内的立法先行者。其《网络安防（杂项修正）法》早在2023年生效，要求社交巨头屏蔽包括儿童在内的有害内容，违者最高罚100万新元（约560万元人民币）；自今年4月起，应用商店须核验用户年龄以下载限龄内容；今年6月成立的网络安防委员会，有权命令即时移除有害内容并限制违规账号。路径清晰，却也说明：监管的有效性，取决于执行资源而非纸面条文。

## 管得住平台，管不住权力边界

值得警惕的是，儿童保护这面"政治正确"的旗，正被一些国家顺势用来扩张对数字空间的管控。分析人士指出，在隐私、表达自由与公民参与保障较弱的政体里，以安全为名收紧平台，往往也顺手收紧了公共讨论的空间。印尼拟议的罚款框架甚至由同一部门"立法、裁判、处罚"一肩挑，程序正义存疑；而屏蔽网站"伤的是本国用户，而非公司"。监管若沦为权力的延伸，保护的初衷便可能被悄悄置换。

## 数字主权的代价

东南亚的集体行动，本质是一场关于"数字主权"的练兵：让总部远在加州的巨头，为本地儿童的福祉负责。方向无可厚非。但硬币的另一面，是市场大小决定了巨头投入的多寡——研究显示，在东盟11国中，有5国连大社交公司的驻在地代表都没有，监管者与公民社会往往求助无门。真正的保护，不能只靠一纸禁令，而要靠可执行的年龄核验、透明的算法问责，以及不被滥用的权力边界。

## 延伸阅读

**年龄验证（Age verification）**：通过证件、生物识别或支付信息确认用户是否达到法定使用年龄；技术可行却常因隐私与误判争议而执行打折。
**数字主权（Digital sovereignty）**：一国对境内数据、平台与网络空间的规则制定权；儿童网络保护正成为其最直接的落脚点之一。
**安全设计（Safety-by-design）**：要求平台在产品研发初期内建儿童保护措施，而非事后补救；印尼民间组织呼吁以此替代"自愿承诺"。

# Can Southeast Asia make Big Tech answer for its youngest users?

When the 14-year-old son of a Jakarta-based writer ticks "I am over 18," he instantly holds accounts on Instagram, TikTok, Minecraft and Roblox. He is hardly alone. According to the South China Morning Post, since Indonesia imposed Southeast Asia's strictest minors' social-media ban this March, children have routinely bypassed the threshold by lying about their age. An administrative order cannot close an account filled with curiosity. That captures the central paradox of Southeast Asia's "digital parent" experiment: lawmakers want to cage the tech giants, yet the first thing trapped is often the regulator's own imagination.

## Indonesia fired first: under-16s barred

In March, Indonesia decreed a ban on under-16s using platforms deemed "high-risk," covering Instagram, TikTok, Minecraft and Roblox. Jakarta's calculus is clear: shift the duty of protection from families to platforms. The communications minister even travelled to New York to meet Meta's global public-policy and safety leadership, pressing for faster deactivation of underage accounts. Yet as observers note, child protection is hard to oppose publicly, so platforms gladly perform compliance when the reputational cost is high. The ban was easy to enact; age verification remains a technical hole.

## Malaysia's fine logic: comply or pay

Malaysia chose the "heavy fines plus mandatory verification" route. Since June, under-16s cannot open or hold social accounts, and platforms must verify users' ages. Non-compliant firms face penalties of up to 10 million ringgit (US$2.5 million), with further sanctions for ignoring regulatory directives. That sum is enough to justify a compliance team in Kuala Lumpur. But the question follows: does the fine truly force "safety-by-design," or merely a process built to pass inspection?

## Vietnam's view-only draft and the Philippines' piecemeal playbook

Vietnam is weighing a draft decree that would lock under-16s into a "view-only" mode — no posting, commenting, sharing or reacting — with accounts registered under a parent's identity and caps on gaming time. The Philippines is more fragmented: existing law compels internet providers to block child sexual abuse material, and regulatory pressure has squeezed direct concessions from platforms. In August, Meta and Roblox struck deals in Manila to tighten age checks, parental controls and takedowns; after a spate of school shootings, authorities ordered Reddit and Discord to appoint local representatives, briefly blocking the latter.

## Singapore: the early legislator

Singapore is the region's early mover. Its Online Safety (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act took effect in 2023, requiring social giants to shield children from harmful content or face fines up to S$1 million (US$781,500). Since April, app stores must verify ages for age-restricted downloads; an Online Safety Commission launched in June can order immediate removal of harmful content and restrict offending accounts. The path is clear — yet it also shows that a regulator's bite depends on enforcement resources, not paper provisions.

## Can it bind the platforms — or just expand state power?

Worth warning: the politically unassailable banner of child protection is being leveraged by some states to expand control over digital space. Analysts note that in polities with weaker safeguards for privacy, free expression and civic participation, tightening platforms in the name of safety often quietly tightens the space for public debate. Indonesia's proposed fine framework even lets one ministry "write the rules, judge compliance and impose the sanction" — raising questions of due process — while blocking sites "hurts Indonesian users more than the companies." When regulation becomes an extension of power, the original purpose of protection can be quietly displaced.

## The price of digital sovereignty

Southeast Asia's collective push is, at heart, an exercise in "digital sovereignty": making giants headquartered in California answer for local children's welfare. The direction is defensible. But the flip side is that market size dictates how much a giant will invest — research finds that among ASEAN's 11 member states, five lack even an in-country representative for the major social companies, leaving regulators and civil society with no real channel to raise problems. Real protection cannot rest on a single ban; it requires enforceable age verification, transparent algorithmic accountability, and power boundaries that are not abused.

## Further reading

**Age verification**: Confirming a user's legal age via documents, biometrics or payment data; technically feasible yet often diluted in practice by privacy and misidentification disputes.
**Digital sovereignty**: A state's rule-making authority over data, platforms and cyberspace within its borders; child online safety is fast becoming its most direct entry point.
**Safety-by-design**: Requiring platforms to embed child-protection measures at the product's inception rather than as afterthought; Indonesian civil society urges this over "voluntary commitments."
