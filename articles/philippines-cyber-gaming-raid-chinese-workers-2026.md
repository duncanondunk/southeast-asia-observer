# 菲律宾今年最大规模网络博彩扫荡：244人落网，绝大多数为中国籍——一道禁令如何把诈骗经济逼入地下

10月2日，菲律宾移民局、国家调查局与军方突袭了南部棉兰老岛（Mindanao）三宝颜锡布格省（Zamboanga Sibugay）的两处窝点。行动结束时，244名外籍人员被拘，其中绝大多数是中国公民。菲律宾移民局局长乔尔·安东尼奥·维亚多（Joel Anthony Viado）称这是「2026年最大规模的一次网络博彩（POGO）扫荡」。值得注意的不仅是规模，还有落网者的身份构成。

## 数字：2026年最大规模的一次扫荡

第一处突袭位于 Siay 镇 Kimos 村，捕获113名外籍人员：110名中国籍、2名柬埔寨籍、1名香港居民。第二处在 Alicia 镇 Tandiong Muslim 村，又抓了131人，「大多是中国人」，其余包括马来西亚、印度尼西亚和缅甸公民。执法部门在 Casa de Coco 旅社大院起获78台电脑显示器、约3000部手机、笔记本电脑和SIM卡。移民局称，这些设备指向「一处有组织的非法线上运营点」。部分被捕者正因涉嫌人口贩运接受调查，其余将被驱逐出境并列入移民黑名单。

## 禁令之后：野火燎原，而非灰飞烟灭

菲律宾总统小马科斯（Ferdinand Marcos Jr.）在2024年国情咨文中宣布取缔菲律宾离岸网络博彩（POGO），同年11月以第74号行政令（Executive Order 74）将禁令成文化，并要求年底前停止运营。国会随后通过2025年《反网络博彩法》（Republic Act 12312）。逻辑很明确：犯罪集团把网络博彩当作掩护，从事人口贩运、洗钱、网络诈骗、绑架与谋杀。禁令的目的，是关上这扇门。

然而10月的突袭表明，这扇门只是被重新上锁，生意转入了暗处。禁令实施两年之后出现「2026年最大规模扫荡」，这并非执法的功绩，而是证明：禁令催生了一个地下经济，而非消灭了一个产业。当一项利润丰厚、劳动密集的活动被定性为非法、需求却依然存在，经营者不会消失——他们只是迁往偏远省份、撕掉牌照文书、继续运转。三宝颜锡布格省是棉兰老岛一块贫穷的农业角落，远离马尼拉的监管视线，正是被禁产业最理想的藏身之所。

## 中国籍员工的悖论：既是劳力，也是筹码

看看照片里那些面孔，一个应当令所有研究东南亚华侨华人的人警惕的模式浮现出来：被捕者「大多是中国人」。这绝非偶然。多年来，网络博彩大量依赖来自中国大陆的普通话员工，岗位从客源招募一直延伸到后台运营。当合法产业关门，许多员工并未回国——他们滑入非法的残余地带，往往身不由己，有时正是被禁令本要摧毁的那些招聘网络所欺骗。

中国驻马尼拉使馆的反应克制而典型。发言人季凌鹏（Ji Lingpeng）表示，中方正「就案件细节与菲律宾执法部门保持沟通」，并提醒「中国法律禁止任何形式的赌博，包括中国公民在海外参与赌博或受雇于博彩公司」。这句话是外交辞令，却也悄悄承认一个严峻事实：马尼拉执法网中捞起的相当部分，正是中国自己的公民，他们困在一个跨越国界、使馆无力监管的灰色经济里。

## 区域共病：诈骗工业园区的幽灵

把镜头拉远，棉兰老岛的突袭更像一则东南亚故事，而非单纯的菲律宾故事。联合国2023年的一份报告估计，缅甸至少有12万人、柬埔寨约10万人可能被困在诈骗运营点中，而在老挝、菲律宾、泰国，犯罪集团经营的企业从加密货币诈骗一直延伸到网络博彩。菲律宾从来就是这张地图的一部分，而非例外。2024年之后改变的，不是诈骗经济是否存在，而是它是否可见：从持牌高楼被赶进旅社和乡村院落，它变得更难看见，也更容易被无视。

这才是真正的区域教训。「诈骗工业园区」并没有因某个国家的一纸禁令而消失，它只是漂移——漂向执法松弛、劳力廉价、官员可被收买的地方。单边禁令无论多么出于原则，都解不了跨国难题，它只能把问题挪个位置。

## 禁令能消除需求吗？

马尼拉真正该问的，不是还能再抓244人，而是单靠禁令能否触及根源。网络博彩与诈骗运营依靠一个简单等式：菲律宾境外的庞大需求、境内外愿意（或被困）的劳力、以及薄弱的本地监管。禁掉「POGO」这个名号，打击的是招牌，不是生意。Casa de Coco 大院不是旧产业的残骸，而是禁令本身孕育出的新产业。

这里有一个值得直说的、关乎人道代价的反讽。2024年的禁令以「保护受害者」为名——保护那些遭贩运、被诈骗、受暴力侵犯的人。但把产业逼入地下，反而可能让劳动者更脆弱，而非更安全。那些正因涉嫌人口贩运接受调查的人，许多恰恰是禁令本要拯救的对象。一项在受害者更深陷阴影时宣告胜利的政策，并没有保护任何人。2026年最大规模的那次扫荡，只是一个症状；药方，尚未写就。

## 延伸阅读

**菲律宾离岸网络博彩（POGO）**：面向菲律宾境外玩家营运的持牌线上博彩企业；2024年被小马科斯总统以涉有组织犯罪、洗钱与贩运为由取缔。

**人口贩运（Human Trafficking）**：以暴力或欺骗手段招募、运送或窝藏他人；菲方正调查244名被捕者中是否有人实为贩运受害者，而非自愿务工。

**反网络博彩法（Anti-POGO Act of 2025 / RA 12312）**：将2024年行政禁令成文化的法律，在产业清退后对网络博彩运营定罪并加重处罚。

# Manila's largest POGO bust of 2026 nets 244, mostly Chinese — a ban that pushed the scam economy underground

On 2 October, Philippine immigration agents, the National Bureau of Investigation and the armed forces raided two compounds in Zamboanga Sibugay on the southern island of Mindanao. By the time the operation ended, 244 foreign nationals were in custody — and the overwhelming majority were Chinese citizens. Immigration chief Joel Anthony Viado called it "the largest POGO bust of 2026." The scale is not the only thing worth noting. So is the profile of the people caught in it.

## The numbers: the largest bust of 2026

The first raid, at Barangay Kimos in Siay, netted 113 foreigners: 110 Chinese nationals, two Cambodians and one Hong Kong resident. The second, at Barangay Tandiong Muslim in Alicia, took another 131 — "mostly Chinese," with Malaysians, Indonesians and Burmese among them. Authorities seized 78 computer monitors, roughly 3,000 mobile phones, laptops and SIM cards from the Casa de Coco Pension House compound. The equipment, the bureau said, pointed to "an organised illegal online operation." Some of those detained are now being investigated for possible human-trafficking offences; the rest face deportation and a place on the immigration blacklist.

## After the ban: underground, not gone

President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. outlawed Philippine Offshore Gaming Operators in his 2024 State of the Nation Address, formalised the prohibition through Executive Order 74 that November, and ordered operations to cease by year's end. Congress followed with the Anti-POGO Act of 2025 (Republic Act 12312). The logic was clear: criminal networks had used POGOs as cover for human trafficking, money laundering, online fraud, kidnapping and murder. Banning them was meant to close the door.

Yet the October raids suggest the door was merely relocked and the business moved into the shadows. A "largest bust of 2026" two years after the ban is not evidence of success; it is evidence that the prohibition created an underground economy rather than erasing one. When a lucrative, labour-intensive activity is outlawed but demand persists, the operators do not disappear — they decamp to remote provinces, shed the licensing paperwork, and keep running. Zamboanga Sibugay, a poor agricultural corner of Mindanao far from Manila's regulatory gaze, is exactly the kind of place where a banned industry goes to hide.

## The Chinese-worker paradox: labour and leverage

Look at the faces in the photographs and a pattern emerges that should trouble anyone who studies Southeast Asia's Chinese diaspora. The workers are "mostly Chinese." This is no accident. For years, POGOs drew heavily on Mandarin-speaking staff from the mainland for roles that ranged from customer recruitment to backend operations. When the legal industry closed, many of those workers did not go home — they migrated into the illegal residue, often under coercion, sometimes deceived by the very recruitment networks the ban was supposed to dismantle.

The Chinese embassy in Manila responded with characteristic restraint. Spokesman Ji Lingpeng said Beijing was "reaching out to the Philippine law enforcement agencies on the details of this case," and reminded that "Chinese law prohibits any form of gambling, including Chinese nationals gambling overseas or working for gambling companies." The statement is diplomatic boilerplate, but it quietly concedes a hard truth: a significant share of the people swept up in Manila's enforcement dragnet are China's own citizens, caught in a grey economy that spans borders the embassy cannot police.

## A regional malaise: the ghost of scam industrial parks

Zoom out and the Mindanao raids look less like a Philippine story than a Southeast Asian one. A 2023 United Nations report estimated that at least 120,000 people may be trapped in scam operations in Myanmar and roughly 100,000 in Cambodia, with criminal-owned enterprises in Laos, the Philippines and Thailand running everything from crypto-fraud to online gambling. The Philippines was always part of this map, not an exception to it. What changed after 2024 was not the existence of the scam economy but its visibility: pushed from licensed towers into pension houses and provincial compounds, it became harder to see and easier to ignore.

That is the real regional lesson. The "scam industrial park" did not vanish when one country banned it. It drifted — to wherever enforcement is lax, labour is cheap, and officials can be bought. A unilateral ban, however principled, cannot solve a transnational problem. It can only move it.

## Can a ban erase demand?

The question Manila should be asking is not whether it can arrest 244 more people, but whether prohibition alone ever reaches the root. Online gambling and scam operations thrive on a simple equation: massive demand from outside the Philippines, willing (or trapped) labour from inside and abroad, and weak local oversight. Banning the label "POGO" attacks the brand, not the business. The Casa de Coco compound was not a relic of the old industry; it was the new one, born of the ban itself.

There is a human-cost irony here that deserves blunt stating. The 2024 prohibition was justified in the name of protecting victims — of trafficking, of fraud, of violence. But by driving the industry underground, it may have made the workers more vulnerable, not less. Those now under investigation for human trafficking were, in many cases, the same people the ban was meant to save. A policy that declares victory while leaving the vulnerable deeper in the shadows has not protected anyone. The largest bust of 2026 is a symptom. The cure has yet to be written.

## Further reading

**Philippine Offshore Gaming Operators (POGOs)** — Licensed online gaming firms that served punters outside the Philippines; banned by President Marcos in 2024 over links to organised crime, money laundering and trafficking.

**Human trafficking** — The recruitment, movement or harbouring of people by force or deception; Philippine authorities are probing whether some of the 244 detainees were trafficking victims rather than willing workers.

**Anti-POGO Act of 2025 (Republic Act 12312)** — The law that codified the 2024 executive ban, criminalising POGO operations and tightening penalties after the sector was wound down.
