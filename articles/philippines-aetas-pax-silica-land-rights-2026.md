# 三十比索一平方米：当人工智能枢纽遇上菲律宾原住民的祖地

据《南华早报（South China Morning Post）》报道，在菲律宾打拉省新克拉克市（New Clark City）连绵的草地上，部落首领佩特罗尼拉·穆尼奥斯（Petronila Munoz）带着族人一遍遍走过他们世代栖居的土地。眼下，一座由美国主导的“硅和平”（Pax Silica）高科技枢纽正从这片草原上生长出来，而约1000名埃塔族（Aeta）原住民，则被悄然推到了失去祖地的边缘。他们拿到的，是一纸要求12月前搬离、迁往16公里外、却连受影响范围都没画清的“通知”；以及一份每平方米30比索（约0.5美元，约合3.6元人民币）的征地报价——所有人都拒绝了。

这不是孤例，而是一道横在“人工智能黄金时代”与“普通人的家园”之间的裂痕。

## 现场：一片没有答案的草原

穆尼奥斯在部落的传统议事厅里对媒体说：“还是没说清楚。我们会怎样？这是我们最大的疑问。”事实上，埃塔族人对自家土地究竟有多少会被划走、未来的生计谁来接住，至今一无所知。今年7月，基地转换与发展管理局（BCDA）寄来一封信，措辞谨慎地提醒：项目“可能影响你们所居住的土地”。随后上门的工作人员告诉他们，12月前必须离开，迁往约16公里外，却始终没有拿出一份标明受影响区域的总体规划。

一句“我们希望你们听一听我们”，道尽了这场对话的失衡：在埃塔人看来，决策者正在自说自话。

## 数字：700亿美元与30比索的落差

一边是宏大的国家叙事。今年4月，美国宣布菲律宾加入“硅和平”倡议——一个旨在强化半导体与稀土供应链、降低参与国对中国依赖的框架。菲政府为新克拉克市划出1620公顷（约4000英亩）地块，目标是生产用于人工智能数据中心的高端组件，预计可吸引高达700亿美元（约合5040亿元人民币）投资，创造约19万个就业岗位。对一个失业人口超过300万的国家而言，这无疑是诱人的。

另一边，却是刺眼的反差。基地转换与发展管理局给埃塔人的补偿报价是每平方米30比索（约0.5美元，约合3.6元人民币）——约合每10平方英尺50美分。穆尼奥斯说得很直白：“我们的生命、生计和文化都系在这片祖地上。土地更重要，钱总会花完的。”

一边是700亿美元的蓝图，一边是30比索一平方米的报价；一边许诺19万个岗位，一边是族人对“我们够不够格去干那些新工作”的担忧。这落差，本身就是一句无声的评论。

## 一张迟到了四十年的地契

更深的症结在于：和菲律宾许多原住民群体一样，埃塔人没有土地所有权证书。在8月的参议院听证会上，基地转换与发展管理局总裁约书亚·宾仓（Joshua Bingcang）称埃塔人“住在政府土地上”。可事实上，这个名为萨潘格·卡瓦扬（Sapang Kawayan）的定居点，自1984年起就在向国家原住民事务委员会（NCIP）申请“祖域权状”（Ancestral Domain Title），最早的一份背书早在1994年就已发出，却至今没有落地。

一边是几十年悬而未决的产权申请，一边是迫不及待推进的征地通知。当一份地契等了四十年仍未到手，所谓的“政府土地”叙事，就难免显得草率。埃塔人的诉求其实很简单：我们不是要反对发展，我们要的是被听见、被算进账里。

## 水的焦虑与电的缺口

即便抛开产权之争，这座枢纽自身的资源账也经不起细看。公民团体反复警告：在常年受干旱和轮流停电困扰的菲律宾，一个占地1620公顷、以芯片与人工智能数据中心为卖点的工业带，对水电的需求将是天文数字。基地转换与发展管理局在参议院承诺，会要求入驻投资者自建水电系统——可在一个连居民用电都难以稳定的国家，这套说辞恐怕很难让人安心。

更具反讽意味的是，美国国务院在回应时表示，环境、水资源、能源与社区影响评估“将影响项目设计”，并“预期在重大开发前进行严格的相关方影响评估”。可现实是，评估尚未开始，搬离通知已经下达。口头上的“严格评估”，与脚下已经踩下的征地油门，节奏并不一致。

## “去中国化”的算盘

为什么是菲律宾？研究供应链与风险评估的阿尔文·坎巴（Alvin Camba）点破了一层窗户纸：在东南亚，菲律宾受中国资本影响最小，因而最能让外资企业“与中国脱钩”。有报道称，华盛顿一度计划警告各国——若加入北京支持的竞争对手框架，就可能被排除在“硅和平”之外。

换句话说，菲律宾被选中的逻辑，与其说是本土产业的自然升级，不如说是地缘棋盘上“最干净的一块拼图”。当一座枢纽的合法性部分来自“对华脱钩”的叙事，它就很难只被当作一个经济项目来讨论。反对团体也指出，该项目位于吕宋经济走廊（Luzon Economic Corridor）内、毗邻军事设施，可能让菲律宾更深地卷入大国紧张——这，才是穆尼奥斯们真正担心的“未来”。

## 透明度赤字

即便是对“硅和平”持支持态度的政治经济学家卡利克斯托·奇基安科（Calixto Chikiamco）也直言：政府在向民众解释“这是什么、好处何在、如何保护地方社区、怎么解决水与电”上，“做得很糟糕”。坎巴同样建议：菲律宾政府必须对相关进展保持高度透明，“一个大问题就是透明度”。

透明度的缺口不止于话术。基地转换与发展管理局直接驳回了要求公开项目官方边界地图与地理空间数据的信息公开申请，称相关信息“在正式行动敲定或公布前暂予保留”。当一块可能决定千人命运的土地，其边界却成了“暂不便公开”的信息，所谓“利益相关方评估”，自然也就无从谈起。

## 谁为人工智能的黄金时代买单

说到底，这不止是埃塔人的故事，也不止是菲律宾的故事。当全球把人工智能视作下一个增长极，每一座数据中心、每一条半导体产线，最终都要落在某块具体的土地上，都要与某群具体的人发生冲撞。700亿美元的蓝图能否兑现、19万个岗位能否落地，尚且悬而未决；可祖地被划走的代价，却已经提前落在了最没有议价权的人头上。

穆尼奥斯说，她担心族人“够不够格”去干那些新工作。这句话里，藏着一个被宏大叙事轻轻略过的命题：发展的红利，会不会只流向能接住它的人，而把接不住的人留在原地、再请出原地？

一座枢纽的成败，不该只以投资规模和岗位数字来衡量。当“硅和平”遇上“三十比索一平方米”，真正该被听见的，或许正是那句朴素到近乎倔强的请求——“我们希望你们听一听我们”。

## 延伸阅读

**祖域权状（Ancestral Domain Title）**：菲律宾1997年《原住民法》确立的制度，承认原住民对祖传土地与领地的集体权利；申请往往耗时数十年，埃塔人自1984年申请、1994年获首份背书却至今未获正式权状。

**吕宋经济走廊（Luzon Economic Corridor）**：美国、日本与菲律宾于2024年推动的基建与投资框架，聚焦苏比克—克拉克—马尼拉—八打雁轴线；“硅和平”枢纽即坐落其内，使其兼具经济与地缘双重属性。

**关键矿产（Critical Minerals）**：稀土、镍、钴等支撑半导体与新能源的战略资源；“硅和平”被设计为绕开中国的关键矿产与芯片供应链节点，也是菲律宾对华“脱钩”叙事的核心抓手。

# 50 US cents a square metre: when an AI hub meets an indigenous tribe's ancestral land in the Philippines

Petronila Munoz has walked the rolling grasslands of New Clark City, in Tarlac province north of Manila, for as long as she can remember. As chieftain of the Aeta Hungey tribe, she now walks them with a question hanging over every step. A sprawling technology hub under the US-led Pax Silica initiative is taking shape on this land, and roughly 1,000 Aetas are being pushed toward the edge of dispossession. What they have received is a notice to vacate by December and relocate 16 kilometres away — with no master plan showing which parts of their ancestral domain will be taken — and a compensation offer of 30 pesos (about US$0.50) per square metre. Every one of them refused.

This is not an isolated footnote. It is a fault line running between the promised "AI gold rush" and the homes of people who were never asked.

## The scene: a grassland with no answers

"I still don't have clarity," Munoz told reporters inside the tribe's traditional meeting hall. "What will happen to us? That is our biggest question." The Aetas still do not know how much of their land will be affected, or who will catch them when their livelihood is gone. In July, the Bases Conversion and Development Authority (BCDA) sent a letter politely warning that the project "could affect your occupied lands." Visiting officials then told them to leave by December and move roughly 16km away, but never produced a map of the affected areas.

"All we ask is that they listen to us," Munoz said. "They are just talking among themselves." The words capture the asymmetry of the whole exchange.

## The numbers: US$70bn against 30 pesos

On one side sits the national narrative. In April, the United States announced that the Philippines had joined Pax Silica, a US-led effort to harden supply chains for semiconductors and rare earths and reduce participants' dependence on China. Manila designated a 1,620-hectare (4,000-acre) site in New Clark City to produce high-end components for artificial-intelligence data centres. The government estimates the initiative could draw up to US$70 billion in investment and create about 190,000 jobs — a tantalising prospect for a country with more than three million unemployed.

On the other side sits an uncomfortable contrast. The BCDA offered the Aetas just 30 pesos (about US$0.50) per square metre — roughly 50 US cents per 10 square feet. Munoz was blunt: "Our lives, our livelihood and our culture are tied to this ancestral land. Land is more important. Money runs out."

A US$70 billion blueprint on one side; a 30-peso-per-square-metre offer on the other. A promise of 190,000 jobs; a tribe worried its members "won't qualify" for them. The gap is itself a silent commentary.

## A land title four decades overdue

The deeper problem is that, like many indigenous groups in the Philippines, the Aetas hold no land title. At an August Senate hearing, BCDA president Joshua Bingcang said the Aetas were living on government land. Yet the community of Sitio Sapang Kawayan has been applying for an Ancestral Domain Title (ADT) with the National Commission on Indigenous Peoples since 1984; the first endorsement came in 1994 and has still not been converted into a final title.

A property claim left unresolved for forty years, and an eviction notice delivered in a hurry. When a title has waited four decades and still not arrived, the "government land" framing looks hasty at best. The Aetas' demand is modest: not to block development, but to be heard and counted.

## A thirsty hub on a fragile grid

Set aside the land dispute, and the hub's own resource maths is shaky. Civic groups warn that in a country plagued by droughts and rotational blackouts, a 1,620-hectare industrial belt built around chips and AI data centres will demand staggering volumes of water and electricity. Bingcang told the Senate that investors would be required to build their own power and water systems — a reassurance that sits uneasily in a nation that struggles to keep the lights on for residents.

There is a sting of irony here. The US State Department said environmental, water, energy and community-impact studies "would inform the project's design" and that it "expects rigorous stakeholder impact assessments" before major development. Yet the relocation notice has already been served before any assessment began. The spoken commitment to "rigorous" review and the accelerated eviction timetable are not moving at the same pace.

## The 'decoupling' calculus

Why the Philippines? Alvin Camba, a research director at an AI supply-chain risk firm, pulled back one layer of the curtain: among Southeast Asian states, the Philippines is the least exposed to Chinese capital, which lets foreign firms "disentangle" from China. Reports last year said Washington planned to warn countries that joining a Beijing-backed rival framework could see them excluded from Pax Silica.

In other words, the Philippines was chosen less for the natural upgrading of its own industry than as the "cleanest tile" on a geopolitical board. When a hub's legitimacy is partly borrowed from a "decouple from China" narrative, it is hard to discuss it as a purely economic project. Opposition groups note that the site sits inside the Luzon Economic Corridor and near military facilities, potentially drawing the Philippines deeper into great-power tension — which is precisely the "future" Munoz's people fear.

## A deficit of transparency

Even Calixto Chikiamco, a political economist who supports Pax Silica as a "historic opportunity" for the Philippines to move beyond its traditional wheelhouse of chip testing and packaging, conceded that the government is "doing a terrible job" explaining what the project is, what the benefits are, how local communities will be protected, and how the water and power problems will be solved. Camba agreed: Manila must be far more transparent, because "a big problem is transparency."

The transparency gap is not merely rhetorical. The BCDA rejected a freedom-of-information request for the project's official boundary map and geospatial data, saying the information was being "withheld temporarily until the official action is finalised or published." When the very boundaries that may decide a thousand people's fate are treated as "not yet convenient to disclose," the promised stakeholder assessment cannot meaningfully begin.

## Who pays for the AI gold rush

Ultimately this is not only the Aetas' story, nor only the Philippines'. As the world treats artificial intelligence as the next growth pole, every data centre and every semiconductor line must eventually land on a specific piece of ground and collide with specific people. Whether the US$70 billion blueprint materialises and the 190,000 jobs arrive remains uncertain; the cost of severed ancestral land, however, has already been charged to those with the least bargaining power.

Munoz worries her people "won't qualify" for the new jobs Pax Silica promises. Inside that worry sits a proposition the grand narrative quietly skips: do the dividends of development flow only to those equipped to catch them, leaving the rest in place — and then out of place?

The success of a hub should not be measured by investment size and job counts alone. When "Pax Silica" meets "30 pesos a square metre," what deserves to be heard may be that plain, almost stubborn request: "I wish they would listen to us."

## Further reading

**Ancestral Domain Title (ADT):** A collective land-rights instrument established under the Philippines' 1997 Indigenous Peoples' Rights Act, recognising indigenous claims to ancestral lands and territories. Applications routinely take decades — the Aetas first applied in 1984 and received an initial endorsement in 1994, yet no final title has been issued.

**Luzon Economic Corridor:** An infrastructure and investment framework pushed by the United States, Japan and the Philippines from 2024, focused on the Subic–Clark–Manila–Batangas axis. The Pax Silica hub sits within it, giving the project both economic and geopolitical weight.

**Critical minerals:** Strategic resources — rare earths, nickel, cobalt — underpinning semiconductors and clean energy. Pax Silica is designed as a China-circumventing node for critical-mineral and chip supply chains, and is central to Manila's "decoupling" narrative.
