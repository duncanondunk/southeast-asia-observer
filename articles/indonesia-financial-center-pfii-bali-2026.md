# 印尼的280亿美元赌注：雅加达与巴厘岛能否从新加坡手中撬走财富管理？

2026年7月21日，印尼国会一致通过《印度尼西亚国际金融中心法》（简称PFII），为雅加达和巴厘岛打造世界级金融、投资、金融科技与仲裁中心扫清法律障碍。据《日经亚洲》（Nikkei Asia）近日报道，这个被官方估值为300万亿至500万亿印尼盾（约167亿至278亿美元，约合1190亿至1980亿元人民币）的蓝图，正把印尼——这个长期被新加坡"吸走"资本与富豪的东南亚最大经济体——推上区域金融枢纽的角力场。问题是，一张法律条文换不来信任，迪拜的模板也不是谁都能复制的。

## 迟到的入场券：雅加达—巴厘岛金融中心法落地

印尼不是第一个想做区域金融中心的国家。马来西亚有纳闽，泰国有投资促进委员会（BOI）框架，新加坡更是坐拥数十年积累的财富管理霸主地位。但印尼的体量摆在那里：作为东盟最大经济体、G20成员，它却始终没有一个对等的金融门户，本国富豪的家族办公室与离岸资金常年盘踞新加坡。普拉博沃（Prabowo Subianto）总统把PFII列为"十项重大转型"之一，意图再明显不过——让全球资本在印尼找到确定性，让印尼财富的定价与交易留在本国。

法律的落地速度倒也够快。从6月17日《金融部门发展与强化法》（P2SK法）生效，到7月21日PFII法在国会全会一致通过，前后不过月余。财政部长普尔巴亚直言，作为东南亚最大经济体，印尼"已经到了建设具有全球竞争力国际金融中心的时候"。

## 数字：300万亿至500万亿印尼盾的算盘

钱从哪来、能引来多少，是这套叙事的核心。财政部官员赫尔曼·萨赫鲁丁7月8日宣布，PFII有望吸引300万亿至500万亿印尼盾（约167亿至278亿美元）的外国投资。雅加达先期临时运营，主枢纽落在巴厘岛库拉库拉经济特区、登巴萨附近的蛇岛（Serangan Island）。主理方是印尼主权财富基金达拉塔拉（Danantara），由其承担基础设施建设，再另行任命中心主任与"行长"搭建治理架构。

说白了，这套算盘打的是"印尼人自己的钱"。印尼政府反复强调，PFII要吸的是境外资本，而非国内既有的流动性，以免抽干本土金融体系。一个被广泛引用的细节是：财政部长曾透露，从海外进入该区域的资金可免征印尼税。再加上可能的全额企业所得税豁免、增值税减免，以及对国际金融机构更熟悉的共同法（common law）原则——这套组合拳，活脱脱是照着迪拜国际金融中心（DIFC）画的。

## 迪拜模板：自贸区与离岸律法

迪拜国际金融中心为何成了东南亚多国的"梦中情模"？关键在于它把"境内关外"玩到了极致：独立的法律框架、专属法院与仲裁机构、英语普通法体系、近乎为零的税率。印尼想复制的，正是这种"飞地式"的制度红利——在雅加达和巴厘岛划出一块不受本土繁杂法规掣肘的金融飞地，用确定性换取全球银行、资产管理公司、金融科技企业与家族办公室的入驻。

但模板易抄、信用难建。国际金融中心的命门从来不是楼有多高、税有多低，而是监管是否透明、合同能否执行、反洗钱与反恐融资（AML/CFT）是否过硬。一个低税飞地若缺乏严密监督，很容易被贴上"保密管辖区"而非"合法枢纽"的标签。印尼已加入针对大型跨国企业的15%全球最低税体系，PFII的特殊待遇如何与之相容，仍是悬而未决的难题。

## 向新加坡叫板：财富管理的蛋糕怎么分

这才是真正的好戏。新加坡能成为东南亚财富管理的"抽水机"，靠的不是运气，而是数十年法治积累、货币稳定与中立地缘位置。如今印尼要正面抢食，底气来自体量：2.8亿人口、庞大的侨汇与华商网络、以及正快速积累的本土财富。普拉博沃的算盘里，PFII既是资本回流的管道，也是把"印尼定价权"攥回手里的抓手。

可现实往往比口号骨感。新加坡的护城河是沉淀了几十年的信任与生态——从私人银行家到基金行政、从法律到税务顾问，整套产业链不是两三年能搬家的。印尼许诺的两到三年筹备期，更像是把目标先立起来，而非真正实现"世界级"。更别提，真要分蛋糕，先得回答：印尼的法治确定性、汇率稳定性与政治可预期性，凭什么让家族办公室把真金白银从新加坡挪过来？

## 中国资本的角力场

在这场区域金融重构里，中国的位置微妙而关键。印尼是中国"一带一路"投融资与镍、电动汽车产业链的重要落脚点，中资银行与金融科技企业早已深度布局。PFII若落地，完全可能成为人民币计价金融与中资财富管理出海的新通道——尤其是在西方对俄制裁外溢、美元体系波动的背景下，一个非西方主导、又对接全球标准的金融飞地，对北京颇有吸引力。

但这也是一盘需要平衡的棋。普拉博沃的中立不结盟姿态，让印尼得以同时向中美两边招手；可一旦PFII把中资与西方资本并置，监管的中立性与地缘敏感度就会上升。对雅加达而言，金融中心既是经济议题，也是大国博弈的试纸。

## 巴厘岛的反噬：避税港疑虑与环境代价

把主枢纽放在巴厘岛，是这套方案最具争议的一笔。这个以神庙、梯田与海岸闻名的岛屿，早已被旅游与高端地产压得喘不过气：交通拥堵、供水紧张、垃圾处理能力不足。如今再叠加一个面向全球富豪与基金的低税金融飞地，土地与租金必然进一步攀升，本地靠工薪生活的居民恐被进一步挤出。

批评者直言不讳：PFII会不会把巴厘岛变成又一处"避税港"？经济红利能否惠及本地社区，还是只肥了开发商、地主与高薪外籍专业人士？反对声音同样指向监管——若不配套强有力的本地培训与监督，高薪岗位大概率先被外籍法律、金融与合规人才占据，巴厘岛工人仍困在建筑施工、酒店与服务业。一个没有严密监督的低税中心，风险不止于经济，更可能侵蚀这座岛屿本就脆弱的文化与生态底色。

## 落地难：监管信用与最低税的两道坎

回到最朴素的问题：蓝图很丰满，落地很难。印尼要的从来不是又一片写字楼，而是能向全球资本"背书"的信用。PFII能否成事，取决于三件事——监管是否真独立于国内政治的起伏、最低税承诺能否兑现、以及本地人才体系能否撑起一个世界级枢纽的运营。

《日经亚洲》把这场赌注形容为印尼的"280亿美元下注"。但280亿是预期，不是到账；法律是起点，不是终点。对雅加达而言，真正的考题不是"要不要做金融中心"，而是"凭什么让人信你"。这道题，迪拜用了二十年，新加坡用了更久。印尼想用两三年走完，恐怕还是乐观了些。

## 延伸阅读

**国际金融中心（Financial Centre）**：指在某一区域内聚集大量银行、资产管理、保险与金融科技机构，并提供跨境资本集散、定价与仲裁服务的城市或特区，典型如新加坡、香港、迪拜。

**迪拜国际金融中心（DIFC）**：阿联酋以普通法体系、独立法院与近零税率打造的"境内关外"金融飞地，是亚洲多国规划金融中心时的参照模板。

**金融部门发展与强化法（P2SK法）**：印尼2026年6月生效的金融基本法，为PFII的设立与立法时限提供了法律母本。

# Indonesia's $28bn gamble: can Jakarta and Bali prise wealth management from Singapore?

On 21 July 2026, Indonesia's House of Representatives unanimously passed the Indonesia International Financial Center (PFII) Law, clearing the legal path for world-class financial, investment, fintech and arbitration hubs in Jakarta and Bali. Nikkei Asia recently reported that the blueprint — officially valued at between 300 trillion and 500 trillion rupiah (about US$16.7 billion to US$27.8 billion) — is pushing Indonesia, the region's largest economy that has long watched its capital and wealthy citizens gravitate to Singapore, squarely into the contest for regional financial hub status. The trouble is that legislation alone cannot manufacture trust, and the Dubai template is not something everyone can copy.

## A late entry ticket: the Jakarta–Bali financial center law lands

Indonesia is hardly the first Southeast Asian country to want a financial center. Malaysia has Labuan, Thailand runs its Board of Investment (BOI) framework, and Singapore has spent decades cementing its dominance in wealth management. Yet Indonesia's heft is undeniable: as ASEAN's largest economy and a G20 member, it has never had a commensurate financial gateway, and its tycoons' family offices and offshore money have long parked in Singapore. President Prabowo Subianto has folded the PFII into his "ten transformations", and the intent is plain — give global capital certainty inside Indonesia, and keep the pricing and trading of Indonesian wealth at home.

The law moved quickly enough. From the 17 June entry into force of the Financial Sector Development and Strengthening (P2SK) Law to the PFII bill's unanimous passage on 21 July was barely a month. Finance Minister Purbaya Yudhi Sadewa argued that, as the largest economy in Southeast Asia, Indonesia "has reached the time to build a globally competitive international financial center".

## The numbers: a 300–500 trillion rupiah wager

Where the money comes from, and how much can be attracted, sits at the heart of the narrative. Finance Ministry official Herman Saheruddin announced on 8 July that the PFII could draw foreign investment of 300 trillion to 500 trillion rupiah (about US$16.7 billion to US$27.8 billion). Operations begin temporarily in Jakarta, while the main hub lands in the Kura Kura Special Economic Zone on Serangan Island near Denpasar, Bali. The master developer is Danantara, Indonesia's sovereign wealth fund, which builds the infrastructure, with a separately appointed center head and "governor" to erect the governance framework.

Stripped to essentials, this wager targets "Indonesians' own money". The government insists the PFII will pull in overseas capital rather than drain domestic liquidity, lest it suck the life out of the home financial system. A widely cited detail: the finance minister has said money entering the zone from abroad could be exempt from Indonesian tax. Add possible full corporate income tax exemptions, VAT relief, and common-law principles more familiar to international institutions, and the package looks like a near-carbon copy of the Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC).

## The Dubai template: a free zone with offshore law

Why has the DIFC become the model that so many Asian governments fantasise about? Because it pushes the "onshore–offshore" logic to its limit: an independent legal framework, dedicated courts and arbitration, an English common-law system, and near-zero tax. What Indonesia wants to replicate is precisely this "enclave" dividend — carving a financial free zone in Jakarta and Bali insulated from domestic regulatory tangles, trading certainty for the presence of global banks, asset managers, fintech firms and family offices.

But templates are easy to copy and credibility is hard to build. The lifeblood of an international financial center has never been how tall the towers are or how low the tax; it is whether supervision is transparent, contracts are enforceable, and anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorism-financing (AML/CFT) controls are robust. A low-tax enclave without rigorous oversight risks being branded a "secrecy jurisdiction" rather than a legitimate hub. Indonesia has also signed onto the 15% global minimum tax for large multinationals, and how the PFII's special treatment squares with that commitment remains an open question.

## Throwing down the gauntlet at Singapore: how to split the wealth-management pie

This is where the real drama lies. Singapore became Southeast Asia's wealth-management "vacuum cleaner" not by luck but through decades of legal accumulation, currency stability and geopolitical neutrality. Indonesia now wants to eat directly into that, and its confidence comes from scale: 280 million people, a vast remittance and ethnic-Chinese business network, and rapidly accumulating domestic wealth. In Prabowo's calculus, the PFII is both a channel to onshore capital and a lever to reclaim "Indonesian pricing power".

Reality, however, is more skeletal than the slogan. Singapore's moat is the trust and ecosystem built over decades — from private bankers to fund administrators, from lawyers to tax advisers — an entire value chain that cannot be relocated in two or three years. Indonesia's promised two-to-three-year preparation period looks more like planting a flag than delivering a "world-class" center. And before the cake can be cut, a harder question must be answered: with what, exactly, does Indonesia's legal certainty, exchange-rate stability and political predictability persuade family offices to move real money out of Singapore?

## A contest ground for Chinese capital

In this regional financial reordering, China's position is delicate and pivotal. Indonesia is a key node for Beijing's Belt and Road financing and its nickel and electric-vehicle supply chains, and Chinese banks and fintech firms are already deeply entrenched. Should the PFII materialise, it could well become a new conduit for RMB-denominated finance and Chinese wealth-management expansion overseas — especially as Western sanctions spill over and the dollar system wobbles, a non-Western-led financial enclave that still plugs into global standards holds clear appeal for Beijing.

But this is also a balancing act. Prabowo's non-aligned posture lets Indonesia court both Washington and Beijing; yet once the PFII places Chinese and Western capital side by side, the neutrality of supervision and geopolitical sensitivity rise sharply. For Jakarta, a financial center is as much a geopolitical litmus test as an economic project.

## Bali's blowback: tax-haven fears and environmental costs

Placing the main hub in Bali is the most contested stroke of the plan. An island celebrated for its temples, terraced rice fields and coastline is already suffocating under tourism and high-end property: traffic gridlock, strained water supplies, inadequate waste handling. Layering a low-tax financial enclave for global tycoons and funds on top of that will inevitably push land and rents higher, squeezing out residents who live on local wages.

Critics put it bluntly: will the PFII turn Bali into yet another "tax haven"? Will the economic gains reach local communities, or enrich only developers, landowners and highly paid foreign professionals? The pushback also targets governance — without strong local training and oversight, the best-paid jobs in an international hub will likely go first to foreign legal, financial and compliance talent, leaving Balinese workers stuck in construction, hospitality and support services. A low-tax center without rigorous supervision risks more than economics; it can erode the fragile cultural and ecological fabric that makes the island attractive in the first place.

## Hard to deliver: regulatory credibility and the minimum-tax hurdle

Back to the simplest question: the blueprint is plump, but delivery is hard. What Indonesia needs is not another cluster of office towers but the credibility to "endorse" itself to global capital. Whether the PFII succeeds hinges on three things — whether supervision is genuinely insulated from domestic political swings, whether the minimum-tax commitment holds, and whether a local talent system can sustain a world-class hub's operations.

Nikkei Asia frames this wager as Indonesia's "$28bn bet". But US$28 billion is an expectation, not a deposit; the law is a start, not a finish. For Jakarta, the real test is not "should we build a financial center" but "why should anyone trust you". That question took Dubai two decades and Singapore far longer to answer. Indonesia's ambition to do it in two or three years may yet prove optimistic.

## Further reading

**Financial Centre**: A city or zone where banks, asset managers, insurers and fintech firms cluster to provide cross-border capital pooling, pricing and arbitration services — typified by Singapore, Hong Kong and Dubai.

**Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC)**: A UAE "onshore–offshore" financial enclave built on a common-law system, independent courts and near-zero tax, the template many Asian governments cite when planning their own centers.

**Financial Sector Development and Strengthening Law (P2SK Law)**: Indonesia's foundational financial statute effective June 2026, providing the legal parent for the PFII and its legislative deadline.
