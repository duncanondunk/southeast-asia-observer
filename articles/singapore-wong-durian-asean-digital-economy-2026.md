# 像榴梿一样：黄循财谈新加坡的生存术与东盟数码经济

“他们可能会这么做，但随后会发现新加坡就像榴梿一样——你可以挤压，但自己也会被刺痛。”

新加坡总理黄循财用这个比喻，回应外界对大国的压力如何传导到这座城市的疑问。10月13日，他在一场公开讲话中系统阐述了新加坡在能源、数码经济与区域合作上的应对思路。

## “可被挤压，也会刺痛对方”

榴梿的比喻之所以流传，是因为它精准。新加坡没有石油、没有天然气、没有腹地，却在全球供应链与金融网络中占据关键节点。对这样的小国而言，安全不来自体量，而来自“被替代的成本”。

黄循财的表述点明了这一逻辑：新加坡要做的，是持续提升自身的不可替代性，让任何试图挤压它的力量都要付出代价。

## 能源：没有资源，却有炼油能力

谈到新加坡作为能源枢纽的角色，黄循财指出，近期中东危机凸显了新加坡虽没有石油和天然气资源，却拥有炼油能力和多元化能源供应渠道的优势。

他说，新加坡仍能从世界各地、包括其他地区取得能源供应，不仅满足本地需求，也继续向本区域国家供应油气。他预计，能源价格将在较长一段时间内维持高位，但新加坡应该仍能取得能源供应，并找到应对之道。

值得注意的是他对霍尔木兹海峡危机的解读。尽管外界原本担心海峡关闭会导致全球能源供应严重短缺，各方仍设法调整并适应局势——这说明全球经济和能源市场的适应能力与韧性可能比预期更强。

## 电力与绿色转型的双重压力

真正的挑战来自另一个方向：人工智能。

黄循财坦言，面对AI带来的庞大电力需求，难点在于如何在满足能源需求的同时减少碳排放。这意味着新加坡的能源议题不再只是“买得到”，而是“买得到、还干净”。

他的答案是两条腿走路：一方面协助本区域具备绿色能源发展潜力的项目取得融资，另一方面加快落实东南亚电网计划——把邻国的水电、太阳能接入区域网络，为城市国家腾出减碳空间。

## 东盟数码经济框架协议：11月见

如果说能源是防御，数码经济就是进攻。

黄循财透露，《东盟数码经济框架协议》预计于11月东盟峰会期间签署。它将成为全球首个区域数码经济框架协议，为推动数码贸易以及支持安全可信的跨境数据流动，确定共同规则和框架。

目前东盟各国采用不同的规则、系统和要求。黄循财希望协议落实后能降低贸易壁垒、促进数据流通，让跨境数码贸易更加便利。最终目标是让企业更顺畅地接触本区域各地的客户，让跨境支付更便捷，并降低交易成本。

## 不要一个封闭的数码系统

黄循财同时划了一条线。

他说，东盟不希望建立封闭的数码系统，而是要“保持开放，自主选择与世界各地不同的系统与合作伙伴对接”。在他看来，这正是新加坡的价值所在。

这句话针对的是当下越来越明显的技术阵营化趋势。对东盟而言，真正的风险不是选错了阵营，而是被迫二选一——一旦区域被迫在一个封闭体系内运行，像新加坡这样靠连接吃饭的经济体就会被削弱。

## 2027：轮值主席国与议程的连续性

新加坡将在2027年接任东盟轮值主席国。黄循财表示，届时将探讨如何推动其中一部分倡议。

轮值主席国的意义，正在于把一年的表态变成多年的议程。数码经济框架协议若在11月签署，2027年的任务就是把它从纸面推进到落地——标准互认、跨境支付清算、数据流动规则，每一项都需要有人推。

## 为什么是现在

这套思路之所以在此刻被集中提出，与外部环境的变化直接相关。

关税与出口管制正在把全球贸易切成若干区块，技术标准开始按阵营划线，能源则因中东局势而波动加剧。对高度依赖贸易与投资的新加坡来说，这三重压力同时到来。

黄循财的讲话因此更像一份路线图：在大国竞争从“摩擦”走向“制度化”的阶段，小国能做的不是选边，而是把规则、连接与信誉做成自己的护城河。

## 小国的方法论

把这场讲话串起来，可以看到一套清晰的小国方法论：在能源上分散风险、在规则上抢先落子、在阵营之间保持开放。

三者指向同一个目标——让自己成为别人绕不开的节点。榴梿的刺不是武器，而是谈判的筹码。对新加坡来说，这个筹码不是靠体量堆出来的，而是靠规则、连接与信誉一点点建起来的。

在一个大国竞争加剧的区域里，这或许是小国唯一可持续的活法。

## 延伸阅读

**东盟数码经济框架协议（ASEAN Digital Economy Framework Agreement, DEFA）**：东盟正在谈判的区域数码经济协定，旨在统一跨境数据流动、数码贸易与电子支付规则，预计成为全球首个区域层面的数码经济框架协议。

**东南亚电网（ASEAN Power Grid）**：旨在通过跨境输电线路连接东盟各国电力系统的长期计划，目标是让区域共享水电、太阳能等清洁能源，提升整体能源安全。

**不可替代性（indispensability）**：小国战略中的核心概念，指通过规则制定、枢纽地位与信誉积累，使自己成为他国难以绕开或低成本替代的对象。

# Like a Durian: Lawrence Wong on Singapore's Survival Strategy and ASEAN's Digital Economy

"They may do so, but they will then find that Singapore is like a durian — you can squeeze it, but you will get pricked yourself."

Singapore Prime Minister Lawrence Wong used that image to answer the question of how pressure from great powers transmits to this city-state. In a public address on October 13, he set out Singapore's thinking on energy, the digital economy and regional cooperation.

## "Can be squeezed, and will prick back"

The durian comparison travelled because it is precise. Singapore has no oil, no gas and no hinterland, yet occupies critical nodes in global supply chains and financial networks. For a small state, security does not come from size but from the cost of replacing it.

Wong's formulation makes the logic plain: Singapore's task is to keep raising its own indispensability, so that any power seeking to squeeze it must pay a price.

## Energy: no resources, but refining capacity

Turning to Singapore's role as an energy hub, Wong noted that the recent Middle East crisis had thrown into relief an advantage: though Singapore has no oil or gas resources of its own, it has refining capacity and diversified supply channels.

He said Singapore can still obtain energy from around the world, including from other regions, not only to meet local demand but to keep supplying oil and gas to regional countries. He expects energy prices to stay high for some time, but believes Singapore should still be able to secure supply and find ways to cope.

His reading of the Strait of Hormuz crisis is worth noting. Although there had been fears that a closure would cause severe global shortages, the parties managed to adjust and adapt — suggesting the adaptability and resilience of the global economy and energy market may be stronger than expected.

## The twin pressures of power demand and green transition

The real challenge comes from another direction: artificial intelligence.

Wong acknowledged that, with AI driving enormous electricity demand, the difficulty lies in meeting that demand while cutting carbon emissions. Singapore's energy question is no longer simply whether it can buy power, but whether it can buy clean power.

His answer runs on two tracks: helping regional projects with green-energy potential secure financing, and accelerating the ASEAN Power Grid — plugging neighbours' hydropower and solar into a regional network to free up decarbonisation space for the city-state.

## The ASEAN digital economy agreement: coming in November

If energy is defence, the digital economy is offence.

Wong disclosed that the ASEAN Digital Economy Framework Agreement is expected to be signed at the ASEAN summit in November. It would be the world's first regional digital economy framework agreement, setting common rules to advance digital trade and support secure, trusted cross-border data flows.

ASEAN states currently operate different rules, systems and requirements. Wong hopes the agreement will lower trade barriers, promote data flows and make cross-border digital trade easier, with the ultimate goal of letting businesses reach customers across the region more smoothly, making cross-border payments easier and cutting transaction costs.

## No closed digital system

Wong also drew a line.

He said ASEAN does not want a closed digital system but wants to "stay open, and to choose for ourselves which systems and partners around the world to connect with." That, in his view, is precisely where Singapore's value lies.

The remark targets an increasingly visible trend of technological bloc formation. For ASEAN, the real risk is not choosing the wrong camp but being forced to choose at all — once the region is pushed to operate inside one closed system, economies that live by connection, like Singapore, are weakened.

## 2027: the chairmanship and continuity of agenda

Singapore takes over the rotating ASEAN chairmanship in 2027. Wong said it will explore how to advance some of these initiatives then.

The point of a chairmanship is to turn one year's statements into a multi-year agenda. If the digital economy framework is signed in November, the 2027 task is to move it from paper to practice — mutual recognition of standards, cross-border payment settlement, data-flow rules; each needs someone to push it.

## Why now

The reason this thinking is being set out now is tied directly to a shifting external environment.

Tariffs and export controls are slicing global trade into blocs, technical standards are being drawn along camp lines, and energy is growing more volatile amid Middle East tensions. For Singapore, which depends heavily on trade and investment, these three pressures have arrived at once.

Wong's address therefore reads like a roadmap: at a stage when great-power competition is moving from friction towards institutionalisation, what a small state can do is not pick a side but turn rules, connections and credibility into a moat.

## A small state's method

Strung together, the speech reveals a clear small-state methodology: diversify risk in energy, move early on rules, stay open between camps.

All three point to the same objective — to become a node others cannot route around. The durian's spines are not a weapon but a bargaining chip. For Singapore that chip is not built from size; it is accumulated, piece by piece, through rules, connections and credibility.

In a region where great-power rivalry is intensifying, this may be the only sustainable way for a small state to live.

## Further reading

**ASEAN Digital Economy Framework Agreement (DEFA)**: A regional digital economy pact under negotiation among ASEAN members, aiming to harmonise rules on cross-border data flows, digital trade and e-payments. It is expected to be the world's first region-level digital economy framework agreement.

**ASEAN Power Grid**: A long-term plan to link the electricity systems of ASEAN countries through cross-border transmission lines, so the region can share hydropower, solar and other clean energy and improve overall energy security.

**Indispensability**: A core concept in small-state strategy — using rule-making, hub status and accumulated credibility to make oneself difficult for others to bypass or replace at low cost.
