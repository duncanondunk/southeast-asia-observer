# 从越南采购再生钨：三菱材料绕开中国管制的代价

《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》报道，日本三菱材料（Mitsubishi Materials）为规避中国对钨的出口管制，已开始从越南大量进口再生钨。这桩看似技术性的"中国+1（China Plus One）"供应链重组，暴露的不仅是成本，更是关键矿产（critical minerals）时代产业话语权的悄然迁移。

## 一组刺眼的反差

如果把日本钨供应链的版图摊开，最先撞进眼里的不是增长，而是两条反向奔跑的曲线。一边是中国份额的塌缩：日本从中国进口的三种受限钨相关产品，截至2026年8月较两年前同期骤降约90%；2026年前8个月，中国占日本钨进口的比重从2024年同期的47%锐减至13%。另一边是越南的跳升：同一窗口，越南占比从5%暴涨至20%，实现近4倍的增长。

同一段时间里，一进一退，把"去风险"四个字写得具象。当一个工业强国发现自己最硬的工具原料，有八成握在别人手里，它所做的第一个动作，必然是另寻炉灶。

## 背景：中国为何卡住钨

钨（tungsten）是一种极硬且耐高热的金属，是切削刀具、灯丝与电子零件的关键原料。中国在全球钨供应链中占据主导，据美国地质调查局（USGS）估计约占全球产量的80%。

2026年1月6日，中国对输往日本军事及民用用途的两用物项（Dual-Use）实施出口管制，受控清单包括碳化钨（tungsten carbide）、氧化钨与仲钨酸铵（ammonium paratungstate, APT）——后者正是钨冶炼的核心中间体。对依赖中国原料的日本制造商而言，这不是关税，而是供应的"开关"。

## 数字：被重塑的进口版图

三菱材料通过旗下日本新金属（Japan New Metals）子公司，计划把秋田（Akita）工厂以废料为原料的仲钨酸铵产能近乎翻倍，到2029年4月提升至年产2400吨（以三氧化钨当量计），以替代约1200吨的进口中间体。

而在当下，越南已是即战力。三菱材料从马山高科技材料（Masan High-Tech Materials）采购仲钨酸铵与氧化钨——后者运营位于太原省（Thái Nguyên）的弩坡（Núi Pháo）多金属矿与加工厂，具备处理国内外原料的能力。一条绕开中国的钨供应链，正在成型。

## 代价：价格与中小企业的挤压

绕路是有价的。据阿尔戈斯传媒（Argus Media）数据，国际市场的钨价较年初已上涨近3倍，远高于中国本土价格。受此影响，三菱材料自2026年6月1日起全面上调超硬刀具及零部件售价，部分产品涨幅甚至翻倍；日本另一巨头住友电工（Sumitomo Electric）也在年内完成第三次涨价。

真正承压的是产业链末端的中小刀具厂。大型企业尚能凭议价权拿到高价原料保障，而许多中小企业面对的是成本与订单的双重挤压——"中国+1"的账单，最终由它们默默付清。

## 越南的角色：从"替代品"到"枢纽"

越南并非临时救急。三菱材料此前向马山高科技材料战略投资9000万美元（约6.5亿元人民币），并于2024年底完成对马山旗下全球钨业务平台 H.C. Starck 的收购，双方签有长期供应协议。

但关系也在微调：三菱材料计划出清其在马山高科技材料10%的持股，作为投资组合重组的一部分；越南方面则因限制钨废料进口的监管约束，正着力优化本土运营。一来一往之间，越南从单纯的"替代来源"，逐渐变成区域钨加工的一极。

## 谁在买单：供应链重组的隐性成本

把镜头拉远，三菱材料的个案是更大趋势的缩影。为对冲中国关键矿产出口管制，日美企业纷纷在东南亚与本土重建回收与冶炼能力；美国更以《国防生产法》（Defense Production Act）下令，要求国内钨废料卖方在2027年8月前优先供应美国买家。

三菱材料截至2026年3月的财年合并营收微降约6%，但得益于精细化管理与产品提价，营业利润大幅增长63%，达605亿日元（约4.4亿元人民币）。这门生意短期赚了，但整个制造业为"去风险"付出的溢价，终将摊进每一把刀具、每一颗螺丝的成本里。

## 延伸阅读

**关键矿产（Critical Minerals）**：指对经济与国防具有战略意义、但供应高度集中或易受地缘扰动的关键原材料（如钨、稀土、锂、钴），其供应链安全已成为大国产业政策的优先议题。

**中国+1（China Plus One）**：企业在保留中国产能的同时，于第二国建立平行供应链，以规避关税、地缘与单一来源风险；东南亚（越南、泰国、马来西亚等）是主要承接方。

# Mitsubishi's Vietnam Tungsten Pivot: The Price of Bypassing China's Controls

Nikkei Asia reports that Japan's Mitsubishi Materials has begun importing recycled tungsten from Vietnam at scale, seeking to sidestep Chinese export restrictions on the critical metal. This seemingly technical "China Plus One" supply-chain reordering exposes not just cost, but a quiet migration of industrial leverage in the age of critical minerals.

## A stark reversal

Japan's tungsten supply map tells the story in two opposing curves. Imports of three restricted tungsten-related products from China fell roughly 90 per cent by August 2026 from two years earlier; in the first eight months of 2026, China's share of Japan's tungsten imports shrank from 47 per cent a year earlier to 13 per cent. Over the same window, Vietnam's share jumped from 5 per cent to 20 per cent — nearly a fourfold rise.

In a single stretch, one door closes and another opens, spelling out "de-risking" in concrete numbers. When an industrial power finds four-fifths of its hardest tooling feedstock in another's hands, the first instinct is inevitably to build a second furnace.

## Why China holds the tap

Tungsten is an exceptionally hard, heat-resistant metal essential to cutting tools, filaments and electronic components. China dominates the global supply chain, accounting for roughly 80 per cent of world production, the US Geological Survey estimates.

On 6 January 2026, China imposed export controls on dual-use items shipped to Japanese military and civilian users. The controlled list includes tungsten carbide, tungsten oxide and ammonium paratungstate (APT) — the core intermediate in tungsten refining. For Japanese manufacturers dependent on Chinese feedstock, this is not a tariff but an on-off switch.

## Reshaping the import map

Through its subsidiary Japan New Metals, Mitsubishi plans to nearly double scrap-fed APT capacity at its Akita plant to 2,400 tonnes a year (tungsten trioxide equivalent) by April 2029, replacing about 1,200 tonnes of imported intermediate annually.

In the meantime, Vietnam is the ready force. Mitsubishi sources APT and tungsten oxide from Masan High-Tech Materials, which operates the Núi Pháo polymetallic mine and processing facility in Thái Nguyên province with capacity to handle both domestic and imported feed. A tungsten chain that bypasses China is taking shape.

## The price tag

Rerouting carries a cost. Argus Media data show overseas tungsten prices have surged nearly threefold since the start of the year, far above Chinese domestic levels. Mitsubishi raised prices on carbide tools and parts from 1 June 2026, with some items doubling; peer Sumitomo Electric completed its third price hike of the year.

The real squeeze lands on smaller toolmakers at the chain's end. Large firms can still secure premium feedstock through bargaining power; many small and mid-sized shops face a double bind of cost and orders. The "China Plus One" bill is quietly settled by them.

## Vietnam's role: from substitute to hub

Vietnam is no stopgap. Mitsubishi earlier invested US$90 million in Masan High-Tech Materials and, at end-2024, acquired Masan's global tungsten platform H.C. Starck, signing a long-term supply agreement.

But the relationship is being fine-tuned: Mitsubishi plans to divest its entire 10 per cent stake in Masan, part of a portfolio restructuring, while Vietnam tightens rules limiting tungsten-scrap imports to optimise domestic operations. In the back-and-forth, Vietnam is evolving from a mere alternative source into a regional pole of tungsten processing.

## Who pays the hidden cost

Zoom out, and Mitsubishi's case mirrors a broader trend. To hedge against Chinese controls on critical minerals, Japanese and US firms are rebuilding recycling and refining capacity in Southeast Asia and at home; Washington has invoked the Defense Production Act to require domestic tungsten-scrap sellers to prioritise US buyers through August 2027.

In the fiscal year to March 2026, Mitsubishi's consolidated revenue dipped about 6 per cent, yet operating profit jumped 63 per cent to ¥60.5 billion (about US$4.1 billion) on tighter management and price hikes. The business won in the short run, but the premium the whole manufacturing sector pays for "de-risking" will eventually fold into the cost of every tool and screw.

## Further reading

**Critical Minerals**: Strategically vital raw materials whose supply is highly concentrated or geopolitically exposed (tungsten, rare earths, lithium, cobalt). Securing their supply chains has become a priority of great-power industrial policy.

**China Plus One**: Firms retain Chinese capacity while building a parallel supply chain in a second country to hedge tariffs, geopolitics and single-source risk; Southeast Asia (Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia) is the main recipient.
