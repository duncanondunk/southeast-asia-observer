# 谷歌Pixel全线撤离中国：「中国+1」走到分水岭

8月28日，越南通讯社（Vietnam+）转述日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）的报道，谷歌已正式通知供应商：自2027年起，把Pixel智能手机、Pixel Watch智能手表与Pixel Buds无线耳机三大终端的整机制造，全部从中国迁出。报道里最耐人寻味的，不是迁移本身，而是迁移的节奏——先把工艺最复杂的高端机跑通，再把门槛较低的手表与耳机一并打包带走。换言之，谷歌不是被「推」出来的，而是「先验证、后撤离」。在中美贸易摩擦未见缓和、出口管制不收反紧的背景下，这是一次早有预演的「去中国化」。问题是：它把整机制造搬去了哪里？又真正搬走了什么？

## 一位工程师坐在深圳工厂的最后一排

让我们设想一个画面：深圳宝安或东莞松山湖的某条SMT贴片线（Surface Mount Technology，表面贴装技术）末端，一位工艺工程师正紧盯一片Pixel主板的炉后良率（post-reflow yield）。他也许不知道，这块板的下一次量产，多半会出现在越南（Vietnam）北宁或海防的洁净车间。这不是哪一家代工厂的决定，而是谷歌已经向供应商发出的明确信号：到2027年，中国将不再出现在Pixel的整机制造版图里。

迁移背后的真正动因，是不确定。路透社与日经此前的报道均提示，谷歌是在为可能进一步加码的关税与出口管制提前布防。中美在半导体、清洁能源、关键矿产上反复拉锯的近三年里，「最坏情况」的门槛一再被刷新；任何一家美国公司，都不愿在旺季前夜，发现自己的旗舰机突然被卡在某一项出口许可上。

## 越南为什么接得住：三星留下的「公共物品」

谷歌的供应商不是从零开始。越南能接住Pixel的整机制造，并非新故事，而是过去十多年三星电子（Samsung Electronics）一砖一瓦铺出来的「公共物品」：河内附近的北宁（Bac Ninh）、太原（Thai Nguyen）、胡志明市西贡高科技园区（Saigon Hi-Tech Park）周边，已经聚集了超过10万名电子产业工人、200余家一级供应商，从外壳、屏幕模组、PCB（Printed Circuit Board，印制电路板）到电池与摄像头模组，都有现成工艺。

日经亚洲援引分析人士的话说，谷歌比苹果（Apple）更「自由」——这种「自由」并非技术意义上的，而是市场意义上的：Pixel不在中国销售，迁出的政治成本与市场回报损失基本为零。这与苹果形成鲜明对比——苹果仍高度依赖中国作为iPhone的重要组装基地与关键市场，其大中华区营收在2024年仍占其全球营收相当比例。两相对照，Pixel移得动，iPhone移不动——这是「中美脱钩」光谱上一个非常具体的差别。

## 「中国+1」从对冲走向主轴

过去十年，「中国+1」（China+1）一直是个防御性概念：到中国之外多备一个选项，对冲供应链风险。但2026年的语境已经变了。惠誉评级（Fitch Ratings）亚太主权评级董事George Xu对越南《青年报》（Thanh Niên）表示，越南是全球供应链重构、特别是「中国+1」战略的最大受益者之一；强劲的外商直接投资（FDI）流入预计将持续，支撑越南中短期增长前景；越南亦具备承接全球AI（人工智能）需求的有利位置，今年上半年AI相关电子产品出口增长强劲。

支撑这一判断的是几条具体线索：越南既有制造设施、测试与封装产能、成熟供应网络，以及电子与半导体领域多年积累的工程能力。越南政府也正主动把目标从「承接」升级为「挑好的承接」——希望借吸引更高附加值的制造业外资，把全要素生产率往上推一档。在更宏观的层面，越南广泛的自由贸易协定（FTA）网络让出口商与外资得以优惠关税进入主要市场，这本身就是被反复验证的「外资引力」。

## 整机搬了，供应链搬了吗？

但要小心一个常见的误读：整机制造的地理迁移，并不等于整条供应链的迁移。越南工信部数据与多项行业研究都显示，越南电子产业的本地化率（local content ratio，本土化率）仍偏低。一台贴上「越南制造」标签的智能手机，核心SoC芯片（System on Chip，系统级芯片）可能来自中国台湾或韩国，存储芯片来自韩国或日本，屏幕来自韩国或中国大陆，摄像头CMOS传感器（Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor，互补金属氧化物半导体）来自日本或索尼越南厂——换句话说，「最后的装配」可以很快挪走，但「前面的零部件」挪起来要慢得多。

Pixel的高端机型已先在越南完成开发与生产，这一点至关重要，因为它意味着谷歌已经把工程验证、可靠性测试、良率爬坡这些「软」环节一并搬了过来。一旦这些软环节在越南立住，剩余的智能手表与无线耳机——技术门槛相对低、所需产线面积小——跟着搬就是顺势而为。这也解释了为什么日经会强调「高端机先跑通，等于给整条产品线打了样」。

## 越南的天花板：电、地、人、本土化

越南不是无限供给的承接地。把它推到极限看，至少有四块天花板在逼近。其一是电。越南北部2024—2025年因高温与干旱出现工业用电紧张，部分工业园区被要求错峰用电；这对一个要支撑一年数千万台消费电子产能的国家，是一道实在的约束。其二是地。大型工业园区集中在河内、海防、胡志明市三大都市圈周边，地价与厂房租金过去三年明显抬升，新进入者要付出更高的「土地溢价」。其三是人。组装环节的劳动力充裕，但能跑主板工程、可靠性测试、自动化运维的工程师仍紧俏；越南每年毕业的工科生数量不足以匹配急速扩张的需求，本地高校与企业内训体系还在补课。其四是本土化率。越南政府希望外资带来更多本地采购与本地研发，但要把SoC、显示驱动IC（Integrated Circuit，集成电路）、高端PCB等关键环节引到本地，所需时间以「五年」为单位。

这些天花板不会让「中国+1」刹车，但会把它从「全面替代」逼成「分段替代」：低端、标准化、价敏感的产品先走；高端、定制化、研发密集的环节慢走。

## 谁在真正攫取价值？

一个更冷静的问题是：越南到底从Pixel整机制造里拿走了多少？在典型的智能手机制造链条中，材料与零部件往往占到整机BOM（Bill of Materials，物料清单）成本的60%—70%甚至更高，这部分绝大多数被海外供应商与品牌方拿走。越南本地主要捕获的是后段的组装人工、洁净车间运营与部分零部件的本地化采购——体量真实存在，但相对整机的全球价值而言，是一个「百分比意义上的小数」。

理解了这一点，就能看清越南制造业升级的真实命题：如何让本土供应商进入「材料与零部件」那一栏，而不只是停留在「组装工时」那一栏？这不是越南北宁一个工厂能回答的问题，而是越南政府、产业政策、本地资本市场（股市、债券、银行）需要协同的长期工程。

## 去风险，还是成本转嫁？

另一个值得追问的判断是：这是「去风险」（de-risking），还是「成本转嫁」（cost pass-through）？在「去风险」的叙述里，迁出中国是为了降低暴露在关税、出口管制与地缘冲突下的脆弱性；在「成本转嫁」的叙述里，迁出是为了把不再便宜的中国制造的「中国溢价」转嫁给美欧消费者。

事实上，Pixel的定价并不便宜——旗舰机型的售价区间大致在800—1300美元（约5700元至9300元人民币）之间——迁移带来的额外成本，最终大概率由美国与欧洲的消费者吸收。换句话说，「去风险」与「成本转嫁」在财务上并非对立，而是一枚硬币的两面。真正的检验在于：迁移之后，谷歌愿不愿意在越南长期投入工程师团队与本地研发预算。这是「真去风险」与「标签去风险」的分水岭。

## 越南、菲律宾、泰国：东南亚的接力

最后，把镜头拉远。谷歌的「+1」选项不止越南，菲律宾（Philippines）与泰国（Thailand）同样在候选名单上。菲律宾的优势在于英语人才与服务业基础，更适合做软件测试、用户运营与售后服务；其制造基础相对薄弱，工业用电成本偏高，但在「服务+硬件」结合的环节有结构性优势。泰国的优势在于汽车与硬盘产业积累的精密制造与模具工程，更适合做结构性电子部件与车载电子。越南的相对优势则是规模化、组装与智能手机产业链的现成生态。

也就是说，谷歌的「+1」不太可能是「单点选择」，更可能是「组合配置」：整机装配在越南，结构件与模具在泰国，服务与软件在菲律宾——东南亚（Southeast Asia）内部会出现「产业再分工」，而不是「哪一个国家赢者通吃」。日经亚洲的判断也呼应了这一点：东南亚半导体产业的发展正在重塑区域经济格局，使越南与菲律宾都走上了迈向高收入经济体的路径——前提是那四块天花板被妥善管理。

## 延伸阅读

**「中国+1」（China+1）**：跨国企业为对冲单一供应地风险，在保留中国产能的同时，把增量或关键产能迁出至越南、印度、墨西哥等地的供应链策略，2018年后逐渐从概念演变为落地实践。

**本土化率（local content ratio）**：衡量一国制造业中本地采购、本地生产的零部件、人工与价值在总产出中占比的指标，越南电子产业的本土化率仍显著低于其组装规模在全球中的份额。

**去风险（de-risking）**：相对「脱钩」（decoupling），强调在不中断经贸联系的前提下降低对单一国家在关键供应链节点上的依赖，2023年后成为欧美对华经贸政策的主流表述。

# Google Moves Pixel Out of China: Vietnam at the Tipping Point of "China+1"

On 28 August, Vietnam's state news agency Vietnam+ cited Nikkei Asia in reporting that Google has told suppliers it will move all final assembly of its Pixel smartphones, Pixel Watch and Pixel Buds out of China starting in 2027. The most telling detail is not the move itself, but its sequencing: the technically hardest product — the flagship handset — was engineered and built in Vietnam first, then the lower-barrier watch and earbuds are to follow. This is less a forced exit than a rehearsed one. With US–China trade frictions and export controls still tightening, Google is now moving Pixel's final assembly out of China on a rehearsed timeline rather than a forced one.

## An engineer in the last row of a Shenzhen line

Picture a surface-mount line in Bao'an or Songshan Lake. At the end of the line, a process engineer watches a Pixel board's post-reflow yield climb past a threshold. He may not know that the next high-volume run of that board is more likely to come from a clean room in Bac Ninh or Hai Phong than from Guangdong. The decision is not his factory's, but Google's: by 2027, China should not appear anywhere on Pixel's final-assembly map.

The motive is uncertainty. Reuters and Nikkei have both reported that Google is pre-empting a possible further tightening of US tariffs and China-side export licensing. Across semiconductors, clean energy and critical minerals, the "worst case" has been repeatedly redefined in the past three years. No American company wants its flagship device to be caught at peak season behind a permit that did not exist the quarter before.

## Why Vietnam can take it: the "public good" Samsung built

Google's suppliers are not starting from zero. Vietnam's ability to absorb Pixel assembly is a story written over more than a decade by Samsung Electronics — a public good built one facility at a time. Around Bac Ninh, Thai Nguyen and the Saigon Hi-Tech Park, Vietnam now has a workforce of more than 100,000 electronics workers and a tier-1 supplier base of more than 200 firms covering casings, display modules, PCBs, batteries and camera modules.

A Nikkei-cited analyst put it bluntly: Google is "freer" than Apple — and the freedom is not technical, it is market-based. Pixel does not sell in China, so the political cost and the revenue loss of moving out are essentially zero. The contrast with Apple is sharp. Apple still depends on China as a major iPhone assembly hub and as a key market; in 2024, Greater China remained a significant share of its global revenue. Pixel can move; iPhone, for now, cannot. That is one of the most concrete asymmetries on the US–China decoupling spectrum.

## China+1: from hedge to main axis

For a decade, "China+1" was a defensive concept — keep China, add an option elsewhere, hedge the risk. The 2026 lexicon is different. George Xu, director of Asia-Pacific sovereign ratings at Fitch Ratings, told Thanh Niên that Vietnam is one of the principal beneficiaries of the global supply-chain realignment, and of the "China+1" strategy in particular. He expects the strong FDI inflow to continue, supporting Vietnam's growth in the medium term, and argued that Vietnam is well placed to capture global AI demand — AI-related electronics exports grew strongly in the first half of 2026.

Underpinning that view are concrete lines of evidence. Vietnam has an installed base of manufacturing capacity, testing and packaging, a mature supplier network, and years of accumulated engineering know-how in electronics and semiconductors. The Vietnamese government is also upgrading its own target: from "capturing" to "capturing well" — that is, deliberately steering FDI toward higher value-added manufacturing to push up total factor productivity. And on the macro side, Vietnam's wide network of free trade agreements gives exporters and foreign investors preferential access to the major end markets — a recurring, measurable magnet for capital.

## Final assembly moved, but did the supply chain?

Beware a common misreading. The geographic move of final assembly is not the same as the move of the supply chain. Vietnamese ministry data and a string of industry studies both show that Vietnam's local content ratio in electronics remains low. A phone that wears a "Made in Vietnam" label typically has its system-on-chip from Taiwan or South Korea, memory from South Korea or Japan, the display from South Korea or mainland China, and the camera CMOS sensor from Japan or a Sony plant in Vietnam. Final assembly is fast to move; the components sitting underneath it move slowly.

That makes it significant that Google has already completed development and production of its premium Pixel in Vietnam. The "soft" parts of the process — engineering validation, reliability testing and yield ramp — have been moved together. Once those soft parts are settled in Vietnam, the watch and the earbuds — smaller in footprint, simpler in process — are a natural follow-on. This is why Nikkei stresses that the flagship line's success "set the template" for the rest of the portfolio.

## Vietnam's ceilings: power, land, talent, localisation

Vietnam is not a bottomless absorption pool. Pushed to the limit, four ceilings are within sight. The first is power. In 2024 and 2025, industrial electricity in northern Vietnam tightened during heat waves and droughts, and several industrial parks were asked to stagger consumption. For a country asked to host tens of millions of consumer-electronics units a year, that is a hard constraint, not an abstraction. The second is land. The major parks cluster around Hanoi, Hai Phong and Ho Chi Minh City; land prices and rents have visibly climbed in the past three years, and new entrants now pay a meaningful "land premium." The third is talent. Assembly labour is abundant, but the engineers who run motherboard processes, reliability tests and automated lines remain tight; Vietnam's annual engineering graduates are not yet enough to match the pace of expansion, and universities and corporate training are still catching up. The fourth is localisation. Hanoi wants more local procurement and local R&D from foreign investors, but pulling SoC, display driver ICs and high-end PCBs onshore is a multi-year, often five-year, proposition.

These ceilings will not stop "China+1." They will reshape it from "full substitution" to "phased substitution": low-end, standardised, price-sensitive products move first; high-end, customised, R&D-intensive stages move later.

## Who actually captures the value?

A cooler question: how much value does Vietnam actually capture from Pixel final assembly? In a typical smartphone manufacturing value chain, materials and components usually account for the largest share of bill-of-materials cost — often 60 to 70 per cent or more. That share is captured overwhelmingly by overseas suppliers and by the brand. Vietnam's domestic capture is mostly back-end: assembly labour, clean-room operations, and a portion of localised component sourcing. The volume is real, but in percentage-of-total-value terms it is a small number.

Seen this way, the real industrial question for Vietnam is how to move its domestic suppliers into the "materials and components" column, rather than staying in the "assembly hours" column. That is not a question a single Bac Ninh factory can answer. It is a long-term project that has to be coordinated across industrial policy, local capital markets — equity, bond and bank financing — and the country's broader technology-education system.

## De-risking, or cost pass-through?

Another question worth asking: is this "de-risking" or cost pass-through? In the de-risking narrative, the move out of China is about reducing exposure to tariffs, export controls and geopolitical shock. In the cost pass-through narrative, the move is about offloading China's rising cost base onto consumers in the US and Europe.

In fact, the two are not financially opposed — they are two sides of the same coin. Pixel is not a cheap device: flagship models sell roughly in the $800–$1,300 range, and the incremental cost of the move is, with high probability, absorbed by US and European consumers. The real test of whether this is genuine de-risking is what happens after the move. Does Google continue to invest in Vietnamese engineering teams and local R&D budgets over a multi-year horizon, or does it settle for a label change? That is the line between real de-risking and label-only de-risking.

## Vietnam, the Philippines, Thailand: the relay within Southeast Asia

Finally, zoom out. Google's "+1" menu is not only Vietnam; the Philippines and Thailand are also on the shortlist. The Philippines' edge is English-language talent and a service-sector base — better suited to software testing, user operations and after-sales support. Its manufacturing base is thinner and industrial power is relatively expensive, but for the "service + hardware" combination it has structural strengths. Thailand's edge is the precision engineering and mould-making accumulated through its automotive and hard-drive industries — a better fit for structural electronics and automotive-grade components. Vietnam's relative edge is scale, assembly and an already-built smartphone ecosystem.

Google's "+1" is therefore unlikely to be a single-country pick. It is more likely to be a portfolio: final assembly in Vietnam, structural parts and moulds in Thailand, software and service work in the Philippines. Southeast Asia is heading for a new intra-regional division of labour, not a single winner-take-all. Nikkei Asia's wider framing is consistent: the rise of Southeast Asian semiconductors is redrawing the regional economic map, putting both Vietnam and the Philippines on a path toward high-income status — if, and only if, the four ceilings are managed.

## Further reading

**China+1**: a corporate supply-chain strategy in which firms keep their China capacity while siting incremental or critical capacity outside it — in Vietnam, India, Mexico and similar — to hedge single-country risk; the term moved from a slogan to a procurement practice after 2018.

**Local content ratio (LCR)**: the share of a country's manufacturing output accounted for by domestically sourced components, labour and value added; Vietnam's electronics LCR remains well below its share of global final assembly.

**De-risking**: a policy and corporate term, distinguished from "decoupling", that describes reducing dependency on a single jurisdiction in critical supply-chain nodes without severing trade ties; it became the dominant Western framing for China policy after 2023.
