# 菲律宾比索跌穿历史低点：油价、美债与侨汇缓冲的三重挤压

9月2日，菲律宾比索盘中触及1美元兑62.652比索的历史低位，领跌亚洲货币。这一刻发生在美国对伊朗新一轮打击推高油价、全球债券收益率冲上多年高位的背景之下——布伦特原油一度逼近每桶95美元（约685元人民币），避险资金涌向美元，新兴市场资产被集体抛售。表面看，这是一次外部冲击下的技术性破位；往深里看，它暴露的是菲律宾经济长久以来脆弱的外部头寸。

## 现象：比索创下62.652的盘中纪录

9月2日的交易中，比索一度跌至62.652，此前一个交易日（9月1日）收于62.4，已是当时的历史新低；而8月28日它还停留在62.265。短短一周内连续刷新低点，显示贬值并非偶然波动。与此同时，菲律宾证券交易所指数约跌1%，区域内风险偏好急剧恶化。

Maybank的分析师点出问题核心：比索承受压力，源于"不利的外部头寸与偏低的实质利率"，而高油价与资本财进口增加的预期，会持续拖累货币。更具警示意味的是，传统上靠海外劳工汇款（OFW remittances）来稳定汇率的"缓冲垫"，如今提供的支撑正在减弱。

## 传导：油价如何击穿新兴市场

这一轮冲击的源头，是中东局势骤然紧张。美国对伊朗目标发动打击后，原油价格两日内飙升，布伦特一度站上每桶95美元（约685元人民币），WTI也在90美元（约650元人民币）上方。对菲律宾这类能源高度依赖进口的经济体而言，油价上涨直接扩大进口账单，推升美元需求，进而施压比索。

同时，全球债券收益率同步走高。美国10年期国债收益率升至近三年高位，日本10年期国债收益率突破3%，为1996年以来首次。当美元资产回报上升、新兴市场资产吸引力下降，资本便从本地债券与股票中撤出，形成比索的另一重卖压。MSCI新兴市场货币指数当日跌约0.2%，终结了此前连续10个交易日的涨势。

## 侨汇：传统稳定器正在失灵

菲律宾经济最常被称道的"稳定器"，是约千万海外劳工的汇款。长期以来，侨汇被视为比索的压舱石——无论经济如何波动，汇款流入都能提供源源不断的外汇供给。但这一次，这块压舱石出现了松动。

Maybank指出，来自主要来源地（尤其是美国与受冲突影响的中东）的汇款增速，已显疲态甚至放缓。中东恰恰是菲律宾海外劳工高度集中的区域，局势不稳直接削弱了其汇款能力。当最大的外汇稳定来源开始减速，比索便失去了最重要的防线。这也提示一个结构性事实：菲律宾的对外韧性，正越来越受制于地缘冲突的远端传导。

## 利差：本币疲弱下的政策两难

面对贬值，菲律宾央行（BSP）并非毫无动作。8月27日，它将关键利率上调25个基点至5%，为本轮周期第三次加息。然而，三菱日联金融集团（MUFG）此前已提示，尽管央行加息，但在控通胀与促增长之间腾挪，比索仍是亚洲表现最弱的货币之一。

这正是小开放经济体的经典困境：加息能吸引一部分利差资金、缓和贬值，却会压制本已疲弱的内需；不加息则贬值与输入性通胀相互强化。更棘手的是，若油价持续高位，输入性通胀将迫使央行在增长尚未企稳时继续紧缩，进一步压缩政策空间。比索的弱势，因此不单是汇率问题，而是宏观政策被多重外部变量夹击的缩影。

## 债市：资本外逃的先行指标

比索的疲弱，在债市已有更刺眼的预演。据LSEG数据，年初至今，印尼与菲律宾10年期国债的价格损失分别达7.6%与23.6%——菲律宾债市的跌幅在区域内尤为惊人。债券价格的下跌，反映外资正在撤离长期本币资产，是比索卖压的前瞻信号。

当美债收益率走高，持有菲律宾国债的相对吸引力下降，外资减持便成趋势。一旦资本外流与货币贬值形成自我强化的循环，央行干预的成本将急剧上升。分析师提到，若菲律宾央行（BSP）出手干预，或能暂时为比索弱势"盖上盖子"，但无法消除根因。

## 对比：区域内的分化

在同一轮冲击中，亚洲货币并非整齐下跌。韩元逆势小幅走强，印尼盾与台币各跌约0.3%，泰铢跌0.1%后触及近一个月低位。菲律宾比索的"领跌"，凸显其外部脆弱性在区域内相对突出。

这种分化背后是基本面的差异：拥有较大经常账户盈余或外汇储备的经济体，抗压能力更强；而菲律宾长期存在贸易逆差，又高度依赖能源与资本财进口，对外部冲击的传导更快、更深。比索的表现，因此更像一份"外部头寸体检报告"，而非孤立的行情事件。

## 冷思考：比索弱势的结构根源

把视野拉长，比索的弱势并非9月2日一天造成。它根植于菲律宾经济的几项结构性特征：持续的商品贸易逆差、对进口能源的高度依赖、以及侨汇增速趋缓带来的外汇供给放缓。油价冲击只是把这些特征一次性点燃。

对马尼拉而言，政策选项其实有限：短期靠央行干预与加息争取时间，中期则必须推动能源多元化（如加快可再生能源与区域电网接入）、改善贸易结构、并设法让侨汇以外的资本流入（如外资直接投资和侨民投资）更稳定。否则，每一次中东或全球利率的风吹草动，都会让比索再次成为亚洲最脆弱的那一环。

## 延伸阅读

**海外劳工汇款（OFW Remittances）**：菲律宾海外劳工汇回国内的资金，长期被视为比索汇率与家庭消费的重要稳定来源，约占经济总量一成。

**菲律宾央行（Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, BSP）**：菲律宾中央银行，负责货币政策与汇率管理，2026年8月第三次加息至5%。

**MSCI新兴市场货币指数（MSCI EM Currency Index）**：跟踪新兴市场货币表现的综合指标，9月2日结束连续10日涨势，反映风险偏好恶化。

# The Philippine Peso Breaks Its Record Low: Triple Squeeze from Oil, US Bonds and Fading Remittances

On September 2, the Philippine peso touched an intraday record low of 62.652 per US dollar, leading Asian currencies lower. The moment came as fresh US strikes on Iran sent oil prices higher and pushed global bond yields to multi-year highs — Brent crude briefly approached US$95 (about RMB 685) a barrel, safe-haven flows rushed to the dollar, and emerging-market assets were sold off across the board. On the surface, this was a technical breakdown under external shock; beneath it lay the long-standing fragility of the Philippines' external position.

## The phenomenon: the peso's 62.652 record

During September 2 trading, the peso fell to 62.652, after closing at 62.4 on September 1 — itself then a record low — and having stood at 62.265 as recently as August 28. Refreshing lows several times in a single week shows the depreciation is no偶然 fluctuation. Meanwhile, the Philippine Stock Exchange index fell about 1%, and regional risk appetite deteriorated sharply.

Maybank analysts pinpointed the core problem: the peso is pressured by an "unfavourable external position and low real rates," and expectations of higher oil prices and increased capital-goods imports will keep weighing on the currency. More tellingly, the traditional "buffer" of overseas Filipino worker (OFW) remittances is offering diminishing support.

## Transmission: how oil pierces emerging markets

The shock originated in the sudden Middle East escalation. After US strikes on Iranian targets, crude surged over two sessions, with Brent above US$95 (about RMB 685) and WTI above US$90 (about RMB 650). For an economy as energy-import-dependent as the Philippines, dearer oil directly enlarges the import bill, lifts dollar demand and pressures the peso.

At the same time, global bond yields rose in tandem. The US 10-year Treasury yield reached a near-three-year high, and Japan's 10-year government bond yield broke 3% for the first time since 1996. As dollar-asset returns climb and emerging-market appeal fades, capital exits local bonds and equities, adding a second layer of peso selling. The MSCI emerging-market currency index fell about 0.2% that day, ending a 10-session winning streak.

## Remittances: the traditional stabiliser is failing

The most praised "stabiliser" of the Philippine economy is the remittance flow from roughly ten million overseas workers. Long treated as the peso's ballast — whatever the economic weather, inflows supply a steady stream of foreign exchange — this time the ballast is loosening.

Maybank noted that remittance growth from major source markets, especially the United States and conflict-affected Middle East, has shown fatigue or slowdown. The Middle East is precisely where Filipino workers are densely concentrated, and instability there directly erodes their capacity to send money home. When the largest source of forex stability begins to decelerate, the peso loses its most important line of defence. It also reveals a structural truth: the Philippines' external resilience is increasingly at the mercy of distant geopolitical transmission.

## Rate differentials: the policy dilemma under a weak currency

Facing depreciation, the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) has not stood idle. On August 27 it raised its key rate by 25 basis points to 5%, the third hike of the cycle. Yet MUFG had already warned that, even with hikes, the peso remains among Asia's weakest currencies as the BSP balances inflation control against growth.

This is the classic small-open-economy trap: hiking attracts some carry flows and eases depreciation but suppresses already-weak domestic demand; not hiking lets depreciation and imported inflation reinforce each other. Worse, if oil stays high, imported inflation forces continued tightening before growth stabilises, further squeezing policy space. The peso's weakness is thus not merely an exchange-rate issue but a microcosm of macro policy caught between multiple external variables.

## The bond market: a leading indicator of capital flight

The peso's softness was already previewed more starkly in bonds. According to LSEG data, year-to-date price losses on 10-year government bonds reached 7.6% for Indonesia and 23.6% for the Philippines — the latter's drawdown is especially alarming regionally. Falling bond prices reflect foreign investors exiting long-dated local-currency assets, a forward signal of peso selling.

When US yields rise, holding Philippine sovereign debt becomes less attractive, and foreign削减 turns into a trend. Once capital outflow and currency depreciation reinforce each other, the cost of central-bank intervention spikes. Analysts noted that BSP intervention could temporarily "put a lid" on the peso's weakness, but cannot remove the root cause.

## Divergence: the regional split

In this same shock, Asian currencies did not fall in unison. The South Korean won bucked the trend and strengthened slightly; the Indonesian rupiah and Taiwan dollar each slipped about 0.3%; the Thai baht eased 0.1% to a near-one-month low. The peso's "lead decline" highlights its relatively prominent external vulnerability in the region.

Behind the divergence lie fundamentals: economies with larger current-account surpluses or reserves weather better, while the Philippines runs a persistent trade deficit and depends heavily on energy and capital-goods imports, transmitting external shocks faster and deeper. The peso's performance reads less like an isolated market event and more like an "external-position health check."

## A cool note: the structural roots of peso weakness

Zooming out, the peso's weakness was not made in a single day on September 2. It is rooted in several structural features: a chronic goods-trade deficit, heavy reliance on imported energy, and slowing remittance-driven forex supply. The oil shock merely lit the fuse on all of them at once.

Manila's policy options are in fact limited: short-term, buy time with central-bank intervention and hikes; medium-term, it must push energy diversification (renewables and regional grid access), improve trade structure, and stabilise capital inflows beyond remittances, such as FDI and diaspora investment. Otherwise, every gust from the Middle East or global rates will again make the peso Asia's most fragile link.
