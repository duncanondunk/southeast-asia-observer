# 越南拉美日俄下海采气：一场被霍尔木兹海峡催化的南海能源博弈

《日经亚洲》10月9日报道，越南正把美国、日本、俄罗斯的能源企业一齐拉进南海的海上油气项目。表面看，这是一盘能源自给的算盘；往深里看，却是一场地缘与电力的双重突围。越南岸外藏着东南亚最丰富的油气储备之一，可它自己既缺资本又缺技术，于是只能请外力入局。霍尔木兹海峡的硝烟、2030年37吉瓦（1吉瓦等于100万千瓦）燃气发电的雄心，以及一份份叠在争议海域之上的商业合同，共同把这局棋推到了台前。

## 能源焦虑：一场被霍尔木兹海峡催化的抢气竞赛

越南的着急，写在电力结构里。煤电约占一半，水电不足三成，其余才轮得到燃气、风电与光伏。可用水靠天吃饭，煤电又背着环保包袱，燃气发电和新能源顺理成章成了增长主力。偏偏本土老气田陆续进入产量衰减期，首座进口液化天然气电厂今年1月才投运，中东一乱，气源稳定性立刻打折。河内给自己定下硬指标：到2030年，燃气发电装机要从不足10吉瓦扩到37吉瓦。进口气靠不住，深海的油气就成了非挖不可的抓手。事实上，越南并非不想把蛋糕留给自己。可上游勘探的资金门槛、深水作业的技术空白，以及漫长回收期带来的财务压力，让国家石油公司独木难支。请外力入局，与其说是主动邀约，不如说是被电力缺口与资本缺口同时逼出的选择。

## 三股外力：美日俄各取所需

这一回，河内把牌摊得很开。日本出光兴产（Idemitsu）运营的金星、大月气田位于越南南部外海约350公里的05-1b和05-1c区块，早在2020年、2022年相继投产，每天向岸上输送约400万立方米天然气。美国墨菲石油（Murphy Oil）在九龙盆地开发的金骆驼油田，已于9月27日产出首批原油。埃克森美孚（ExxonMobil）牵头的蓝鲸气田，紧挨岘港外海，被视为越南最大的单一气源之一，储量据估超过1500亿立方米。俄罗斯扎鲁别日石油（Zarubezhneft）则是越南海上油气领域经营逾40年的老牌玩家，今年6月越南总理还会见了其首席执行官，明确表示支持在更深水域勘探。三家大国的资本与技术同时入场，越南要的从来不是某一家撑场面，而是用多元外力补上自己的能源短板。

## 数字：被外资托起的海上油气版图

数字最能说明越南对外力的依赖有多深。越南计划投资部2023年年报显示，能源领域的外资依存度已达68%。一座深水平台动辄数亿美元，水越深、离岸越远，成本与难度成倍往上翻——金星、大月项目总投资超过1000亿日元（约6.3亿美元，约合45亿元人民币），单口深水井的勘探开发成本常超过100亿日元。越南国家石油公司一家承担不起，引入国际伙伴不是权宜，而是结构性现实。出光兴产的项目还预留了再接入5口新井的空间，可见这盘棋远没下完。

## 深水之困：技术、资本与时限

深水气田的开发周期普遍要5到7年，叠加地质复杂性与高技术门槛，本土资本根本独立撑不住。越南想把产能尽快变现，却绕不开两个硬约束：一是深水作业的核心技术掌握在美日俄手里；二是资本回收期长，油价与气价一波动，项目回报就跟着晃。换句话说，河内握着资源，却把钥匙交到了外人手上。这种「有矿无链」的处境，正是许多东南亚资源国共同的尴尬。对河内而言，最棘手的不是找不到合作伙伴，而是伙伴越多，自己对资源和收益的掌控力反而越薄。能源自主的口号喊得响，账本上的依赖却越写越沉。

## 南海之结：合同换不来主权

商业合同可以一签再签，海洋权益的争议却不是几页纸能抹平的。越南附近的项目，有的落在没有争议的水域，有的则踩在中越海洋权益主张重叠的区域。中国对南海诸岛及其附近海域拥有无可争辩的主权，对相关海域享有主权权利和管辖权，这一立场一贯明确。对于损害中方主权和权益的油气活动，中方会提出交涉。所以看清这局棋，不能只数来了哪几国公司，更要看具体的勘探区块与钻井坐标——谁有资格开发海底资源，终究不能由商业合同单独说了算。

## 电力账单：37吉瓦野心的底气与软肋

把镜头拉回国内，37吉瓦的燃气发电雄心，是越南守住两位数增长目标的底层支撑。可底气背后是软肋：本土产量下滑、进口液化天然气受制于人、深水开发又离不开外资。河内想用自主开发填上缺口，现实却是越挖越依赖外力。能源自主这道题，越南短期内算不平——它要的不仅是气，更是一张不被中东局势和外国资本同时卡脖子的安全网。

## 北京视角：维权与大局的平衡

站在北京的角度，这局棋并不只是商业新闻。美日俄高调进入南海能源开发，客观上让外部军事与政治存在有了经济支点，南海问题的复杂程度随之抬升。中方的应对一贯是双线：一方面依法维护领土主权和海洋权益，对任何侵害行为表明立场；另一方面，仍把与东盟的务实合作、保持南海总体稳定放在优先位置。维权与维稳之间，北京更倾向于用规则和多边框架把争议框住，而不是让单一能源项目放大对抗。

## 结语：算得清的账，算不清的局

越南请美日俄下海采气，算的是一本清清楚楚的能源账：缺电、缺钱、缺技术，所以请人入局。可这局棋真正难算的，是能源自主与外力依赖之间的拉扯，是商业合同与主权主张之间的错位，更是大国在南海的棋子越落越多之后，区域该怎么稳住。越南要气，邻居要权，大国要势——一笔账，三种算盘。这局棋的吊诡之处正在于此：越南越想靠外力补上能源短板，外力在南海的存在就越扎实；而外部存在越扎实，区域关于主权与规则的拉锯就越难收尾。霍尔木兹的硝烟或许会散，但南海底下的能源与地缘博弈，才刚刚升温。

# Vietnam's Offshore Oil and Gas Draw Japan, U.S. and Russia into the South China Sea

Nikkei Asia reported on October 9 that Vietnam is pulling American, Japanese and Russian energy companies into its offshore oil and gas projects in the South China Sea. On the surface this looks like a straightforward bid for energy self-sufficiency; looked at more closely, it is a dual breakout on the fronts of both geopolitics and electricity supply. Vietnamese waters hold some of the richest hydrocarbon reserves in Southeast Asia, yet Hanoi lacks both the capital and the technology to tap them alone, so it has had to invite outside forces in. The smoke over the Strait of Hormuz, an ambition to build 37 gigawatts of gas-fired power by 2030, and a stack of commercial contracts laid over disputed waters have together pushed this game to centre stage.

## The energy anxiety: a gas scramble accelerated by Hormuz

Vietnam's urgency is written into its power mix. Coal accounts for roughly half of generation, hydropower for less than a third, and gas, wind and solar fill the rest. Hydropower depends on the rains, coal carries an environmental burden, so gas and renewables have become the natural growth engines. Yet Vietnam's mature gas fields are entering decline, and its first imported-LNG plant only came online in January. When the Middle East turns turbulent, the reliability of imported gas immediately takes a hit. Hanoi has set itself a hard target: raise gas-fired capacity from under 10 GW to 37 GW by 2030. If imported gas cannot be trusted, the deep-water fields become a resource it simply must extract.

## Three external forces: the U.S., Japan and Russia each take what they need

This time Hanoi has laid its cards wide open. Japan's Idemitsu operates the Sao Vang and Dai Nguyet (Golden Star and Big Moon) fields, located about 350 km off Vietnam's southern coast in blocks 05-1b and 05-1c, which began production in 2020 and 2022 and now send roughly 4 million cubic metres of gas ashore each day. America's Murphy Oil brought the Golden Camel oil field in the Cuu Long basin on stream with first oil on September 27. ExxonMobil's Blue Whale field, off the coast near Da Nang, is one of the country's largest single gas sources, with estimated reserves above 150 billion cubic metres. Russia's Zarubezhneft, a veteran of more than four decades in Vietnamese waters, saw its chief executive meet the Vietnamese prime minister in June, who expressed support for exploration in deeper blocks. With capital and technology from three major powers entering at once, Vietnam is not seeking a single flagship partner but using diversified external force to patch its own capability gaps.

## The numbers: an offshore map propped up by foreign capital

The figures show how deep Vietnam's reliance on outside help runs. According to the Ministry of Planning and Investment's 2023 annual report, the energy sector's dependence on foreign capital had already reached 68 percent. A single deep-water platform can cost several hundred million dollars, and the deeper and farther from shore, the more the cost and difficulty multiply — the Sao Vang-Dai Nguyet project invested over 100 billion yen (roughly US$630 million), and a single deep-water well often exceeds 10 billion yen. PetroVietnam cannot carry this alone; bringing in international partners is structural reality, not a stopgap. Idemitsu's project even reserves space to tie in five more wells, a sign this game is far from finished.

## The deep-water dilemma: technology, capital and time

Developing a deep-water field typically takes five to seven years, and with geological complexity and high technical thresholds, local capital simply cannot stand on its own. Vietnam wants to monetise capacity quickly, yet runs into two hard constraints: the core deep-water know-how sits with the U.S., Japan and Russia, and the long payback period means returns swing with oil and gas prices. In other words, Hanoi holds the resources but has handed the keys to others. This "have the ore, lack the chain" predicament is the shared awkwardness of many Southeast Asian resource states.

## The South China Sea knot: contracts cannot buy sovereignty

Commercial contracts can be signed again and again, but maritime-rights disputes are not erased by a few pages of paper. Some of Vietnam's projects sit in undisputed waters; others straddle overlapping claims with China. China holds indisputable sovereignty over the islands of the South China Sea and the adjacent waters, and enjoys sovereign rights and jurisdiction over relevant waters — a position it has stated consistently. Where hydrocarbon activities undermine China's sovereignty and rights, Beijing raises its concerns. So to read this game one must look not only at which countries' firms have arrived, but at the specific blocks and well coordinates — who is entitled to develop the seabed cannot ultimately be decided by commercial contracts alone.

## The power bill: the backbone and soft spots of a 37 GW ambition

Back on the domestic front, the 37 GW gas-power ambition is the bedrock of Vietnam's drive to hold double-digit growth. Yet behind the backbone lie soft spots: falling domestic output, reliance on imported LNG, and deep-water development that cannot do without foreign capital. Hanoi wants to close the gap through autonomous extraction, but the reality is that the more it drills, the more it depends on outside force. The energy-autonomy equation is one Vietnam cannot balance in the short term — what it wants is not only gas, but a security net that is not simultaneously throttled by Middle East turbulence and foreign capital.

## Beijing's view: balancing rights protection and the bigger picture

From Beijing's vantage point, this game is not merely commercial news. The high-profile entry of the U.S., Japan and Russia into South China Sea energy development objectively gives external military and political presence an economic foothold, raising the complexity of the dispute. China's response has been two-track: on one side it defends territorial sovereignty and maritime rights in accordance with law and states its position against any infringement; on the other it keeps practical cooperation with ASEAN and overall stability in the South China Sea as priorities. Between defending rights and preserving stability, Beijing leans toward using rules and multilateral frameworks to contain disputes rather than letting a single energy project amplify confrontation.

## Conclusion: a ledger that adds up, a game that does not

Vietnam's invitation to the U.S., Japan and Russia to tap the sea is a perfectly clear energy ledger: short of power, short of money, short of technology, so it invites others in. Yet the truly hard part to calculate is the tug-of-war between energy autonomy and external dependence, the mismatch between commercial contracts and sovereignty claims, and what happens to regional stability as the great powers drop more pieces onto the South China Sea board. Vietnam wants gas, its neighbours want rights, the great powers want leverage — one ledger, three sets of calculations. The smoke over Hormuz may clear, but the energy and geopolitical contest beneath the South China Sea is only heating up.
