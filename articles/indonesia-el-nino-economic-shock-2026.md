# 印尼"超级厄尔尼诺"的经济账：当干旱烧进 CPI

二〇二六年九月，厄尔尼诺在印尼进入峰值。林业部长拉查·尤利直言，"九月是厄尔尼诺的峰值期，也是我们的关键战役"。但比起森林大火与跨境烟霾，更深远的冲击藏在经济体内部：干旱正沿着粮食、棕榈油、水电与通胀的链条层层传导。野村（Nomura）的脆弱性评分显示，印尼是全球第三大"受厄尔尼诺经济冲击暴露"的市场，仅次于印度与菲律宾。一场气候异常，正在变成一张需要财政、产业与民生共同买单的账单。

## 气候信号：ENSO 指数 +1.59，干旱还将持续

印度尼西亚气象、气候与地球物理局（BMKG）局长特库·费萨尔确认，尼诺 3.4 区海温异常已将 ENSO 指数推高至 +1.59，达到"强"级别，且可能进一步走强。干燥季预计在九月达峰，直到十月中下旬季风雨到来才会缓解；而大气层的异常增温，甚至可能延续至二〇二七年。

这并非孤立事件。马来西亚、菲律宾与泰国同样步入干旱，区域性的降雨缺口正同时挤压农业、水电与民生用水。对印尼而言，真正的麻烦在于：它的经济结构与气候高度绑定，季风迟到一天，账单就多一分。

## 粮食账：食品占 CPI 的 22.5%

印尼统计局的篮子结构决定了冲击的放大效应：食品占消费者价格指数（CPI）权重的 22.5%，其中大米单独占 3.4%，能源占 11.5%。更关键的是，农业贡献全国 GDP 的 13.1%，却雇佣了 27.3% 的劳动力——这意味着一场农业减产，会同时打击产出、农村收入与家庭消费，而不是像新加坡、香港那样仅通过进口价格间接传导。

彭博经济研究曾测算，强厄尔尼诺可能为印尼与菲律宾的消费者价格通胀各添近两个百分点，主因正是减产推高食品价格。当二十七分之一的就业人口绑在田地上，粮价波动就是社会议题，而非单纯宏观数据。

## 棕榈油两难：B50 掺混 vs 出口缩水

棕榈油是印尼的"战略商品"，也是矛盾焦点。印尼棕榈油协会（Gapki）主席埃迪·马尔托诺警告，极端厄尔尼诺可能使毛棕榈油（CPO）产量减少多达五百万吨，影响最快明年显现。与此同时，普拉博沃政府力推的 B50 生物柴油强制掺混，将把国内 CPO 年吸收量抬升至一千六百三十万至一千七百万吨区间。

这就形成"油与粮"的拉扯：棕榈油本可出口创汇，如今被能源指令大量内吸。二〇二六年全国 CPO 产量预计约五千三百万吨，国内燃料需求优先，出口自然缩水。期货市场上，吉隆坡棕榈油一度升至每吨 4977林吉特（约1180美元/吨），创二〇二四年以来高位；美国农业部更预计二〇二六至二七季全球棕榈油库存降至九年低点。能源与粮食在同一株油棕上争夺，价格信号被双重放大。

## 水电与能源：水库见底的风险

干旱的另一面是水电。印尼约半数以上电力来自燃煤，但区域电网仍高度依赖水库调节，而其主要水电站多位于苏门答腊与爪哇。BMKG 协调公共工程与住房部，在爪哇等地对约二百四十座关键水库实施人工增雨，以防水位跌破发电与供水红线。

问题是，人工增雨只能"补水"、不能"造雨"。若干燥季如期延长，水电站出力下降，电网或将更多依赖昂贵的燃油与燃气调峰，进而推高工业与居民电价。对一个本就易受输入型能源冲击的经济体，这是又一道成本叠加。

## 宏观脆弱性：Nomura 的暴露评分

野村的评分卡把印尼列为全球第三大厄尔尼诺经济暴露市场，综合 z 值达 102.0，仅次于印度与菲律宾。其风险并非来自粮食净进口——印尼其实是轻微净食品出口国——而是来自内部结构：庞大的棕榈油现金作物出口，掩盖了小麦、大豆、食糖、大蒜等日常主粮的深度进口依赖。

换句话说，厄尔尼诺可以一面压低出口型农产品产出，一面逼出关键口粮的紧急进口，形成"产出受损+进口涨价"的双向挤压。这种传导风险主要蛰伏于国内经济，而非仅通过外贸价格外溢，这正是印尼评分高企的根由。

## 区域共振：东盟的"油转粮"通胀

印尼并非孤例。分析指出，东盟下一轮通胀考验正从石油转向食品：油价与化肥抬升种植成本，强厄尔尼诺又恰在成本传到消费端的当口威胁作物收成。印尼、菲律宾、泰国上行风险最明显，而 B50 这类生物柴油指令，会在粮价吃紧时进一步把棕榈油拽向能源市场，加剧"粮食 vs 燃料"之争。

对区域政策制定者而言，补贴能暂时压住 CPI 数字，却把压力转移到财政；价格管制能抑制表观通胀，却可能催生短缺或迫使消费者转向更贵的替代品。延迟显现的食品通胀，往往比油价冲击更难治理。

## 政策空间与隐忧

印尼并非毫无准备。政府已分发约十万台水泵、修复约九十万公顷灌溉网络、推广抗旱种子，并成立多部门特别工作组管控林火。但执行力是老问题：地方协调、资金到位与农户信任，常常赶不上旱情速度。

更深的隐忧在于节奏。厄尔尼诺的冲击是滞后且叠加的——油价与化肥成本本已高企，天气又转坏，未来几个季度东盟的食品价格底色只会更艰难。对印尼这样的结构型经济体，真正的考题不是"是否下雨"，而是能否在财政、产业与民生之间，为这张气候账单找到可持续的分摊方式。

## 延伸阅读

**厄尔尼诺（El Niño）**：赤道中东太平洋海温异常增暖的气候现象，常致东南亚干旱、林火与作物减产，并通过食品价格向宏观通胀传导。

**B50 生物柴油指令（B50 Biodiesel Mandate）**：印尼将棕榈油基生物柴油掺混比例由 40% 提升至 50% 的政策，意在减少柴油进口，但会大量内吸棕榈油供给。

**毛棕榈油（CPO, Crude Palm Oil）**：全球用量最大的植物油，印尼为最大生产国与出口国；其产量与价格高度受天气与生物柴油政策影响。

# Indonesia's "Super El Niño" Economic Bill: When Drought Burns Into the CPI

In September 2026, El Niño reaches its peak over Indonesia. Forestry Minister Raja Juli Antoni put it bluntly: "September is the peak of El Niño, and the main fight for us." Yet beyond forest fires and transboundary haze lies a deeper shock inside the economy: drought is transmitting, link by link, through food, palm oil, hydropower and inflation. Nomura's vulnerability scorecard ranks Indonesia as the world's third-most economically exposed market to El Niño shocks, behind only India and the Philippines. A climate anomaly is becoming a bill that fiscal, industrial and household budgets must share.

## The climate signal: ENSO at +1.59, and the dry spell lingers

Teuku Faisal Fathani, head of Indonesia's Meteorology, Climatology and Geophysics Agency (BMKG), confirmed that sea-surface anomalies in the Nino 3.4 region have pushed the ENSO index to +1.59—firmly "strong," with potential to intensify. The dry season is expected to peak in September and ease only with October–November monsoonal rains, while elevated ambient temperatures may persist into 2027.

This is not an isolated event. Malaysia, the Philippines and Thailand are also drying out; a regional rainfall deficit is squeezing agriculture, hydropower and household water at once. For Indonesia the real trouble is structural: its economy is bound tightly to the climate, and every day the monsoon is late, the bill grows.

## The food ledger: food is 22.5% of the CPI

Indonesia's consumption basket determines the amplification. Food weighs 22.5% of the consumer price index, with rice alone at 3.4% and energy at 11.5%. More critically, agriculture contributes 13.1% of GDP yet employs 27.3% of the workforce—so a harvest shortfall hits output, rural incomes and household spending simultaneously, rather than transmitting only indirectly through import prices as in Singapore or Hong Kong.

Bloomberg Economics has estimated that a strong El Niño could add nearly two percentage points to consumer-price inflation in both Indonesia and the Philippines, chiefly because reduced harvests lift food prices. When one in twenty-seven workers is tied to the land, grain-price swings are a social issue, not merely a macro statistic.

## The palm-oil dilemma: B50 blending vs shrinking exports

Palm oil is Indonesia's strategic commodity and its point of contradiction. Eddy Martono, chairman of the Indonesian Palm Oil Producers Association (Gapki), warned that an extreme El Niño could cut crude palm oil (CPO) output by as much as five million tonnes, with the effect felt as early as next year. Meanwhile, President Prabowo's B50 biodiesel mandate is lifting domestic CPO absorption toward 16.3–17 million tonnes a year.

This creates a food-versus-fuel tug-of-war: palm oil that could be exported is increasingly pulled into energy. With national CPO output projected at about 53 million tonnes in 2026, domestic fuel demand takes priority and exports naturally shrink. On futures markets, Kuala Lumpur palm oil rose to RM4,977 a tonne (about US$1,180/tonne), a high since 2024, while the USDA expects global palm inventories to fall to a nine-year low in 2026–27. Energy and food compete over the same oil palm, doubling the amplification of price signals.

## Hydropower and energy: the risk of emptying reservoirs

The other face of drought is hydroelectric power. Although Indonesia's grid is mostly coal-fired, regional networks still rely heavily on reservoir regulation, and its main hydro plants sit in Sumatra and Java. BMKG has coordinated with the Ministry of Public Works and Housing to conduct weather modification across roughly 240 critical reservoirs in Java, guarding against levels breaching power-generation and water-supply red lines.

The problem is that cloud-seeding can "add" water but not "make" rain. If the dry season extends as forecast, hydro output drops and the grid may lean more on costly oil- and gas-fired peaking plants, pushing up industrial and household tariffs. For an economy already exposed to imported-energy shocks, this is yet another layer of cost.

## Macro vulnerability: Nomura's exposure score

Nomura's scorecard places Indonesia third among the world's most El Niño-exposed economies, with an aggregate z-score of 102.0, behind India and the Philippines. The risk does not come from net food trade—Indonesia is a slight net food exporter—but from its internal structure: huge palm-oil cash-crop exports mask deep import dependence on everyday staples such as wheat, soybeans, sugar and garlic.

In other words, El Niño can simultaneously curb exportable farm output and force emergency imports of key food supplies, producing a two-way squeeze of "lower output plus pricier imports." This transmission risk lives mostly inside the domestic economy rather than leaking only through trade prices—which is precisely why Indonesia's score sits so high.

## Regional resonance: ASEAN's oil-to-food inflation

Indonesia is not alone. Analysts note that ASEAN's next inflation test is shifting from oil to food: dearer oil and fertiliser raise planting costs, while a strong El Niño threatens crops exactly as those costs reach consumers. Indonesia, the Philippines and Thailand face the clearest upside risks, and mandates like B50 pull palm oil toward energy precisely when grain prices are tight, intensifying the food-versus-fuel contest.

For regional policymakers, subsidies can temporarily suppress CPI figures but shift the burden onto fiscal accounts; price caps can dampen measured inflation yet breed shortages or push consumers to pricier substitutes. Delayed food inflation is typically harder to govern than an oil shock.

## Policy room and lingering worries

Indonesia is not unprepared. The government has distributed about 100,000 water pumps, rehabilitated roughly 900,000 hectares of irrigation, promoted drought-resistant seeds, and formed a multi-agency task force against land fires. But execution is the old problem: local coordination, fund disbursement and farmer trust often lag the pace of the drought.

The deeper worry is timing. El Niño's impact is lagged and叠加: oil and fertiliser costs were already elevated, and the weather has turned worse, so the food-price backdrop across ASEAN will only harden over coming quarters. For a structurally exposed economy, the real exam is not "whether it rains" but whether a sustainable way can be found to spread this climate bill across fiscal, industrial and household accounts.

## Further reading

**El Niño**: A climate phenomenon of anomalous warming in the equatorial central-eastern Pacific that often brings Southeast Asian drought, forest fires and crop losses, transmitting to macro inflation through food prices.

**B50 Biodiesel Mandate**: Indonesia's policy raising the palm-oil-based biodiesel blend from 40% to 50%, intended to cut diesel imports but heavily absorbing domestic palm-oil supply.

**Crude Palm Oil (CPO)**: The world's most-used vegetable oil; Indonesia is its largest producer and exporter, and its output and price are highly sensitive to weather and biodiesel policy.
