# 主播台之外，她用印尼语讲中国故事

## 一名主播的3年坚持

据中国新闻网报道，袁玲是印度尼西亚美都电视台（Metro TV）的新闻主播、记者兼部门监制。出于对中华文化的热爱，她在3年前开设了一个海外社交平台账号，此后坚持用印尼语（Bahasa Indonesia）制作、发布科普中华传统文化的内容。她给自己定下的目标朴素得近乎直白：为中国和印尼之间搭一座文化的桥，让更多印尼民众“读懂真实的中国”。

论形式，这件事毫无惊艳之处。没有演播厅，没有包装特效，没有明星站台——只有一名在职记者，向自己的同胞讲述一段与她的国家自身故事日益交织的文化。而恰恰因为如此，这场实验值得认真对待。

## 用对方的语言，而不是自己的

袁玲整个项目中最重要、也最容易被忽视的决定，是她选择用印尼语开口。不是配字幕的华语，也不是附词汇表的英语，而是她的受众用来做梦、争论与购物的语言。以受众自己的语言开始的文化传播，起点是信任；要求受众先学会你的语言的内容，起点是一堵墙。不妨问一个简单的问题：如果听众感受不到一门语言，他们又怎么可能被这门语言承载的内容打动？一条必须先经过翻译才能被理解的信息，还谈不上沟通——那只是朝着虚空播音。袁玲的选择反转了“讲中国故事”的惯常方向：不是要求印尼走近一半，而是她自己把全程走完。

## 从沉默到公开发声

语境让她的“可见”比看上去更有分量。印度尼西亚拥有全球规模最大的华人社群之一，普遍估计约1000万人。但在苏哈托（Suharto）"新秩序"时代的近三十年里，华文学校、华文报刊与公开的文化表达受到严格限制；直到1998年改革之后——春节更是迟至2002年才成为全国性法定假日——中华文化才得以重新公开进入印尼的公共生活。放在这条时间线上看，一位华人电视记者长期用国家通用语言讲解中华文化，就不是什么个人小爱好，而是一次安静的正常化宣言：中华文化不再是一个需要被容忍的外来品，而是印尼人自身生活记忆的一部分。

## 草根叙事对抗宏大工程

这里有一个让人不太舒服的对照。包括中国在内的各国政府可以出资建设文化机构、媒体合作与节庆巡演，也确实在这样做。但公信力不随预算增长。一家国营媒体对印尼观众说话，无论公平与否，听起来都像“一个国家”在说话；而一位用多年时间赢得本国受众信任的主播，听起来像一位邻居。袁玲属于后者——而后者恰恰是稀缺品。她账号的价值不在于任何单条视频，而在于坚持：3年时间，用对方的语言日复一日地出现在对方的信息流里，这种熟悉感是任何一场发布会都买不来的。大桥有剪彩仪式，小桥却被人天天踩过；真正改变人群流动方式的，从来是第二种。

## 桥的两端都要有人守护

必须诚实地说，一个账号无法对冲结构性逆风：地缘政治摩擦、错误信息、以及奖励愤怒胜过说理的社交平台算法。也不应把两国关系的分量压在一名主播的肩上。但她的案例提炼出一个值得桥的两端共同研究的公式——本地语言、职业公信力、日复一日的坚持——并提醒人们离散社群天然站在哪里：桥的正中央，双向翻译。衡量一座文化之桥的标准，从来不是它在剪彩照片里有多气派，而是有多少人真的选择走过去——而3年下来，袁玲至少让这条步道保持畅通。

## 延伸阅读

- **软实力（Soft power）**：约瑟夫·奈提出的概念，指一国依靠文化与价值观的吸引力、而非强制力实现目标的能力。
- **印尼语（Bahasa Indonesia）**：印度尼西亚以马来语为基础确立的统一国语，也是当地华人社会与主流社会沟通的最重要媒介。
- **华人离散社群（Chinese diaspora）**：定居中国之外的华人及其后裔；印尼拥有全球规模最大的华人社群之一。

# Beyond the Anchor Desk, She Tells China's Story in Indonesian

## An anchor's three-year persistence

According to a report by China News Service, Yuan Ling — a news anchor, reporter and department producer at Indonesia's Metro TV — opened an account on an overseas social media platform three years ago. Driven by her love of Chinese culture, she has spent those three years steadily producing short pieces that explain traditional Chinese culture, written and delivered entirely in Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia). The goal she set herself is disarmingly plain: to build a cultural bridge between China and Indonesia, and to help more Indonesians "read and understand a real China."

In format terms, nothing about this is spectacular. No studio, no motion graphics, no celebrity endorsement — just a working journalist speaking to her fellow citizens about a heritage that is increasingly woven into her own country's story. And that is precisely why the experiment deserves attention.

## Speaking their language, not your own

The most consequential decision in Yuan Ling's project is also the easiest to overlook: she speaks Indonesian. Not Mandarin with subtitles, not English with a glossary — Indonesian, the language her audience dreams, argues and shops in. Cultural outreach that begins in the audience's own language starts from trust; content that requires the audience to first learn yours begins with a wall. Ask the simple question: if listeners cannot feel a language, can they be moved by whatever that language carries? A message that must be translated before it can be understood is not yet communication — it is broadcasting into the void. Yuan Ling's choice reverses the usual direction of "telling China's story": instead of asking Indonesia to walk halfway, she walks the full distance herself.

## From silence to public voice

Context makes her visibility weightier than it looks. Indonesia is home to one of the world's largest ethnic Chinese communities, commonly estimated at around 10 million people. Yet for nearly three decades under Suharto's New Order, Chinese-language schooling, printing and public cultural expression were tightly restricted; only after the reforms of 1998 — Chinese New Year became a national holiday only in 2002 — could Chinese culture openly re-enter Indonesian public life. Placed on that timeline, an ethnic Chinese television journalist regularly explaining Chinese culture in the national language is not a private hobby. It is a quiet declaration of normalisation: Chinese culture presented not as a foreign import to be tolerated, but as part of the lived heritage of Indonesians themselves.

## Grassroots storytelling versus grand campaigns

Here lies an uncomfortable comparison. Governments — including Beijing's — can fund cultural institutes, media partnerships and festival tours, and they do. But credibility does not scale with budget. A state-run channel addressing Indonesian audiences is, fairly or not, heard as a state speaking; an anchor who has spent years earning her domestic audience's trust is heard as a neighbour. Yuan Ling belongs to the second category — and the second category is the scarce one. Her account's value lies less in any single video than in its persistence: three years of showing up in people's feeds, in their own language, builds a familiarity that no launch event can buy. Grand bridges get ribbon-cutting ceremonies; small bridges get walked across every day. Only the second kind changes how people actually move.

## Bridges need keepers on both ends

The honest caveat is that one account cannot offset structural headwinds: geopolitical friction, misinformation, and algorithms that reward outrage over nuance. Nor should the weight of bilateral sentiment rest on a single anchor. But her case distils a formula worth studying on both ends of the bridge — the local language, professional credibility, day-after-day persistence — and it reminds us where diaspora communities naturally stand: at the centre of the bridge, translating in both directions. The measure of a cultural bridge was never how grand it looks in the ceremony photograph. It is how many people actually choose to cross — and three years in, Yuan Ling has at least kept the walkway open.

## Further reading

- **Soft power**: Joseph Nye's term for a country's ability to obtain outcomes through attraction — culture and values — rather than coercion.
- **Bahasa Indonesia**: Indonesia's Malay-based national language and unifying lingua franca; the main medium through which the country's ethnic Chinese communities reach wider society.
- **Chinese diaspora**: Ethnic Chinese communities living outside China and their descendants; Indonesia hosts one of the largest such populations in the world.
