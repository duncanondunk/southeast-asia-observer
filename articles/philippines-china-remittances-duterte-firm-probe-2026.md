# “中国汇款”搅动菲律宾政坛：莎拉弹劾案里的资金罗生门

一家做香蕉干生意的菲律宾公司，收到了来自中国大陆和香港的汇款；几个月后，这笔钱成了副总统弹劾案的证据、国家安全委员会的议题，以及政坛各方争夺的叙事高地。据路透社报道，菲律宾国家安全委员会10月6日表示，正在评估副总统莎拉·杜特尔特弹劾审讯期间披露的资金信息——一家与她丈夫有关联的公司收到的汇款，因被银行标记为可疑交易而上报反洗钱委员会。一场本应聚焦“来源不明财富”的宪政程序，正在滑向一场围绕“中国资金”的罗生门。

## 数字精确，性质模糊

先看数字。反洗钱委员会执行主任布埃纳文图拉10月5日在弹劾庭第33天作证时给出明细：一家食品制造公司——香蕉干出口商凯乐88食品公司（Cale88 Foods Corporation）——收到来自中国大陆和香港实体的汇款共计3.19亿比索（约3660万元人民币），其中约3.16亿比索来自中国大陆，约339万比索来自香港。这笔汇款被控方列为弹劾条款中“积累来源不明财富”一项的证据之一；控方还要求审查莎拉夫妇申报的资产、负债与净资产，核对申报数字是否与收入和金融交易相符。

但证词自己也承认了边界：布埃纳文图拉明确表示，他拿到的记录只有金额与来源地，无法判断这些交易是否指向洗钱，汇款人的身份与汇款用途均未查明。换言之，目前摆在桌面上的，是一组没有情节的数字。

## 一家食品公司与消失的股权

凯乐88并非凭空捏造的符号。据《菲律宾明星报》报道，莎拉的丈夫、律师卡皮奥在2021年至2024年间是这家公司的发起人、董事和股东，持股比例2024年升至47.5%，到2025年又悄然归零。莎拉本人从未持股，但控方认为，丈夫的商业往来足以纳入对副总统“来源不明财富”的指控。布埃纳文图拉还披露，这对夫妇账户中被上报的“须申报与可疑交易”总额达44亿比索（约5.0亿元人民币），低于他此前在众议院出示的67亿比索（约7.7亿元人民币）——因为涉事银行修正了自己的数据。

疑问也随之而来：一家出口香蕉干的公司，为什么会有来自海外的巨额汇款？目前没有人能回答。连控方证据都停留在“金额加来源地”的层面，缺的恰恰是最关键的一环——这些钱究竟是货款、借款、投资，还是别的什么。

## “中国连接”：政治叙事的万金油

把这件事推上头条的不是反洗钱报表，而是前参议员特里兰尼斯。10月2日，这位杜特尔特家族的宿敌在记者会上声称，中国政府及关联企业向凯乐88注入逾3.19亿比索，另有1.5亿比索（约1720万元人民币）的中国大使馆捐款流向莎拉创办的非营利组织“勇气与关怀联盟（Tapang at Malasakit）”——按该组织的财务报表，这笔捐款用于在达沃市修建13栋教学楼，并与教育部有协调记录。耐人寻味的是时机：众议院控方前一天刚把他从证人名单撤下，他随即选择在无需面对交叉质证的记者会上公布“猛料”。杜特尔特发言人帕内洛的反击不留情面：一个不敢宣誓作证的人，把指控留在了无法被检验的舞台上。

值得较真的是细节的分量。使馆捐款有报表、有教室、有对接部门；凯乐88的汇款至今只有金额和来源地。两件事被搅拌进同一个叙事——“杜特尔特家族拿中国的钱”——政治杀伤力瞬间拉满，证据链却悬在半空。

## 大使馆否认之后

10月5日，中国驻菲律宾大使馆否认向菲律宾任何政治派系提供资金，称相关说法是“虚假叙事”，呼吁有关人士停止政治操弄、不要把菲律宾内政拖进中菲关系。这份声明值得逐字读：它否认的是“向政治派系输血”，却没有——也不可能——逐一解释每一笔商业汇款的来龙去脉。另一边，国防部长特奥多罗喊话特里兰尼斯尽快提交证据，称指控“涉及国家安全”；众议员德利马则要求有关机构深挖此案。

说穿了，这场罗生门里每一方都在用“中国”这个词，但各方说的不是同一件事：反洗钱委员会说的是可疑交易报告，特里兰尼斯说的是外国政府的钱，大使馆说的是政治污名化，弹劾控方说的是来源不明财富。四个概念彼此缠绕，唯独证据还缺席。

## 华侨华人视角：合法资本不该替政治买单

这场风波里最容易被误伤的，是菲律宾的华侨华人商业社群。菲律宾华商经济植根于中小贸易与制造业，跨境汇款、贸易结算是日常经营的一部分。一旦“来自中国的汇款”被自动等同于“可疑”，最先承担审查成本的，将是那些与政治毫无瓜葛的华商企业和普通侨眷家庭。反洗钱制度的本意是追踪资金流向，而不是给资金来源地贴标签。

这里必须区分三件事：外国政府的官方资金、有商业凭证的企业汇款、真正的犯罪所得。第一类受国际法与国内法严格约束，第二类是正常的全球经济活动，第三类才轮到刑事调查。把三者混作一谈，既是程序上的偷懒，也是对整个华社的连坐——而连坐恰恰是“中国资金”叙事最危险的副作用。

## 十二月的判决与更长的阴影

弹劾庭预计12月作出裁决。无论结果如何，“中国资金”作为菲律宾政治斗争的万能标签，恐怕都会留了下来：它太好用了——既能指控对手“卖国”，又不必拿出完整的证据链。真正的考题因此不在参议院，而在制度韧性：反洗钱机制能否只按证据说话，司法程序能否把商业账与政治账分开算。对北京而言，教训同样直白——在东南亚，任何一笔资金的流向都可能被放大检视，透明本身就是最好的辩护。

## 延伸阅读

**反洗钱（Anti-Money Laundering）**：金融机构与监管机构识别、上报可疑资金流动的制度安排，核心工具是大额交易与可疑交易报告，判断依据是凭证与用途而非资金来源国。

**资本流动（Capital Flows）**：资金跨境移动的总称，贸易结算、投资与个人汇款皆属其列；其合规性取决于用途与文件，而非汇出地。

**弹劾（Impeachment）**：立法机关对高级官员启动的宪政审查程序，依菲律宾宪法由参议院出任弹劾法庭，处于政治审判与刑事调查的交叉地带。

# "Chinese remittances" shake Philippine politics: the money mystery in Sara Duterte's impeachment

A Philippine company that exports banana chips received remittances from mainland China and Hong Kong; months later, that money has become evidence in a vice-presidential impeachment, an agenda item for the National Security Council, and contested narrative territory for the country's politicians. According to Reuters, the Philippine National Security Council said on October 6 that it was reviewing financial information disclosed during the impeachment trial of Vice President Sara Duterte — remittances received by a company linked to her husband, flagged by banks as suspicious and reported to the Anti-Money Laundering Council. A constitutional process that should be about "unexplained wealth" is sliding into a whodunit about "Chinese funds."

## Precise numbers, vague substance

Start with the numbers. Testifying on day 33 of the trial on October 5, Anti-Money Laundering Council Executive Director Ronel Buenaventura gave the details: a food manufacturing company — the banana-chip exporter Cale88 Foods Corporation — received a total of 319 million pesos (about US$5.1 million) in inward remittances from entities in mainland China and Hong Kong, of which roughly 315.9 million pesos came from the mainland and 3.4 million from Hong Kong. Prosecutors have entered the remittances as evidence under the impeachment article accusing Duterte of amassing unexplained wealth, and have sought to scrutinize the couple's statements of assets, liabilities and net worth to check whether the declared figures match their income and financial dealings.

Yet the testimony itself marks the boundary: Buenaventura said plainly that the records he received contained only amounts and places of origin, that he could not conclude whether the transactions pointed to money laundering, and that neither the senders nor the purpose of the payments has been established. What sits on the table, in other words, is a set of numbers without a plot.

## A food company and the vanishing stake

Cale88 is no invented symbol. According to the Philippine Star, Duterte's husband, the lawyer Manases Carpio, was an incorporator, director and shareholder of the company from 2021 to 2024; his stake rose to 47.5 percent in 2024, then quietly fell to zero in 2025. Sara Duterte herself never held shares, but prosecutors argue that her husband's business dealings belong inside the accusation of "unexplained wealth" against the vice president. Buenaventura also disclosed that "covered and suspicious transactions" across the couple's accounts totaled 4.4 billion pesos (about US$70 million), down from the 6.7 billion pesos (about US$107 million) he had earlier presented to the House of Representatives — because the bank involved had corrected its own figures.

Hence the obvious question: why would a banana-chip exporter receive large remittances from abroad? Nobody can answer yet. Even the prosecution's evidence stops at the level of "amount plus origin." What is missing is precisely the most important link — whether this money is payment for goods, a loan, an investment, or something else entirely.

## The "China link": an all-purpose political narrative

What pushed the story onto front pages was not the anti-money-laundering paperwork but former senator Antonio Trillanes IV. On October 2, the Duterte family's longtime antagonist alleged at a press conference that the Chinese government and China-linked corporations had funneled more than 319 million pesos into Cale88, and that a 150-million-peso (about US$2.4 million) donation from the Chinese Embassy had gone to Tapang at Malasakit, a nonprofit founded by Duterte — a donation which, the group's financial statements say, was used to build 13 classroom buildings in Davao City under an agreement coordinated with the Department of Education. The timing is telling: the House prosecution had removed Trillanes from its witness list a day earlier, and he promptly chose a press conference — where cross-examination cannot reach — over the witness stand. Duterte's spokesman Paolo Panelo was unsparing: a man unwilling to testify under oath leaves his accusations on a stage where they can never be tested.

The details deserve scrutiny, because they do not carry equal weight. The embassy donation has financial statements, classrooms and a government counterpart; the Cale88 remittances so far have only amounts and places of origin. Stirred into a single narrative — "the Dutertes take Chinese money" — the political damage is instant, while the chain of evidence hangs in midair.

## After the embassy's denial

On October 5, the Chinese Embassy in Manila denied funding any Philippine political faction, called the claims a "false narrative," and urged those concerned to stop political manipulation and stop dragging Philippine domestic politics into China-Philippines relations. The statement deserves a careful reading: it denies state funding of political factions; it does not — and cannot — explain every commercial remittance line by line. On the other side, Defense Secretary Gilberto Teodoro urged Trillanes to submit his evidence, saying the claims "raise serious implications for national security," while Representative Leila de Lima demanded that government agencies dig deeper.

To put it bluntly, everyone in this affair is using the word "China," but they are not talking about the same thing: the Anti-Money Laundering Council means suspicious transaction reports; Trillanes means a foreign government's money; the embassy means political stigmatization; the impeachment prosecutors mean unexplained wealth. Four concepts twist around one another — and the evidence is still missing.

## The diaspora lens: legitimate capital should not pay for politics

The likeliest collateral damage in this affair is the Chinese-Filipino business community. Chinese-Filipino commerce is rooted in small and mid-sized trade and manufacturing; cross-border remittances and trade settlement are part of daily business. Once "money from China" is automatically read as "suspicious," the first to bear the cost of scrutiny will be Chinese-Filipino firms and ordinary diaspora families with no connection to politics whatsoever. The whole point of anti-money-laundering work is to follow the flow of funds, not to staple a label onto the country of origin.

Three things must be kept apart: official funds from a foreign government, corporate remittances backed by commercial documentation, and genuinely criminal proceeds. The first is tightly constrained by international and domestic law; the second is ordinary economic life; only the third belongs to criminal investigation. Collapsing the three into one category is procedural laziness — and collective punishment for an entire community, which is the most dangerous side effect of the "Chinese funds" narrative.

## A December verdict and a longer shadow

The impeachment court is expected to rule in December. Whatever the outcome, "Chinese funds" look set to survive as an all-purpose label in Philippine political combat: it is simply too useful — it accuses rivals of selling out the country without demanding a complete chain of evidence. The real test therefore lies not in the Senate chamber but in institutional resilience: whether the anti-money-laundering machinery can speak only through evidence, and whether the courts can keep commercial accounts and political accounts separate. For Beijing, the lesson is equally blunt — in Southeast Asia, the flow of any sum of money may be examined under a magnifying glass, and transparency is the best defense.

## Further reading

**Anti-Money Laundering**: the framework through which financial institutions and regulators identify and report suspicious fund flows; its core instruments are large-value and suspicious transaction reports, judged by documentation and purpose rather than by the sender's country.

**Capital Flows**: the umbrella term for cross-border movement of money — trade settlement, investment and personal remittances alike; compliance depends on use and paperwork, not on the point of origin.

**Impeachment**: the constitutional process by which the legislature puts senior officials on trial; under the Philippine Constitution the Senate sits as the impeachment court, at the intersection of political judgment and criminal investigation.
