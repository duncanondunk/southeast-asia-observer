# 菲律宾史上最大教育账单：1.37万亿比索，能填平"功能性文盲"的坑吗？

菲律宾参议院通过了2026年教育拨款1.37万亿比索（约23亿美元，约合164亿元人民币），规模创历史之最；教育经费占国内生产总值（GDP）比重首次达到约4%，终于踩上联合国教科文组织建议的4%—6%线。在6.793万亿比索（约119亿美元，约合845亿元人民币）的全年总预算中，教育拿走各部门最大的一块，其中教育部（DepEd）一家就分到了约9927亿比索（约174亿美元，约合1240亿元人民币）。官员们把这组数字称作历史性突破：教育部长洪雅拉（Sonny Angara）说，这是他从政以来见过的最大增幅，比去年高出约三成。

## 数字：史上最大的一笔教育经费

菲律宾参议院通过2026年教育拨款1.37万亿比索（约23亿美元，约合164亿元人民币），为该国史上最大的一笔教育经费。今年教育经费占国内生产总值（GDP）比重首次达到约4%，终于踩上联合国教科文组织建议的4%—6%线。在6.793万亿比索（约119亿美元，约合845亿元人民币）的全年总预算中，教育拿走各部门最大的一块；教育部（DepEd）一家即分到约9927亿比索（约174亿美元，约合1240亿元人民币）。官员称其为历史性突破——教育部长洪雅拉（Sonny Angara）表示，这是他从政记忆中最大的一次增幅，较上年高出约三成。

## 危机底色：1800万"功能性文盲"

然而欢呼声撞上了一组刺眼的数据。2026年5月，菲律宾统计局报告称，超过1800万名初高中毕业生属于"功能性文盲"——至多能认字，却读不懂意思。一项更宏观的识字调查把问题说得更直白：2019年单按读写与计算衡量，约7900万菲律宾人算"功能性识字"；去年把"理解能力"也纳入定义后，这个数字一下子跌到约6000万。换言之，标准一旦提高到"读懂"，近五分之一的人口就掉了队。这不是边角上的技能缺口，而是这套体系系统性失灵、等着这笔新钱去扭转的硬伤。

## 钱花在哪：教室、课本与餐桌

这笔钱并非空白支票，而是按系统的可见裂缝逐项划拨。约680亿比索（约12亿美元，约合85亿元人民币）用于建教室；290亿比索（约5.09亿美元，约合36亿元人民币）用于印制82种新课本，预计覆盖2000万名学生。校园供餐计划加码到2866亿比索（约5.03亿美元，约合36亿元人民币），把覆盖天数从120天拉长到一整个200天的学年，惠及逾480万儿童——这正对应"每三名菲童就有一名发育迟缓"的发现。另有893亿比索（约1.57亿美元，约合11亿元人民币）支撑学业恢复与可及学习（ARAL）补习计划，预计为44万名家教提供报酬，给670万名学生在阅读与数学上补课。纸面上，这些条目读起来像一份给破损体系的维修清单。

## 历史欠账：16.5万间教室的缺口

可欠账实在太大。诊断这场危机的菲律宾第二届国会教育委员会（EDCOM 2）估算，全国教室缺口达16.5万间，逼出人满为患的课堂和天不亮就开课的"黎明班"。新预算承诺新建数万间——不同部门口径在2.4万到3.5万间之间——但即便取上限，要填平这个量级的缺口也得数年，而工程鲜少能在单个预算周期内完工。洪雅拉自己承认，这2.4万间不会在365天内全部建成，有些项目通常要两三年。钱是必需的，但光有钱不够。

## 隐忧一：钱能否落到教室？

担忧随之从"给多少"转向"花得好不好"。一个长期瓶颈是建设周期本身。教室建设权从教育部移交给公共工程与公路部（DPWH）后，成效惨淡：洪雅拉去年10月说，在新机制下，全年目标1700间教室只建好21间。"看来他们是修防洪工程修出甜头来了，"他对本地记者揶揄道——这话戳向一个正身陷贪腐调查的部门。2026年预算试图打破僵局，允许教育部、地方政府甚至合格民间组织直接盖教室，但这些未经检验的渠道要立起来尚需时日。

## 隐忧二：腐败阴影与执行短板

防洪工程丑闻是房间里的大象。调查人员正在追查公共工程与公路部经手的疑似问题水坝与排水项目，教育界担心教室预算会落得同样下场——被截留、拖延或注水。曾主笔免学费法的参议员巴姆·阿基诺（Bam Aquino）呼吁民众盯紧支出，"确保资金真的进了教室、供餐计划、免费大学学费……而不是被贪腐吞掉"。这份谨慎并非假想：菲律宾在工程超支与采购漏损方面长期位居区域前列。一笔创纪录的预算，若没有创纪录的监督，恐怕会演变成创纪录的丑闻。

## 争议：是胜利还是"误导性的捷报"？

并非所有人都喝彩。关心教师联盟（Alliance of Concerned Teachers）主席鲁比·贝尔纳多（Ruby Bernardo）把"历史性预算"的说法斥为"误导性的捷报，掩盖了公立教育体系危机的严重性"。她的账算得很直白：如果预算要填的窟窿同样是创纪录的，那么创纪录的拨款毫无意义。她指出，占国内生产总值4.1%的支出，仍不足以给每所薄弱学校配齐师资、设备与校舍。支持者则反驳说，这份预算是来之不易的成果，只需严加看管——但"拨得出"与"落得实"之间的那段距离，恰恰是菲律宾教育此前屡屡栽倒的地方。

## 区域镜鉴：东南亚的教育竞赛

把镜头拉远，菲律宾并不孤单。在整个东南亚，疫情造成的学习损失叠加数字化浪潮，已把教育变成一场静悄悄的竞争力竞赛。越南凭借相对扎实的分数吸引了制造业投资；马来西亚与印尼正砸钱提升技术技能，以抢占人工智能产业链。菲律宾坐拥年轻、英语流利的人力红利，却眼睁睁看着这笔红利因基础能力薄弱而漏走。因此，更大的教育预算不只是一项社会开支，更是一场赌注——赌这个国家能否赶上那些花钱更快、交付也更利落的邻居。

# Philippines' largest-ever education budget: can ₱1.37 trillion fix a learning crisis?

The Philippine Senate has approved a ₱1.37 trillion (US$23 billion) allocation for education in 2026 — the single largest in the country's history. For the first time, education spending reaches roughly 4 per cent of gross domestic product, finally meeting the UNESCO benchmark that governments earmark between 4 and 6 per cent of GDP for schooling. Set against a national budget of ₱6.793 trillion (US$119 billion), education takes the biggest slice of any sector, and the Department of Education (DepEd) alone is slated to receive about ₱992.7 billion (US$17.4 billion). Officials have hailed the figure as historic: Education Secretary Sonny Angara called it the biggest increase in his political memory, up some 30 per cent on the previous year.

## The numbers: the largest education outlay in history

The Philippine Senate has approved a ₱1.37 trillion (US$23 billion) allocation for education in 2026 — the single largest in the country's history. For the first time, education spending reaches roughly 4 per cent of gross domestic product, meeting the UNESCO benchmark that governments earmark between 4 and 6 per cent of GDP for schooling. Against a national budget of ₱6.793 trillion (US$119 billion), education takes the largest share of any sector; the Department of Education (DepEd) alone is slated to receive about ₱992.7 billion (US$17.4 billion). Officials call it a historic breakthrough — Education Secretary Sonny Angara says it is the biggest increase in his political memory, up roughly 30 per cent on the previous year.

## The crisis backdrop: 18 million "functionally illiterate"

The celebration, however, collides with uncomfortable data. In May 2026 the Philippine Statistics Authority reported that more than 18 million junior and senior high school graduates were "functionally illiterate" — able, at best, to recognise words but unable to comprehend what they read. A broader literacy survey tells the same story in starker terms: when measured on reading, writing and numeracy alone, some 79 million Filipinos were counted as functionally literate in 2019; once comprehension was added to the definition last year, that number collapsed to around 60 million. In other words, nearly a fifth of the population lost their "literate" label the moment the bar was raised to include understanding. This is not a marginal skills gap; it is a systemic failure that the new money is meant to reverse.

## Where the money goes: classrooms, books and meals

The budget is not a blank cheque; it is earmarked for the visible cracks in the system. About ₱68 billion (US$1.2 billion) is set aside for classroom construction, while ₱29 billion (US$509 million) will fund the production of 82 new textbook titles expected to reach 20 million learners. The school feeding programme jumps to ₱28.66 billion (US$503 million), extending coverage from 120 days to the full 200-day school year and reaching more than 4.8 million children — a response to findings that one in three Filipino children is stunted. And ₱8.93 billion (US$157 million) underwrites the ARAL tutoring programme, projected to pay some 440,000 tutors to give catch-up lessons in reading and maths to 6.7 million learners. On paper, the line items read like a repair manual for a broken system.

## The accumulated deficit: a 165,000-classroom gap

Yet the deficit is enormous. EDCOM 2, the congressional commission that diagnosed the crisis, estimates a national shortage of 165,000 classrooms, forcing overcrowded conditions and "dawn classes" that start before sunrise. The new budget promises tens of thousands of new rooms — agency estimates range from 24,000 to 35,000 — but even the upper figure would take years to close a gap of that size, and construction rarely finishes within a single budget cycle. Angara himself conceded that not all 24,000 projected classrooms will be built in 365 days; some projects typically take two to three years. The money is necessary, but it is not, by itself, sufficient.

## Concern one: will the money reach the classrooms?

Here the worry shifts from how much to how well. A chronic bottleneck is the build cycle itself. Classroom construction was transferred away from DepEd to the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH), and the results have been dismal: Angara noted in October that only 21 of a targeted 1,700 classrooms had been completed that year under the new arrangement. "It seems that they've gotten spoiled from the flood control projects," he told local reporters — a pointed jab at an agency now mired in its own corruption scandal. The 2026 budget tries to break the logjam by allowing DepEd, local governments and even qualified civil-society groups to build classrooms directly, but unproven channels take time to stand up.

## Concern two: the shadow of corruption and weak execution

The flood-control scandal is the elephant in the room. As investigators probe allegedly irregular dam and drainage projects overseen by the DPWH, educators fear the classroom budget could meet the same fate — siphoned off, delayed or padded. Senator Bam Aquino, who authored the free-tuition law, urged citizens to monitor the spending "to ensure that the funds go to classrooms, feeding programmes, free college tuition… and not lose it to corruption." The caution is not hypothetical: the Philippines consistently ranks among the region's worst for construction overruns and procurement leakages. A record budget without record oversight risks becoming a record scandal.

## The debate: a victory or "misleading victory talk"?

Not everyone is cheering. Ruby Bernardo, chair of the Alliance of Concerned Teachers, dismissed talk of a "historic" budget as "misleading victory talk that obscures the severity of the crisis gripping the country's public education system." Her argument is arithmetical: a record-high budget is meaningless if the shortages it must address are also record-high. At 4.1 per cent of GDP, she notes, spending still falls short of what is needed to staff, equip and rebuild every underserved school. Supporters counter that the budget is a hard-won gain that simply requires vigilance — but the gap between "allocated" and "delivered" is precisely where Philippine education has stumbled before.

## A regional lens: Southeast Asia's education race

Zoom out, and the Philippines is not alone. Across Southeast Asia, pandemic learning loss compounded by rapid digitalisation has turned education into a quiet competitiveness contest. Vietnam's comparatively strong scores attracted manufacturing investment; Malaysia and Indonesia are pouring funds into technical skills to capture the AI supply chain. The Philippines, blessed with a young, English-speaking workforce, has watched that demographic dividend leak away through poor foundational skills. A bigger education budget is therefore not just a social spend — it is a bet on whether the country can keep pace with neighbours who are spending, and delivering, faster.

## Further reading

**EDCOM 2 (Second Congressional Commission on Education)**: A bicameral commission established by the Philippine Congress whose 2024 report systematically exposed structural failures in basic education — in teachers, classrooms, textbooks and nutrition — and became the blueprint for the 2026 budget reform.
**ARAL Program (Academic Recovery and Accessible Learning)**: A nationwide tutoring mechanism mandated by the Philippine ARAL Law, offering free one-on-one or small-group catch-up lessons in reading and maths to public-school students who fall behind; it received its first dedicated allocation of ₱8.93 billion in 2026.
**Functional Illiteracy**: A condition in which a person has basic reading, writing and numeracy skills yet cannot understand what they read or handle everyday information tasks; Philippine statistics in 2026 placed more than 18 million secondary graduates in this category.
