# 11月新规之下：侨胞的日常，正被这些"毛细血管"规则重塑

每逢月初，中国侨网都会梳理一批"将影响华侨华人生活"的新规。2026年11月的这份清单，表面看是税务、儿童用品、成品油开票、陪诊服务、签证、航空健康申报与欧盟清关费等七项技术性条文的堆叠，细读却藏着一条清晰的逻辑：海外侨胞与祖（籍）国的联结，正从宏大的"血脉认同"下沉到最具体的生活场景——跨境流动要不要签证、留守父母谁来陪诊、从淘宝寄出的包裹要不要多交一笔清关费。规则越是细碎，越能照见侨务治理的真实温度。

## 黑山撤免签：流动性从来不是单向开放

11月1日起，黑山取消对中国等五国公民的免签或签证便利化措施。中国驻黑山使馆随即提醒，持因私护照者赴黑须提前办妥签证。

这则看似不起眼的通知，戳破了一个流行错觉：不少人默认"中国越开放，中国人走出去就越容易"。事实恰恰相反。当中国大开国禁、对多国实施72/144小时过境免签、欢迎外国人来华之时，部分国家却在收紧对中国公民的入境便利。黑山并非个例，近年欧盟、北美多地对中国游客与商旅者的审查趋严已是常态。

对海外侨胞而言，这里有个微妙区分：已取得住在国国籍的侨胞，凭本国护照出行，基本不受中国出境签证政策影响；但持中国护照的侨眷、留学生与频繁往返者，却要实时盯紧目的地的新规。一条免签的撤销，提醒的是"流动性从来不是单向的"——开放是双行道，而侨胞恰好站在两条车道的交叉口。

## 陪诊"国标"：远程尽孝有了制度托底

11月1日实施的《社会化陪同就医服务 基本要求》（GB/T 47801—2026），是我国陪诊服务领域的首项国家标准。它要求陪诊人员经专业培训、掌握基础医学知识与应急流程，并取得合格证明，方可独立上岗。

这条标准与侨胞有何关系？答案藏在"远程尽孝"四个字里。千万海外侨胞的父母仍留在国内，年事渐高、就医频繁，子女却远隔重洋。过去，侨胞只能靠亲戚帮忙或临时雇人陪父母看病，服务质量参差不齐、纠纷难究。如今有了国标，意味着这一"孝心外包"的行业被纳入规范——侨胞在海外也能为父母预约合规、可追责的陪诊服务。

这恰是侨务政策"毛细血管"化的样本：国家未必直接替你照顾父母，却通过抬高服务门槛，让离散家庭的照护更可预期。它不喧哗，却比任何口号都实在。

## 社保罚则：归侨创业的隐性成本

同一天施行的《全国税务行政处罚裁量基准（2026年版）》，首次把社保缴费违法行为纳入全国统一处罚体系，用人单位欠缴社保最高罚3倍，并明确"自愿放弃社保协议"一律无效。

对在国内经商办厂的归侨、侨眷而言，这是一条切身的提醒。许多侨胞带着资金与经验回乡投资，雇工、开店、办厂，却未必熟悉内地劳动合规的细节。过去"员工自愿不交社保、公司省一笔"的灰色操作，在新基准下风险陡增。规则的统一，既保护劳动者，也倒逼侨企把合规成本算进账本。

换个角度看，这也是"引资"与"引制"并行的缩影：侨资回来，不光带钱，也要接住一套更精细的治理规则。

## 欧盟包裹费：侨胞"淘宝"乡愁要加价

11月1日起，欧盟依海关改革法规，对从境外直寄欧盟消费者的电商包裹加收单件固定清关处理费。这意味着，住在欧洲的法、德、荷等国侨胞，从淘宝、拼多多跨境直邮的衣物、零食、小家电，每单都可能多一笔费用。

这笔钱数额不大，象征意义却不小。跨境电商早已成为侨胞与故土之间最日常的"情感物流"——一箱螺蛳粉、一副老花镜、一件年货，承载的是隔着万里的乡愁。当清关费叠加，侨胞"一键下单寄乡愁"的成本被悄悄抬高，离散生活的物质纽带又紧了一扣。

它提醒我们：侨务不只是外交与认同，也藏在每一笔跨境包裹的账面上。

## 健康申报：公共卫生仍是流动的边界

11月3日起，美国疾控中心要求赴美或经美转机的旅客，登机前确认21天内未到过刚果民主共和国，以防范埃博拉经航空传入。

对经美转机的侨胞与留学生而言，这是又一道需提前准备的"边界手续"。公共卫生从来是流动性的隐形闸门：疫情三年已让所有人领教，一道健康令足以改写千万人的行程。埃博拉风险虽远在非洲中部，美国仍选择以航空申报前置拦截，说明"以防万一"的边境逻辑并未随疫情退场。

侨胞出行，既要看签证，也要看健康。规则清单里的这一条，是全球化下流动性脆弱性的一个微小注脚。

## 延伸阅读

**远程尽孝（Remote filial piety）**：指海外侨胞通过雇请家政、陪诊、远程照护等服务，跨越地理距离履行对国内父母的赡养义务，是离散家庭应对老龄化的典型策略。

**过境免签（Transit visa-free）**：中国对多国公民实施的72/144小时过境免签政策，便利外国人来华中转；其与部分国家收紧对华签证形成对照，凸显流动性的不对称。

**跨境直邮（Cross-border direct shipping）**：电商平台将商品从原产国直接寄达海外消费者的物流模式，是侨胞维系故土物质与情感联结的重要渠道。

# Under November's New Rules: How Micro-Regulations Reshape the Everyday Life of Overseas Chinese

Every month, China Qiaowang compiles a checklist of "new rules that will affect the lives of overseas Chinese." The November 2026 list looks, on the surface, like a pile of technical clauses — taxation, children's products, fuel receipts, companion-care services, visas, aviation health declarations, and EU customs handling fees. Read closely, however, it reveals a clear logic: the bond between overseas Chinese and their ancestral homeland is sinking from grand narratives of "shared blood" into the most concrete scenes of daily life. Will you need a visa to cross a border? Who accompanies your ageing parents to the hospital? Does the parcel you mailed from Taobao now carry an extra customs fee? The finer the rule, the more honestly it shows the real texture of diaspora governance.

## The Montenegro reversal: mobility was never a one-way open door

From November 1, Montenegro cancelled its visa-free or facilitative arrangements for citizens of China and four other countries. The Chinese embassy in Montenegro promptly reminded private-passport holders to obtain a visa in advance.

This minor notice punctures a popular illusion: many assume that "the more China opens up, the easier it becomes for Chinese citizens to go out." The opposite is often true. As China throws open its borders — rolling out 72/144-hour transit visa-free schemes and welcoming foreign visitors — some countries are tightening entry facilities for Chinese passport holders. Montenegro is not alone; tighter scrutiny of Chinese tourists and business travellers across the EU and North America has become routine in recent years.

For overseas Chinese, a subtle distinction matters. Those who have taken up the citizenship of their country of residence travel on that passport and are largely insulated from China's outbound visa policy. But relatives on Chinese passports, students, and frequent cross-border travellers must track destination rules in real time. The withdrawal of one visa waiver is a reminder that mobility was never one-directional — openness is a two-lane road, and the diaspora stands right at the intersection.

## A national standard for companion care: remote filial piety gets institutional backing

Implemented on November 1, the "Basic Requirements for Socialised Accompanied Medical Services" (GB/T 47801—2026) is China's first national standard for the companion-care industry. It requires attendants to receive professional training, master basic medical knowledge and emergency procedures, and hold a qualification certificate before working independently.

Why should this concern the diaspora? The answer lies in two words: remote filial piety. Millions of overseas Chinese have parents who remain in China — elderly, frequently unwell, yet separated from their children by oceans. In the past, emigrants could only rely on relatives or ad hoc hires to take parents to hospital, with uneven quality and little recourse when things went wrong. A national standard means this "outsourced filial duty" is now regulated: from abroad, a child can book compliant, accountable companion care for a parent.

This is precisely what diaspora governance looks like at the capillary level. The state may not care for your parents directly, but by raising the service threshold it makes care for dispersed families more predictable. It is quiet, yet more tangible than any slogan.

## A social-security penalty clause: the hidden cost of returnee entrepreneurship

Also effective that day, the "National Benchmark for Administrative Penalties in Taxation (2026 Edition)" brings social-security contribution violations into a unified national penalty system for the first time. Employers who underpay social insurance now face fines of up to three times the amount, and "voluntary waivers of social insurance" are declared uniformly invalid.

For returned overseas Chinese and their relatives who run businesses in China, this is a personal reminder. Many emigrants bring capital and experience home to invest, hire, open shops, or build factories, yet are not always fluent in the finer points of mainland labour compliance. The grey practice of "employees voluntarily skip social insurance so the company saves" now carries sharply higher risk. Uniform rules protect workers and force emigrant enterprises to price compliance into their books.

Seen another way, it mirrors the parallel tracks of "attracting capital" and "attracting institutions": diaspora capital returns not only with money but also with a set of more refined governance rules to absorb.

## The EU parcel fee: pricing the diaspora's Taobao nostalgia

From November 1, under its customs reform, the EU began charging a flat customs-handling fee on e-commerce parcels mailed directly from outside the bloc to consumers inside it. That means overseas Chinese in France, Germany, the Netherlands and elsewhere, who order clothes, snacks and small appliances via direct cross-border shipping from Taobao or Pinduoduo, may now pay a little extra per parcel.

The sum is small; the symbolism is not. Cross-border e-commerce long ago became the most everyday "affective logistics" between emigrants and their homeland — a box of luosifen noodles, a pair of reading glasses, a parcel of New Year goods, all carrying homesickness across ten thousand miles. As handling fees stack up, the cost of "one-click ordering homesickness" is quietly raised, tightening the material tie of dispersed life yet another notch.

It reminds us that diaspora affairs are not only diplomacy and identity; they also sit on the ledger of every cross-border parcel.

## The health declaration: public health remains a border

From November 3, the US Centers for Disease Control requires travellers bound for or transiting through the United States to confirm, before boarding, that they have not been to the Democratic Republic of the Congo in the preceding 21 days, to guard against Ebola entering by air.

For emigrants and students transiting the US, this is another "border procedure" to prepare in advance. Public health has always been the invisible gate of mobility: the three years of pandemic taught everyone that a single health order can rewrite the itineraries of millions. Though the Ebola risk sits in central Africa, the US still chooses to intercept it upstream with an aviation declaration — evidence that the "better safe than sorry" border logic has not receded with the pandemic.

For the diaspora, travel means watching both the visa and the health form. This one line in the rule list is a small footnote to the fragility of mobility under globalisation.

## Further reading

**Remote filial piety**: the strategy by which overseas Chinese fulfil filial duties toward ageing parents in China — through hired domestic help, companion care, or remote monitoring — across geographical distance; a typical response of dispersed families to population ageing.

**Transit visa-free**: China's 72/144-hour transit visa-free policy for citizens of many countries, facilitating stopovers; its contrast with other countries' tightening of visas for Chinese nationals highlights the asymmetry of mobility.

**Cross-border direct shipping**: a logistics model in which platforms mail goods straight from the origin country to overseas consumers, a vital channel through which emigrants sustain material and emotional links with the homeland.
