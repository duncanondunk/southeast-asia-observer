# 郑和清真寺：一张"中国面孔"里的离散与归属

> 一座飞檐翘角的清真寺，如何成为华侨华人在印尼（Indonesia）寻找归属的注脚。

在印尼第二大城市泗水，矗立着一座红墙绿瓦、飞檐翘角的清真寺——那是公认的中国寺庙建筑轮廓。这便是郑和清真寺，全印尼第一座以十五世纪明朝航海家郑和命名的清真寺。它悄然抛出一个克制却尖锐的问题：在一个穆斯林占人口多数的国家，一座宗教建筑"长着中国面孔"，究竟意味着什么？

## 一个名字的三重用法

以郑和命名一座清真寺，是一次精心而多层的举动。郑和——本名马和，云南籍的穆斯林宦官——于 1405 至 1433 年间七下西洋，屡次停靠印尼群岛。援引他的名字，等于同时调动三重记忆：中华海洋文明的辐射力、早于欧洲殖民便已存在于此的伊斯兰共同信仰，以及一段以贸易而非征服定义的交往叙事。

最后这一点尤为关键。对印尼的华侨华人——这个曾在苏哈托（Suharto）"新秩序"（1966–1998）时期经历限制同化政策、华文学校被关闭、华裔身份被强制更改、并在 1998 年五月事件中遭遇暴力的少数族群——郑和提供了一件稀缺之物：一个"非威胁性"的中国符号。他来过、贸易过、未留下殖民地。在一个将"华人属性"长期编码为异己、乃至潜在颠覆的国家，一位和平到访的海军将领，是一段"可用的过去"。

2002 年落成、部分参照北京（Beijing）牛街清真寺形制的这座寺院，因此借助历史记忆完成了一种政治工作：它把华人在印尼的存在，重新框定为不是殖民残余、也不是买办式的闯入，而是一段更为古老、且被彼此承认的海洋邂逅。

## 建筑即论辩

端详这座建筑，论点便化为实体。八角屋顶、红色立柱、飞檐翘角、绿釉瓦——这是中华宗教建筑的语法；而殿内，阿拉伯文书法沿着礼拜大殿铺展，信众如印尼任何一座清真寺那样席地而坐。设计同时借用中华、爪哇与伊斯兰三种传统，拒绝让任何一方独占所有权。

建筑从来不止是美学，它是在空间里书写的、关于"谁属于哪里"的论辩。在一个族裔华人常被刻画为永远置身原住民（*pribumi*）行列之外的商业少数群体的穆斯林国家里，一座"中国面孔"的清真寺，正是在申明：身为华人的身份，与伊斯兰归属并不对立——人可以在中式屋檐下祈祷，却依然完全属于这片土地。

这也正是基金会那句措辞的用心所在——"这不是中国建筑搬到印尼，而是印尼文化的一部分"。这句话说得谨慎，它拒绝了那种"离散博物馆"的逻辑——把故土文化在异乡装进玻璃罩里保存；它主张的是一种真正的融合：中华元素不是外来沉积，而是已被印尼宗教生活吸收的一层。

## 一张网络，而非一座纪念碑

单座清真寺或可被当作奇观看待。但哈夷郑和基金会已在印尼各地建起十九座郑和清真寺，这就改变了问题的尺度。这不是一座纪念碑，而是一套基础设施——一个由站点构成、共同重新编码"华人身份"与"印尼伊斯兰"之间关系的网络。

"基础设施"在此是恰切的词。每一座清真寺同时也是一个社会机构：固然是礼拜之所，也是学校、图书馆、社区节点。泗水这座院落里设有教授华文的伊斯兰寄宿学校（*pesantren*），图书馆书架上摆着中文书，学生们据说还会问起何时能去北京。这是最贴地气的软实力——不靠使馆广播，而织进祈祷与日常教育的节奏里。宗教学校内的语言教学，消弭了"文化外交"与普通人生活之间的惯常距离。

## 航海家与海权争议

值得在此停顿片刻，看看这套叙事抚平了什么。郑和故事之所以讨喜，部分正因为它属于前现代——那是在现代国家体系、专属经济区、以及"九段线"成为南海（South China Sea）焦点之前的时代。清真寺所礼赞的同一海洋传统，在当代地缘政治里，恰恰成了北京对那些印尼、越南（Vietnam）、菲律宾（Philippines）、马来西亚（Malaysia）皆存争议的水域提出"历史存在"主张的依据。

清真寺里的郑和，是一位外交人物：穆斯林、商人、交往者；而海权争议话语里的郑和，是一位主权人物：争议水域中"古代中国存在"的凭证。这是同一段历史，却被调动去服务相反的目的。由此观之，这座清真寺不仅是文化间的桥，也是一次安静的取舍——选择了"连接"的那位郑和，而非"声索"的那位。

## 离散的归属，慢慢建起

郑和清真寺最终揭示的，是离散群体的归属从来不是被宣告的，而是被建构的——一砖一瓦、一课一堂、一名一字。印尼华人花了数十年协商他们被接纳的条件：通过皈依、通过通婚、通过 1998 年之后华文与中华文化缓慢地重新合法化。一座以穆斯林华人命名的清真寺，正是那场漫长协商中的一个节点。

它也在提醒：离散群体不单靠记忆存活。它们靠基础设施存活——那所教授母语的寄宿学校、那座继续兴建下一座清真寺的基金会、那个周五聚在既像家又像天的屋檐下的社群。媒介是建筑，它要传的话只有一句：我们会一直在。

## 结语

在泗水那片飞檐之下，信仰、记忆与身份仍在被协商。郑和清真寺不是献给一段已完结过去的纪念碑，而是一场用砖瓦木石写就的、进行中的论辩——关于一个少数群体如何既能是华人、又能是印尼人，既能是穆斯林、又能是它自身；也关于一位十五世纪的将领，如何仍在完成任何使馆都难以完成的那种外交工作。

# The Mosque with a Chinese Face: Zheng He, Diaspora, and the Architecture of Belonging in Surabaya

> How a fifteenth-century admiral's name became a vocabulary for Chinese-Indonesian coexistence.

In Surabaya, Indonesia's second-largest city, there stands a mosque with red walls, green tiles, and upturned eaves — the unmistakable silhouette of Chinese temple architecture. It is the Zheng He Mosque, the first in Indonesia named for the fifteenth-century Ming admiral, and it asks a quiet but pointed question: what does it mean for a religious building to wear a "Chinese face" in the world's largest Muslim-majority country?

## A Name That Does Three Things at Once

Naming a mosque after Zheng He is a deliberate, layered act. Zheng He — born Ma He, a Muslim eunuch of Yunnan origin — commanded seven voyages across the Indian Ocean between 1405 and 1433, calling repeatedly at the Indonesian archipelago. To invoke him is to mobilize three memories simultaneously: the reach of Chinese maritime civilization, a shared Islamic faith that predates European colonialism in the region, and a narrative of contact defined by trade rather than conquest.

This last point matters. For overseas Chinese communities in Indonesia — a minority that endured the restrictive assimilation policies of Suharto's New Order (1966–1998), the closure of Chinese-language schools, the forced renaming of Chinese identities, and the violence of May 1998 — the figure of Zheng He offers something scarce: a Chinese symbol that is non-threatening. He came, he traded, he left no colony. In a country where Chineseness has historically been coded as alien and potentially subversive, an admiral who arrived in peace is a usable past.

The mosque, completed in 2002 and modeled in part on Beijing's Niujie Mosque, thus does political work through historical memory. It reframes the Chinese presence in Indonesia not as a colonial residue or a comprador intrusion, but as part of a much older, mutually recognized maritime encounter.

## Architecture as Argument

Look at the building and the argument becomes material. Octagonal roofs, red pillars, upturned eaves, green-glazed tiles — these are the grammar of Chinese religious architecture. Inside, Arabic calligraphy lines the prayer hall; worshippers sit on the floor as in any Indonesian mosque. The design borrows from Chinese, Javanese, and Islamic traditions at once, refusing to let any single source claim ownership.

Architecture is never only aesthetic. It is an argument made in space about who belongs where. In a Muslim-majority nation where ethnic Chinese have often been cast as a commercial minority forever outside the *pribumi* (indigenous) fold, a mosque that looks Chinese is making a claim: that Chineseness and Islamic belonging are not opposed — that one can pray under a Chinese roof and still be fully of this place.

This is why the foundation's insistence matters — "this is not Chinese architecture transplanted to Indonesia, but a part of Indonesian culture." The phrasing is careful. It refuses the logic of the diaspora museum, where the homeland's culture is preserved under glass in a foreign setting. Instead it asserts synthesis: the Chinese elements are not a foreign deposit but an absorbed stratum of Indonesian religious life.

## A Network, Not a Monument

A single mosque might be read as a curiosity. But the Haji Zheng He Foundation has built nineteen Zheng He mosques across Indonesia. That changes the scale of the question. This is not a monument but an infrastructure — a network of sites that together recode the relationship between Chinese identity and Indonesian Islam across the archipelago.

Infrastructure, here, is the right word. Each mosque is also a social institution: a place of worship, yes, but also a school, a library, a community node. The Surabaya complex houses an Islamic school (*pesantren*) that teaches Chinese; its library shelves Chinese books; its students ask, reportedly, when they might visit Beijing. This is soft power at its most granular — not broadcast from an embassy, but woven into the daily rhythms of prayer and education. Language instruction inside a religious school collapses the usual distance between "cultural diplomacy" and ordinary life.

## The Admiral and the Sea Dispute

It is worth pausing on what this narrative smooths over. The Zheng He story is congenial in part because it is pre-modern — it belongs to a time before the modern state system, before exclusive economic zones, before the Nine-Dash Line became a flashpoint of the South China Sea. The same maritime tradition that the mosque celebrates is, in contemporary geopolitics, the basis for Beijing's expansive historical claims in waters that Indonesia, Vietnam, the Philippines, and Malaysia contest.

The Zheng He of the mosque is a diplomatic figure: a Muslim, a trader, a man of contact. The Zheng He of the maritime-dispute discourse is a sovereignty figure: proof of "ancient Chinese presence" in contested waters. These are the same history, mobilized for opposite purposes. The mosque, in this light, is not only a bridge between cultures but also a quiet act of selection — choosing the admiral who connects over the admiral who claims.

## Diaspora Belonging, Built Slowly

What the Zheng He Mosque ultimately reveals is that belonging for a diaspora is not declared but constructed — brick by brick, lesson by lesson, name by name. The Indonesian Chinese have spent decades negotiating the terms of their inclusion: through conversion, through intermarriage, through the slow re-legitimation of Chinese language and culture after 1998. A mosque named for a Chinese Muslim is one node in that longer negotiation.

It is also a reminder that diasporas do not survive on memory alone. They survive on infrastructure — the pesantren that teaches the language, the foundation that builds the next mosque, the community that gathers on Fridays under eaves that look like home and a roof that looks like heaven. The medium is architecture; the message is permanence.

## Conclusion

Under the upturned eaves in Surabaya, faith, memory, and identity are still being negotiated. The Zheng He Mosque is not a monument to a finished past. It is an ongoing argument, made in tile and timber, about how a minority can be both Chinese and Indonesian, both Muslim and itself — and about how a fifteenth-century admiral can still do diplomatic work that no embassy quite can.
