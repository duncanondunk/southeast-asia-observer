在马来西亚柔佛州（Johor）永平（Yong Peng）的油棕（oil palm）园里，六十八岁的小园主钟贵华（Chung Kee Wa）读懂了炎热的语法。三年以下的油棕几乎不遮阳，他便把喷药、施肥与除草挪到清晨与傍晚。"我们尽量避开正午去园里，"他说。多带一瓶水，自己决定何时歇脚——身为小园主，他尚且有这样的余裕。

## 正午的缺席

钟贵华的作息，是东南亚极端高温下无数露天劳动者的缩影。当白昼的热度攀升，许多人把重活移向晨昏。但灵活从来不是人人可得的奢侈：受雇于人或受产量考核的工人，作息由他人或指标决定，正午的烈日里，他们往往没有说"慢下来"的权利。热，成了一种隐形的工时与风险。

## 评估的缺位

更深的裂缝在制度层面。一份报告指，东南亚多座城市缺乏充分的极端高温风险评估，用以保护露天劳动者的规则或薄弱、或悬置。当气温屡破纪录，城市却仍未把"热"当作一项可测量的职业危害来对待。没有评估，就没有阈值；没有阈值，防护便无从谈起。

## 谁是脆弱者

暴露在热下的，远不止油棕小园主。建筑工、环卫工、外卖与快递骑手、田间农人，构成区域最庞大也最沉默的露天劳动群。女性、高龄者与非正规就业者尤为脆弱——他们既少有议价能力，又常缺乏社保兜底。气候变化的成本，正悄悄从大气沉降到这些最无力转嫁的肩膀上。

## 适应从制度开始

修补裂缝，不能只靠个人多带一瓶水。报告指向的方向清晰：建立可执行的高温劳动标准，把中暑明确纳入职业病目录，让雇主承担避暑与轮休的硬性义务。当热浪从偶发变为常态，劳动保护的防线必须前移。城市若想把"韧性"写进蓝图，先得把劳动者从正午的烈日里接回来。

## 一场静默的气候账单

极端高温照见的，不只是气温计上的数字，更是一道社会保障的裂缝。当发展在热浪中继续向前，真正衡量文明成色的，是那些在正午仍要户外谋生的人，能否被制度温柔地接住。风会停，热不退；唯制度可御暑。

## 延伸阅读

**油棕（Oil Palm）**：热带经济作物，其果仁可榨取棕榈油，广泛种植于马来西亚与印尼，是区域农业与出口的支柱之一。

**职业病（Occupational Disease）**：因职业活动引起的疾病，将中暑纳入目录可使受热劳动者获得法定保障与补偿。

**非正规就业（Informal Employment）**：缺乏正式劳动合同与社保覆盖的用工形态，在东南亚露天劳动中占比可观，抗风险能力弱。

===EN===

On an oil palm plantation in Yong Peng, Malaysia's southern Johor state, 68-year-old smallholder Chung Kee Wa has learned the grammar of heat. Oil palms under three years old offer little shade, so he moves spraying, fertilising and weeding to the early morning and evening. "We try our best to avoid going to the plantation around noon," he says. He carries extra water and, as a smallholder, decides himself when to slow down—a latitude not everyone enjoys.

## The Absence at Noon

Chung's routine is a microcosm of countless outdoor workers across Southeast Asia under extreme heat. As daytime temperatures climb, many shift heavy work to dawn and dusk. But flexibility is hardly a universal luxury: workers employed by others, or measured by output, have schedules set by employers or quotas, and at noon's glare they often hold no right to say "slow down." Heat becomes an invisible tax on hours and a quiet risk.

## The Missing Risk Assessments

A deeper fissure lies in institutions. A report finds that several Southeast Asian cities lack adequate extreme-heat risk assessments, and the rules meant to protect outdoor workers are weak or unenforced. As temperatures repeatedly break records, cities still fail to treat "heat" as a measurable occupational hazard. No assessment means no threshold; no threshold means protection cannot even begin.

## Who Is Exposed

Those exposed go far beyond oil-palm smallholders. Construction crews, sanitation workers, food-delivery and courier riders, and field farmers form the region's largest yet most silent outdoor labour force. Women, the elderly and informal workers are especially vulnerable—they have little bargaining power and thin social-security cover. The cost of climate change is quietly settling from the atmosphere onto the shoulders least able to pass it on.

## Adaptation Begins With Rules

Patching the fissure cannot rest on individuals carrying one more bottle of water. The report points a clear way: build enforceable heat-labour standards, explicitly list heatstroke as an occupational disease, and make employers bear hard obligations for cooling and rotation. As heatwaves shift from occasional to routine, the line of labour protection must move forward. If cities want "resilience" in their blueprints, they must first bring workers back from the midday sun.

## A Silent Climate Bill

Extreme heat exposes not just numbers on a thermometer but a crack in the social safety net. As development pushes on through the heat, what truly measures a civilisation's quality is whether those who must labour outdoors at noon are gently caught by institutions. Winds pause; heat does not. Only institutions can shield against summer.

## Further reading

**Oil Palm**: A tropical cash crop whose fruit yields palm oil, widely grown in Malaysia and Indonesia and a pillar of the region's agriculture and exports.

**Occupational Disease**: Illness caused by work activity; listing heatstroke brings legally mandated protection and compensation to heat-exposed workers.

**Informal Employment**: Work without formal contracts or social-security cover, sizable in Southeast Asia's outdoor labour and weak in shock resistance.
