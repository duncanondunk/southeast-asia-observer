# 从端窝点到立规矩：马尼拉反诈声明能走多远

## 罕见的一致：十九国在马尼拉找到共同语言

11月17日，第21届东亚峰会（East Asia Summit）在菲律宾（Philippines）马尼拉（Manila）举行。据《日经亚洲》（Nikkei Asia）报道，与会领导人通过了一份专门针对网络诈骗及相关犯罪的联合声明。这个圈子不小：东盟（ASEAN）11国，加上中国、美国、俄罗斯、印度（India）、日本（Japan）、韩国（South Korea）、澳大利亚（Australia）、新西兰（New Zealand）8个伙伴国，共19个政府。能让这19方在同一份文件上签字，本身就是一条新闻。

更耐人寻味的是同一场峰会的另一个细节。据日经亚洲报道，美国方面曾试图淡化声明中关于气候变化与多样性的表述。也就是说，在一堆容易吵起来的议题上，各方最终能一致落笔的，是「打击诈骗」。这不是说反诈不重要，而是说它恰好踩在了各方利益的交集上：没有领土争议，没有制度对抗，谁都可以支持。国际会议最有意思的地方，往往不是签成了什么，而是为什么偏偏是这一件签成了。

## 数字：一年883亿至1141亿美元的损失

支撑这份声明的，是一组不太好看的数字。联合国毒品和犯罪问题办公室（UNODC）7月发布的报告估算，2025年东亚、东南亚、澳大利亚和新西兰的受害者因诈骗合计损失883亿至1141亿美元（约6358亿至8215亿元人民币）。另一个口径来自全球反诈骗联盟（GASA），其估算的全球年度损失约4420亿美元（约3.18万亿元人民币）；该联盟对42个市场4.6万名成年人的调查显示，57%的人在过去一年遇到过诈骗，23%的人真的丢了钱。

人力成本更直观。金融行动特别工作组（FATF）11月5日的报告估计，仅缅甸（Myanmar）一国就有约12万人被强迫从事网络诈骗。这些数字彼此口径不同，不能简单相加，但方向一致：诈骗已经不是零散的街头犯罪，而是一条跨国的产业链——有人负责招工，有人负责运送，有人负责话术，有人负责洗钱。任何只盯着最后一个环节的方案，都低估了对手。

## 从情报到钱流：声明究竟写了什么

这份声明的全名是《东亚峰会领导人关于加强打击网络诈骗及相关犯罪合作的声明》。它不是又一句「严重关切」。按已披露的文本，各国承诺共享情报、开展联合调查、加强司法协助与引渡安排，并加快追查、冻结、扣押和返还犯罪所得；协调对象也不只警察，还包括金融情报机构、电信监管部门、金融机构与数字平台，并鼓励通过东盟警察组织（ASEANAPOL）与国际刑警组织（INTERPOL）开展协同行动。

在防范端，声明提出「安全内建」与「隐私内建」原则、身份验证、安全认证和欺诈检测系统，并把网络诈骗风险纳入数字与金融扫盲教育。

点评：从「关掉窝点」到「顺着资金流和电话卡往上查」，这是一个实质升级。诈骗是一门生意，打掉几个园区只是拆了厂房；账户、通道、平台不被动，钱还是会重新流回新的厂房。

## 受害者条款：遣返之后还有更难的事

声明的另一段值得单独拿出来说：各国将加强筛查、识别和保护受害者，促其安全及时返乡并重返社会，防止再次受害，并鼓励受害者参与调查和起诉。同时强调应对须符合国际法和人权原则。

这不是套话。近年来，包括菲律宾和柬埔寨（Cambodia）在内的东南亚国家都通报过这样的案例：工人被招聘中介以高薪诱骗入境，抵达后被扣留、威胁甚至虐待，被迫从事诈骗；想逃跑的人反而面临暴力。印度（India）政府公布的数据显示，2022年至2025年间从柬埔寨、老挝（Laos）和缅甸救回的印度公民达6998人，且逐年上升。

问题在于，「受害者」和「参与者」的界限在实践中常常模糊。一个人可能先是被骗来的，后来又被迫骗别人；如果筛查标准不严，遣返就可能变成把人推回同一个招聘陷阱。声明写到了「不二次受害」，这是进步；但没有统一的筛查标准、没有安置资金，这句话暂时还只是方向。

## 贫困、腐化与过期法律：最刺眼的一段

整份声明里最有分量的，可能是这段看似「务虚」的表述：网络诈骗的深层动因包括贫困、社会保护不足、国内法律过时、公私部门协调缺口、腐败以及经济机会有限。

说实话，这段话等于承认了一件事：只要年轻人的合法出路不够，窝点就会源源不断地招到人。扫荡能抓人，抓完人还得回答「他为什么当初会去」。这比跨境追赃更难，因为它落在各国的财政、就业与基层治理上，不是一次联合行动能解决的。

但也要看到另一面：点出病因容易，开出处方难。声明没有为社会保障、法律修订或就业项目列出任何资金安排和时间表。把结构性问题写进文件，是诚实；不写谁出钱，则是这份文件仍然停留在宣言层面的证据。

## 谁来执行：一份没有预算和时钟的清单

东亚峰会是领导人战略对话平台，共识决策、文件不具约束力。这次的声明列出了一长串该做的事——情报共享、联合调查、引渡、追赃、平台协作——但没有预算、没有监督机构、没有履约评估，也没有明确的完成期限。

据日经亚洲报道，菲律宾总统小马科斯在会上呼吁整合执法、金融、电信、数字与司法机构。方向没错，但对19个司法辖区来说，这几乎是最难的一种协作：证据标准不同、数据跨境规则不同、引渡条约网络残缺，连「什么算犯罪所得」都可能各说各话。声明越长，落点越轻——这是区域多边文件的通病，不应因为议题重要就免于追问。

## 中国角色：绕不开的协作方

对中国和东南亚的华侨华人而言，这份声明并非远方的外交新闻。中国既是被诱骗出境务工人员的来源国之一，也是诈骗集团最重要的目标市场之一；而在东南亚经商、求学、探亲的华侨华人，同样可能成为精准话术的对象。

反过来说，任何有效的跨境机制都绕不开中国的参与：资金流经的银行渠道、嫌疑人可能回流的路径、受害者返乡后的安置，都需要中方执法与金融监管部门的实际配合。这次中国在这份声明的签署方之列，这是必要的一步，但「签了」和「接通了」是两回事。真正的检验是第一起三方联合调查、第一次跨境冻结账户、第一批按统一标准完成筛查的返乡人员。

这里还要加一句提醒：华侨华人社群因为语言、亲缘和商业网络密集，既容易成为诈骗的目标，也能成为预警和防骗教育最有效的渠道。把他们当成天然的怀疑对象，既不公平，也会让执法失去最重要的帮手。

## 为什么是现在：最低公分母的多边主义

把视线拉回整场峰会：南海行为准则、缅甸局势、能源安全，每一项都能让会场分裂。据日经亚洲报道，美国还在试图弱化气候与多样性方面的措辞。在这样的背景下，网络诈骗成了少有的「谁都能同意」的议题。

这解释了为什么各国还纷纷往这份共识上挂自己的项目：印度总理莫迪提议由印度国家法医科学大学承办东亚峰会网络犯罪会议；韩国总统李在明宣布明年启动韩国—东盟反诈执法合作项目；越南总理提议建立东亚峰会执法热线网络；泰国则推自己的全球反网络诈骗伙伴关系。一致同意的背后，是各自盘算。

这不等于声明没有价值。最低公分母往往是可执行度的起点——先在不敏感的领域把情报和资金追查的管道接通，再谈更难的事。但也不该把它误读成战略趋同。19国能在反诈上站在一起，不代表它们在其他问题上站在一起。

## 结语：声明是起点，不是终点

平心而论，把网络诈骗写进东亚峰会的领导人声明，本身就是一个信号：这已经不是某个国家的治安问题，而是区域治理的必答题。清单也确实比过去更细，尤其是追赃和受害者保护两块。

但东南亚并不缺声明。这个地区最不缺的，就是写得很好的共同文件。真正的考试在往后：第一次联合调查能不能办成，第一笔被骗资金能不能冻结并返还，第一批获救者能不能得到筛查、安置而不是二次伤害。一年之后，如果只剩下一份声明，它就只是书架上多了一份文件；如果有了案子和账户，那才是一次真正的制度建设。

## 延伸阅读

**东亚峰会（East Asia Summit）**：2005年设立的领导人战略对话机制，由东盟11国与澳大利亚、中国、印度、日本、新西兰、韩国、俄罗斯、美国8个伙伴国组成，每年举行。

**诈骗中心（Scam centres）**：指犯罪集团以高薪招聘诱骗并扣留外来劳工、迫使其从事网络诈骗的园区，报道见于缅甸、柬埔寨、老挝、菲律宾与马来西亚等地；金融行动特别工作组估计仅缅甸就有约12万人被迫参与。

**犯罪所得追缴（Recovery of proceeds of crime）**：对诈骗资金进行追踪、冻结、扣押与返还的执法环节，直接打击犯罪的经济动机；跨境执行依赖司法协助与引渡安排，是声明中最难落地的一项。

# From raids to rules: how far can Manila's anti-scam statement go

## A rare consensus: nineteen governments find common ground in Manila

On 17 November, the 21st East Asia Summit was held in Manila, the Philippines. According to Nikkei Asia, leaders adopted a dedicated joint statement on online scams and related offences. The room was large: all 11 ASEAN members plus eight partners — China, the United States, Russia, India, Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand. Nineteen governments putting their names to one document is itself news.

There is a more revealing detail from the same summit. Nikkei Asia also reported that Washington sought to soften language on climate change and diversity. In other words, among the issues most likely to divide the room, the one the parties could finally agree on was fighting scams. That is not to say the issue is trivial. It is to say it sits precisely at the intersection of their interests: no territorial dispute, no ideological confrontation, something everyone can endorse. The interesting question about a multilateral meeting is often not what was signed, but why this particular thing was signed.

## The numbers: US$88.3 to 114.1 billion lost in a year

Behind the statement sits an unflattering set of figures. A July report by the UN Office on Drugs and Crime estimated that victims in East Asia, Southeast Asia, Australia and New Zealand lost between US$88.3 billion and US$114.1 billion to scams in 2025. A separate estimate from the Global Anti-Scam Alliance (GASA) puts global annual losses at about US$442 billion; its survey of 46,000 adults across 42 markets found that 57% had encountered a scam in the previous 12 months and 23% had lost money.

The human cost is starker. A 5 November report by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) estimated that about 120,000 people were being forced to carry out online scams in Myanmar alone. These figures use different definitions and cannot simply be added together, but they point the same way: fraud is no longer street crime writ large; it is a transnational production chain. Some recruit, some transport, some write the scripts, some launder the proceeds. Any response that watches only the last link underestimates the opponent.

## From intelligence to money flows: what the statement actually says

The full title is the East Asia Summit Leaders' Statement on Strengthening Cooperation Against Online Scams and Related Offences. This is not another expression of "grave concern." Under the disclosed text, governments commit to sharing intelligence, conducting joint investigations, strengthening mutual legal assistance and extradition arrangements, and moving faster to trace, freeze, seize and recover criminal proceeds. The agencies named extend beyond the police to financial intelligence units, telecommunications regulators, financial institutions and digital platforms, with coordinated operations encouraged through ASEANAPOL and Interpol.

On prevention, the statement calls for security-by-design and privacy-by-design, identity verification, secure authentication and fraud-detection systems, and the inclusion of scam risks in digital and financial literacy programmes.

Worth noting: moving from "shut down the compounds" to "follow the money and the SIM cards" is a genuine upgrade. Fraud is a business. Razing a compound dismantles a factory; unless accounts, payment rails and platforms are touched, the capital simply rebuilds elsewhere.

## The victim clause: the harder work after repatriation

Another passage deserves separate attention. Governments will strengthen screening, identification and protection of victims, facilitate their safe and timely return and reintegration, prevent re-victimisation, and encourage survivors to take part in investigations and prosecutions. Responses, the statement adds, must be consistent with international law and respect human rights.

That is not boilerplate. Authorities in Southeast Asian countries including the Philippines and Cambodia have described workers lured across borders by recruitment syndicates with promises of high wages, then confined, threatened or tortured and forced to run scams. India's government has said that 6,998 Indian nationals were rescued from cybercrime centres in Cambodia, Laos and Myanmar between 2022 and 2025, with the annual number rising sharply.

The difficulty is that the line between victim and participant is often blurred in practice. A person may be deceived into coming and later coerced into deceiving others. Without rigorous screening, repatriation can become a return ticket to the same recruitment trap. The statement's commitment to preventing re-victimisation is progress, but without common screening standards or funding for reintegration, it remains a direction rather than a delivery.

## Poverty, corruption and outdated laws: the sharpest passage

The most consequential language may be the passage that looks most abstract: the underlying drivers of online scams include poverty, inadequate social protection, outdated domestic laws, gaps in public-private coordination, corruption and limited economic opportunities.

Frankly, this is an admission. As long as young people lack decent legal options, compounds will never run short of recruits. A raid can arrest people; it still has to answer why they went in the first place. That question is harder than cross-border asset tracing because it lands in national budgets, employment policy and local governance — no joint operation can settle it.

Yet naming a cause is easier than funding a cure. The statement sets out no money and no timetable for social protection, legal reform or job creation. Putting structural causes on the record is honesty. Leaving out who pays is evidence that the document remains a declaration.

## Who enforces it: a list without a budget or a clock

The East Asia Summit is a leaders' dialogue that works by consensus, and its documents are not binding. This statement lists a great many things governments should do — intelligence-sharing, joint investigations, extradition, asset recovery, platform cooperation — but supplies no budget, no monitoring body, no compliance review and no deadline.

Nikkei Asia reported that Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. urged participants to integrate law enforcement, financial, telecommunications, digital and judicial agencies. The direction is right, but for 19 jurisdictions this may be the hardest form of cooperation available: different evidentiary standards, incompatible cross-border data rules, an incomplete extradition network, and even disagreement over what counts as criminal proceeds. The longer a regional statement grows, the lighter its landing — a habit that should not be excused by the importance of the cause.

## China's role: the partner that cannot be bypassed

For China and for ethnic Chinese communities across Southeast Asia, this is not distant diplomatic news. China is one of the source countries for workers lured abroad and one of the most important markets targeted by scam operations; Chinese business owners, students and visitors in the region can be targets of precise, well-researched approaches as well.

Conversely, no effective cross-border mechanism can work without Chinese participation. The bank channels through which funds move, the routes by which suspects return, and the arrangements for reintegrating rescued victims all require practical cooperation between Chinese law-enforcement and financial regulators. China is among the signatories, which is necessary but not the same as connected. The test will be the first joint three-country investigation, the first cross-border account freeze, and the first group of returnees screened to a common standard.

One caution is in order. Diaspora communities, with dense language, kinship and commercial networks, are both attractive targets and the most credible channels for warning and prevention. Treating them as inherent suspects would be unfair and would deprive enforcement of its most useful ally.

## Why now: multilateralism at the lowest common denominator

Seen against the wider summit — the South China Sea code of conduct, Myanmar, energy security, each capable of splitting the room — online scams were one of the few issues nobody could oppose. Nikkei Asia reported that the United States sought to dilute wording on climate and diversity. Against that backdrop, the scam consensus is easy to explain.

It also explains why delegations queued up to attach their own projects: Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi proposed an EAS cybercrime conference at India's National Forensic Sciences University; South Korean President Lee Jae-myung announced a Korea-ASEAN anti-scam law-enforcement cooperation programme starting next year; Vietnam's prime minister proposed an EAS law-enforcement hotline network; Thailand promoted its own global anti-scam partnership. Consensus on the surface, separate calculations beneath it.

That does not make the statement worthless. The lowest common denominator is often where implementation begins: connect intelligence and asset-tracing channels in a low-sensitivity area first, then attempt harder ones. But it should not be mistaken for strategic convergence. Nineteen governments standing together against fraud does not mean they stand together on anything else.

## Conclusion: a statement is a start, not a finish

To be fair, putting online scams on the leaders' agenda of the East Asia Summit is a signal in itself: this is no longer one country's policing problem but a regional governance question. The checklist is more detailed than before, especially on asset recovery and victim protection.

But Southeast Asia does not lack statements. What it lacks is follow-through. The real examination lies ahead: whether the first joint investigation can be completed, whether the first stolen funds can be frozen and returned, whether the first rescued workers receive screening and support rather than a second victimisation. A year from now, if all that remains is the statement, it is one more file on a shelf. If there are cases and frozen accounts, it will have been an act of institution-building.

## Further reading

**East Asia Summit**: a leaders-level strategic dialogue established in 2005, bringing together the 11 ASEAN members and eight partners — Australia, China, India, Japan, New Zealand, South Korea, Russia and the United States.

**Scam centres**: compounds where criminal syndicates confine trafficked workers and force them to conduct online fraud. Reported in Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos, the Philippines and Malaysia; the Financial Action Task Force estimated about 120,000 forced workers in Myanmar alone.

**Recovery of proceeds of crime**: the tracing, freezing, seizing and return of fraud-derived funds, which strikes directly at the economic incentive. Cross-border execution depends on mutual legal assistance and extradition, making it the hardest item in the statement to deliver.
