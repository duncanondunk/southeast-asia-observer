# 《欢迎来龙餐馆》热映：当"海外中餐馆"站到了聚光灯下

文牧野的新片《欢迎来龙餐馆》上映六天，内地票房便突破 9 亿元（约1.25亿美元）。沈腾饰演的东北厨子徐福，为还债远赴中东战乱之地，在一家名为"龙餐馆"的中餐厅执掌后厨，用一锅锅热饭，为流离失所的战争遗孤撑起一片避难所。一部讲"海外中餐馆"的电影，何以在当下击中这么多观众？

答案或许不在电影本身，而在它挪动了我们熟悉的叙事坐标。过去三十年，中国流行文化里的华侨华人，几乎被锁死在两条线索里：要么是东南亚唐人街里"落叶归根"的乡愁，要么是欧美唐人街里刻苦谋生的打工者。而《欢迎来龙餐馆》把镜头对准了一群更少被讲述的人——散落在全球冲突地带、靠着一口灶台讨生活的华商。它提醒我们：华侨华人的故事，从来不止于东南亚。

## 一部电影，为什么让"海外中餐馆"站到了聚光灯下

中餐馆大概是中国人走向世界最古老的"名片"之一。从十九世纪淘金热里的杂碎馆，到今天每座世界名城都少不了的宫保鸡丁，这口锅飘到哪里，中国人的身影就跟到哪里。可也正因太熟悉，我们反而很少认真去想：当中餐馆开在炮火连天的异地，它还是一桩生意吗？还是一种别的东西？

《欢迎来龙餐馆》的聪明之处，正在于它把中餐馆从"背景板"拉成了"主角"。导演文牧野在采访里说得很直白：战争是残酷的，而美食代表一种幸福感；他想用战乱地区的一家中餐馆做叙事载体，用美味去抚慰人心。一句话，把宏大的反战主题，收束到"好好吃饭"这样最朴素的人间愿望上。据新华网报道，该片在中国电影资料馆的观众满意度调查中拿下高分，不少观众感慨"口水和泪水齐流"。

但我们也要说句直话：这部片子能火，靠的从来不是"中餐"这个噱头，而是它终于把一个被长期忽视的群体——战乱之地的华商——推到了前台。这比任何口号都更有力量。

## 原型：在巴格达绿区掌勺的深圳 IT 男

电影不是凭空编的。银幕之外，二十多年前的巴格达街头，确实有过一间中餐厅，在硝烟里见证人间百味。

据中国新闻网报道，刘磊和帅学军本是深圳一家 IT 公司的员工。2003 年 3 月伊拉克战争打响，刘磊当年 7 月便踏上伊拉克的土地，想"赚一笔钱"。他们最早在安德鲁斯公寓开起"中国龙"餐馆，本想接待往来战地记者，可持续的轰炸让客流惨淡。熬到 12 月，两人把店搬进巴格达绿区——那里曾是萨达姆的官邸与政府部门所在地，战后成了美军与临时政府驻地，相对安全，美军客源也稳定，生意才慢慢回暖。

现实比电影更克制，也更荒诞。影片里徐福坚持地道中式口味、对"左宗棠鸡"嗤之以鼻；现实中的刘磊却不得不妥协，主营酸甜鸡等改良美式中餐，去迎合美军士兵的胃。2004 年 4 月，帅学军也来到巴格达，此后一年里，两人多次经历人肉炸弹袭击，还零距离目睹美军车辆被炸毁。运营一年多后，因局势恶化，两人相继回国，把战时见闻编撰成书。

帅学军后来用一句"兴，百姓苦；亡，百姓苦"概括那段日子。这句话，比任何煽情都更精准——在风雨飘摇的伊拉克，不同阵营、不同信仰的人，在炮火里各有挣扎，不过是想尽力讨生活。据中新网引述，那位曾为美军服务的伊拉克少年雇员，最终考上了大学、走出了伊拉克，却始终惦念家乡。

## 数字：中餐出海，早就不是东南亚的专利

把视野拉远，会看到一个常被忽略的事实：中餐出海，从来不是东南亚的专利，今天更不是。

据行业估算，仅美国中餐业年营收即达数百亿美元量级（约数千亿元人民币）——这还不包括东南亚、欧洲与中东那张庞大的网络。从十九世纪的"淘金杂碎"到今日的米其林中餐，华人餐饮业是海外华侨华人最稳固的经济基座之一。东南亚固然拥有历史最悠久、根脉最深的唐人街，但新的"前沿"早已转向全球南方那些动荡与资源交织的地带：中东、非洲、拉美。刘磊们的巴格达，只是这条漫长链条上的一段。

这也解释了为什么"海外中餐馆"会成为一个值得认真打量的文化符号。它既是生意，也是一种软性的存在：无论战火还是封锁，灶台一开，热饭一端，陌生人之间最起码的体面就被短暂地接上了。开一家中餐馆，往往是一个华人家庭在异国站稳脚跟的第一步，也是离散群体与在地社会发生接触的第一个界面。

但我们要提醒一句：别把"中餐馆"浪漫化成什么文化输出的利器。对绝大多数华商而言，它首先是一门要交租金、要防抢劫、要在异国法规与族群关系里小心腾挪的苦生意。电影把苦难升华为温情，现实里的华商，更多是在夹缝里求生。

## 镜头里的"中东小城"，为什么拍在中国

一个有意思的细节是：影片里那座硝烟弥漫的"中东小城"，其实是在中国取景的。据中国侨网另一篇报道，这个"藏"在国内的中东小城，靠的是国内实景与美术置景的拼接。

这引出一个值得玩味的问题：当中国电影要去"想象"一个陌生的离散现场，为什么选择在本土搭建，而不是真的把摄影机架到那里？答案很现实——安全、成本、可控。但更深一层是观看的方式：我们习惯于用一套既有的镜头语言去"再现"远方，而不是真的去理解远方。巴格达的鸽群、绿区的美军、街角的乞讨者，在银幕上成了一批可调动的情绪符号。

这本身无可厚非，商业电影本就要做取舍。但如果华侨华人研究只满足于"中餐馆很温暖"这类意象，就会漏掉真正重要的事：那些真实活在战乱里、既要谋生又要面对身份与安全的华商，他们的处境远比一句"好好吃饭"复杂。镜头可以在国内搭，问题却不在国内。

## 观察者说：离散叙事的一次转向

《欢迎来龙餐馆》最值得肯定的，是它让华侨华人的离散叙事出现了一次微妙转向——从"回乡寻根"与"刻苦谋生"的两套老剧本里走出来，把一个嵌入全球冲突地带的华商故事，拍出了朴素的人性光泽。这比空喊"文化出海"要有诚意得多。

但我们也要保持清醒。其一，温情不能替代结构。电影把战乱里的华人塑造成"善良的庇护者"，这当然动人，却也容易让观众忽略一个事实：在异国的冲突现场，华侨华人同样可能是被怀疑、被勒索、被卷入的对象，而非总是施予者。其二，要避免把离散群体当成软实力的注脚。文牧野借角色之口说出"龙餐馆只有和平和美食"，传递的是中国人"以和为贵"的价值追求，这没问题；可一旦把复杂的在地政治简化成一碗烩饭的温情，叙事就会失重。

对"东南亚观察"这类以华侨华人为重要栏目的平台而言，这部电影也是一面镜子：我们长期聚焦于东南亚这片与中国血缘最深的离散热土，这没错；但当中国电影开始把镜头伸向中东、非洲的华商，我们的研究视野也不该停在唐人街。离散中国的版图，远比我们惯常书写的更大。把熟悉的东南亚，和这些不那么熟悉的远方放在同一张地图上看，反而能让我们更清楚地理解：所谓华侨华人，从来不是某一个地区的代名词。

## 延伸阅读

**华商（Huashang / Overseas Chinese Merchant）**：泛指在海外从事工商经营的华侨华人，是华侨华人经济网络的核心节点，从中餐、零售到贸易、基建，构成离散群体与在地社会接触的第一界面。

**离散叙事（Diaspora Narrative）**：关于移居群体如何讲述自身处境、记忆与归属的叙事框架；从"落叶归根"到"落地生根"再到全球冲突地带的谋生故事，折射出中国对海外侨民想象的代际变化。

**以和为贵（He harmony as the highest good）**：中华处世伦理的核心命题之一，强调以和平、和睦为价值优先；影片借中餐馆空间将其转化为"一起好好吃饭"的反战母题。

# When a Wartime Chinese Restaurant Took the Spotlight: Diaspora, Soft Power, and the Politics of "Just Eat Well"

Wen Muye's new film *Welcome to Dragon Restaurant* (*Huan Ying Lai Long Can Guan*) grossed more than 900 million yuan (roughly US$125 million) in its first six days of release. Shen Teng plays Xu Fu, a cook from China's Northeast who travels to a war-torn corner of the Middle East to pay off a debt, takes over the kitchen of a Chinese eatery called the Dragon Restaurant, and uses one hot meal after another to shelter orphaned children displaced by war. Why does a film about an overseas Chinese restaurant strike such a nerve right now?

The answer may lie less in the film itself than in the way it shifts a familiar coordinate. For three decades, popular Chinese culture has locked the overseas Chinese into two storylines: the nostalgic "returning to roots" migrant of Southeast Asia's Chinatowns, or the hardworking laborer of Western Chinatowns. *Welcome to Dragon Restaurant* turns instead to a far less told group — ethnic Chinese merchants scraping by on a stove in the world's conflict zones. It is a reminder that the overseas Chinese story was never only about Southeast Asia.

## Why a wartime Chinese restaurant stepped into the spotlight

The Chinese restaurant is perhaps one of the oldest "business cards" China has sent into the world. From the chop-suey joints of the nineteenth-century gold rushes to the kung pao chicken no world city can now do without, wherever the wok drifted, Chinese faces followed. Yet precisely because it is so familiar, we rarely ask the harder question: when a Chinese restaurant opens amid active artillery, is it still just a business — or something else?

The film's clever move is to pull the restaurant out of the backdrop and make it the protagonist. Wen Muye put it plainly in interviews: war is cruel, but food represents a kind of happiness; he wanted a wartime Chinese restaurant as the narrative vehicle, using flavor to soothe people. In one line, he folds a grand anti-war theme into the most ordinary human wish — "just eat well." Xinhua reported that the film scored highly in the China Film Archive audience-satisfaction survey, with many viewers noting they "cried as much as they salivated."

But let us be frank: the film's success never rested on the "Chinese food" gimmick. Its real power is that it finally pushes a long-ignored group — ethnic Chinese merchants in war zones — into the foreground. That speaks louder than any slogan.

## The prototype: Shenzhen IT men cooking in Baghdad's Green Zone

The film was not invented from thin air. Off-screen, more than two decades ago, a Chinese restaurant did stand on the streets of Baghdad, witnessing the full texture of life amid gunfire.

According to China News Service, Liu Lei and Shuai Xuejun were employees of an IT company in Shenzhen. When the Iraq War broke out in March 2003, Liu flew to Iraq that July hoping to "make some money." They first opened the "China Dragon" restaurant in the Andrews apartment complex, expecting to serve war correspondents, but sustained bombardments kept customers away. By December they relocated into Baghdad's Green Zone — once Saddam's palace and government seat, then the base of U.S. forces and the interim government — where relative safety and a steady American clientele let business recover.

Reality was more restrained, and more absurd, than the movie. On screen, Xu Fu insists on authentic Chinese flavors and scoffs at "General Tso's chicken"; the real Liu had to compromise, serving sweet-and-sour chicken and other Americanized dishes to suit U.S. soldiers' palates. Shuai joined in April 2004, and over the following year the two survived multiple suicide bombings and watched at close range as an American vehicle was blown up. After operating for just over a year, worsening conditions sent them home, where they compiled their wartime notes into a book.

Shuai later summed up those days with the classical line, "In triumph the people suffer; in ruin the people suffer." That single sentence lands harder than any sentimentality: in a shaken Iraq, people of every camp and creed struggled through the shelling simply to get by. As China News Service noted, the Iraqi teenager who served the Americans eventually won a university place, left Iraq, yet never stopped missing home.

## The numbers: Chinese food left Southeast Asia behind long ago

Step back, and an often-missed fact appears: sending Chinese food abroad was never Southeast Asia's monopoly, and certainly is not today.

Industry watchers estimate that the Chinese-food sector in the United States alone turns over hundreds of billions of dollars a year (several trillion yuan) — before counting the vast networks across Southeast Asia, Europe, and the Middle East. From the "gold-rush chop suey" of the 1800s to today's Michelin-starred Chinese kitchens, the restaurant trade is one of the most durable economic foundations of the overseas Chinese. Southeast Asia surely holds the oldest and deepest-rooted Chinatowns, but the new "frontier" has long shifted toward the Global South's zones where turmoil and resources intertwine — the Middle East, Africa, Latin America. Liu Lei's Baghdad is only one link in that long chain.

This is why the "overseas Chinese restaurant" deserves serious analytic attention. It is both commerce and a soft form of presence: whether under shelling or lockdown, the moment the stove lights and the hot meal is served, a minimal dignity between strangers is briefly restored. Opening a Chinese restaurant is usually a family's first foothold abroad, and the first interface where a diaspora meets local society.

But a caveat: do not romanticize the restaurant into some instrument of cultural export. For most merchants it is, first and foremost, a grim business of paying rent, dodging robbery, and navigating foreign law and ethnic relations. The film sublimates suffering into warmth; the real merchant survives in the cracks.

## Why a "Middle Eastern town" was filmed in China

An intriguing detail: the shell-shocked "Middle Eastern town" in the film was actually shot in China. As China Qiao Wang reported in a separate piece, this "Middle Eastern town hidden domestically" was assembled from domestic locations and set design.

That raises a worthwhile question: when Chinese cinema sets out to "imagine" an unfamiliar diaspora site, why build it at home rather than point the camera there? The practical answer — safety, cost, control — is obvious. But something deeper is at stake in the act of seeing: we are used to "representing" distant places through a ready-made visual grammar, rather than actually understanding them. Baghdad's pigeons, the Green Zone's troops, the beggar at the corner become movable emotional symbols on a reel.

None of this is blameworthy; commercial cinema must make trade-offs. But if overseas-Chinese studies content themselves with the image of "the warm Chinese restaurant," they will miss what truly matters: the real merchants who live in war zones, who must both make a living and confront questions of identity and security, face situations far more complex than "just eat well." The camera can stay home; the problems do not.

## The observer's take: a pivot in the diaspora narrative

What *Welcome to Dragon Restaurant* deserves credit for is a subtle pivot in the diaspora narrative — stepping away from the two old scripts of "returning to roots" and "struggling to survive," and photographing an ethnic Chinese merchant embedded in a global conflict zone with plain human luminosity. That is far more sincere than shouting about "cultural export."

Yet we should stay clear-eyed. First, warmth cannot substitute for structure. The film casts the wartime Chinese as "benevolent protectors" — moving, yes, but it risks letting viewers forget a fact: in a foreign conflict zone, overseas Chinese can just as easily be suspected, extorted, or swept up, not always the giver. Second, avoid reducing the diaspora to a footnote of soft power. Through his characters Wen delivers the line "the Dragon Restaurant has only peace and food," conveying the Chinese value of harmony as the highest good — perfectly legitimate; but once complex local politics is flattened into the warmth of one stew, the narrative loses its weight.

For a platform like *Southeast Asia Observer*, where the overseas Chinese are a central column, the film is also a mirror. We have long focused on Southeast Asia, the diaspora heartland bound to China by the deepest ties of blood — and rightly so. But as Chinese cinema begins pointing its lens at merchants in the Middle East and Africa, our research horizon should not stop at Chinatown either. The map of a diasporic China is far larger than we usually write. Placing the familiar Southeast Asia and these less-familiar peripheries on the same map actually sharpens our understanding: the overseas Chinese were never the surname of any single region.

## Further reading

**Huashang (Overseas Chinese Merchant):** A broad term for ethnic Chinese running businesses abroad; the core node of the overseas-Chinese economic network, from restaurants and retail to trade and infrastructure, forming the first interface between a diaspora and local society.

**Diaspora Narrative:** The framework through which migrant groups tell their own condition, memory, and belonging — from "falling leaves returning to roots" to "taking root abroad" to survival stories in global conflict zones, tracing a generational shift in how China imagines its emigrants.

**He, harmony as the highest good:** A core proposition of Chinese ethical life that prizes peace and concord; the film transforms it, via the restaurant space, into an anti-war motif of "sitting down to eat well together."
