# 尚泰考虑首聘外姓掌门：华商家族的护城河，为什么改成了董事会

据联合早报转引彭博社报道，泰国尚泰集团正为新一代领导层交接做准备，并首次认真考虑把帅印交给郑氏家族以外的人。集团首席执行官兼执行主席郑昌接受泰国媒体访问时说，急速变化的商业环境要求最有能力的人领航，必须由强大的董事会和良好治理托底。报道很克制：既无人选，也无时间表。但把这句话放进泰国华商家族的语境里，它比一次普通的高管调整更有分量——那层靠姓氏维系的接班默契，第一次被公开摆上可修改的台面。

这不是「家族企业走向现代化」的老套故事。尚泰已经有现代化外壳：上市平台、职业经理人、遍布泰国与欧洲的零售资产。真正被拿出来重新议价的，是控制权与经营权之间那条多年未动的边界。谁有资格坐上首席执行官的位子，从「姓什么」改成了「能不能被制度考核」——这才是值得盯住的变化。

## 一句表态，不是一次任命

先把事实与推论分开。郑昌没有宣布继任者，也没有给出交接时间表；他说的是「需要考虑」与「最终要选最能干、最合适的人选」。据联合早报转述，这番话出自对泰国媒体的访问，彭博社据此判断集团正在重新审视接班模式。严格说，这只是一个信号，不是一份任命公告。评论若直接写成「尚泰终结家族统治」，是把试探当结果。

但信号之所以有价值，恰在它来自掌门人本人。家族企业的禁忌往往不是「引入职业经理人」，而是「让外人坐上最高位」。前者是雇一批打工的，后者是把最终决策权交出去。郑昌把两类都纳入同一句治理表述里，等于承认：在必要时，姓氏可以让位于岗位要求。

## 七十九年，一个没有退休制的家族

郑心平1947年在曼谷创立尚泰，近80年来集团一直由郑氏家族成员领导。彭博社今年较早时把这个家族列入亚洲最富有家族之列。在泰国，家族财团长期是经济主干，业务越做越大、越做越跨境，领导权仍按血缘往下传；这并非泰国独有，而是东南亚华人商业的共同脚本。

这个脚本的效率在于低交易成本：信任靠亲缘，传承靠家规，重大决定不必向陌生人解释。但它的成本会随规模上升。当业务从一家曼谷商店变成横跨商场、酒店与欧洲百货的资产包，家族里能胜任的人未必恰好出生在正确的年份；即便有，也未必愿意接。把「必须由家里人」当成硬约束，等于把继承人数量当成人才池上限。郑昌这句话，本质上是承认这个上限已经不够用。

## 欧洲百货为什么先成为压力点

尚泰的国际化不是典故里的修辞。集团旗下有泰国最大的购物中心开发商，也在泰国与越南经营百货与量贩；海外一端则握有英国的知名百货名店，以及意大利、德国的奢侈品百货。据联合早报转引彭博社报道，郑昌直言国际地缘政治冲突正在打击消费者信心与支出，令欧洲业务短期承压，集团仍在观察疲弱会持续多久。

把这段背景与接班表态放在一起看，逻辑就清楚了：需要被修补的不是「家族荣誉」，而是一条正在变薄的利润线。欧洲奢侈品零售的高固定成本、租金与库存周期，对管理层的判断速度要求极高；掌门人若由血缘自动产生，董事会很难在业绩下滑时追问「为什么是这个人」。让一个可被聘、可被考核、也可被解聘的专业人士来扛，反而恢复了问责链条——虽然这并不保证他能救活生意，它只是让失败有人负责。

## 四年一选、66%门槛：家族规则早就不是嫡长制

更值得注意的是，尚泰的家族治理并非外界想象的「家长一人拍板」。据泰国媒体今年11月披露的家族治理安排，集团设有家族宪章，领导人每4年选举一次，候选人须获得至少66%的支持；家族成员多达数百人，至今没有一人卖出公司股份，尽管并无禁令。郑昌本人还说，董事会支持率在75%至90%时决策很少出错，若意见接近五五开，他宁可搁置。

这组数字把「外姓掌门」从文化爆炸新闻拉回制度连续性的轨道。一个每4年重新授权、设有明确门槛的体系，本来就已经把继承权改成了选任权；从「家族成员里选」走到「所有人里选」，是同一逻辑再往前一步。换句话说，尚泰不是突然西化，而是把自己已经写了几十年的规则，往前推到最后一个还没碰的岗位。

## 外姓掌门不是家族退场，而是权力换层

反对者最容易犯的错，是把引入外部首席执行官读成家族认输。看股权就明白了：数百名成员没有卖出任何股份，控制权仍牢牢留在家族手里；掌门人换姓，换的是经营权，不是所有权。郑昌长期兼任首席执行官与执行主席，本身就说明集团早就把「谁掌舵」与「谁定方向」拆成两个问题。

家族让出的，是日常经营这台机器；保留的，是董事会、政策方向与长期资本。这是一种再配置，不是撤退。对东南亚华商而言，这一点尤其重要：外界常用「家族企业能否现代化」来框架化问题，仿佛现代化必然以家族退出为代价。尚泰给出的答案更务实——家族继续当所有者与规则制定者，把管理交给可替换的专业人员。姓氏不再是任命条件，但仍可以是治理锚点。

## 董事会能不能管住一个非家族首席执行官

问题随之而来：谁来管那个被请来管公司的人？家族掌门出事，家族舆论能施压；外部掌门若失误，既没有宗亲网络兜底，也可能缺乏足够长的信任期。治理文本写的是选举与门槛，写不出董事会能否克制住每周过问采购、门店与人事的冲动。

这里有双重风险。其一，4年一选的固定任期可能诱导短期行为：欧洲百货的扭亏需要时间，而一位外来者最怕的，是交不出任期内的漂亮数字。其二，66%的门槛既是资格筛选，也可能成为派系否决工具——当家族成员数以百计，赞成票的分布比候选人的履历更能决定结果。若最终选出的仍是「大家都不得罪」的家族成员，那么这场讨论就只是姿态。真正的检验不是谁来，而是董事会有没有能力按同一套标准评估、续聘乃至解聘一个不姓郑的人。

## 华商叙事的转折点：信任网络怎样变成治理成本

华人家族企业在东南亚的崛起叙事，向来强调亲缘、乡谊与商会网络如何降低交易成本。这套解释在创业阶段成立：一张宗亲关系网可以替代合同、征信与法院。但同一张网在集团化之后会反向收费——数百名股东各有期待，人情与业绩难以分开，董事席位按辈分而非资质分配，外部资本无从判断决策是怎么做出来的。

尚泰的经验说明，维系家族团结的共同价值，恰恰是专业化的前提。因为没人卖股、没人闹分家，家族才有余裕把经营权外包；如果股权本身处在不稳定状态，外包就成了失控。那些把「华人家族企业」当成天然低治理成本的说法，忽略了这个条件：信任网络只有在能转化为成文规则时，才不会在规模面前变成负债。这不是文化优劣问题，而是组织阶段问题。

## 真正的考验：规则能否管住创始人之后的人

因此，别急着为「唯才是举」鼓掌，也别忙着为传统落幕哀悼。尚泰目前给出的，是一个治理选项，不是一次已完成的交接。后续值得盯住3件事：候选人池是否真的向外打开；那道66%的门槛是否会被用来选人，而不是用来卡人；以及第一位非家族掌门若在任内表现不佳，董事会是否敢按同一规则让他离开。

这三件事决定了这次表态的分量。如果答案是肯定的，郑氏家族留给下一代的遗产就不是商场与百货，而是一套能在家族之外运转的授权机制——那比任何一块资产都更难复制。如果答案是否定的，外姓掌门只会成为一个体面的挡箭牌：业绩好归功于家族选人眼光，业绩差归咎于外人不懂生意。东南亚华商是否真正跨过这道坎，看的从来不是姓氏，而是规则能不能在人不在场时继续说话。

## 延伸阅读

**家族治理（Family governance）**：家族就所有权、董事会构成与领导岗位选任所订的规则体系，用来把亲缘默契转为可执行的程序。

**所有权与经营权分离（Separation of ownership and control）**：家族保留股权与长期方向，把日常经营交给可考核、可替换的专业管理者。

**职业经理人（Professional manager）**：以专业履历与业绩指标受聘的管理者，其任职资格不来自血缘，而来自可核验的能力与考核。

# Central weighs a leader outside the family: why a Chinese-Thai conglomerate is rewriting the succession rule

Lianhe Zaobao, citing Bloomberg, reports that Central Group is preparing a new generation of leadership and, for the first time, seriously considering handing the helm to someone outside the Chirathivat family. Tos Chirathivat, chief executive and executive chairman, told a Thai outlet that a rapidly changing business environment demands the most capable person at the helm, backed by a strong board and good governance. The report is deliberately thin: no candidate, no timetable. Yet set against the history of Thai Chinese family conglomerates, the remark carries more weight than a routine executive reshuffle. The tacit rule that the surname decides the successor has, for the first time, been publicly placed on the table as something that can be amended.

This is not the familiar story of a family firm "going modern". Central already has the outer shell of modernity: listed vehicles, professional managers, retail assets across Thailand and Europe. What is being repriced is the boundary between control and management. The qualification for the chief executive's seat is shifting from who you are born as to what you can be measured against. That is the change worth watching.

## A remark, not an appointment

Keep the facts separate from the inference. Tos did not name a successor or offer a timeline; he said the group needs to consider the most capable and suitable person, supported by strong governance. According to Lianhe Zaobao's rendering of the Bloomberg report, the interview has prompted a reassessment of the succession model. Strictly speaking, this is a signal, not an announcement. Commentary that declares the end of family rule is treating a probe as a verdict.

Even so, the signal matters because it comes from the incumbent. In a family firm the taboo is rarely hiring professional managers; it is letting an outsider occupy the top seat. The first is hiring employees, the second is surrendering final decision-making authority. By folding both into one sentence about governance, Tos has conceded that, when necessary, the surname can yield to the requirements of the job.

## Seventy-nine years and a family with no retirement rule

Tiang Chirathivat founded Central in Bangkok in 1947, and the group has been led by family members for nearly eight decades. Bloomberg ranked the clan among Asia's richest earlier this year. In Thailand, family conglomerates have long been the backbone of the economy: businesses expand internationally while leadership still passes down the bloodline. This is not a Thai peculiarity so much as a shared script among Southeast Asian Chinese businesses.

That script is efficient because it lowers transaction costs: trust comes from kinship, succession from family rules, and major decisions need no explanation to strangers. Its cost rises with scale. Once a Bangkok shop becomes a portfolio of malls, hotels and European department stores, the most capable family member may not have been born in the right year — or may not want the job. Treating "must be a family member" as a hard constraint caps the talent pool at the number of heirs. Tos's remark is an acknowledgement that the cap is no longer enough.

## Why European department stores became the pressure point

Central's internationalisation is not decorative. Its listed arms include Thailand's largest shopping-mall developer and a retail business operating department stores and hypermarkets in Thailand and Vietnam; abroad it holds well-known luxury department stores in Britain, Italy and Germany. Citing Bloomberg, Lianhe Zaobao reports Tos saying that geopolitical conflicts are weighing on consumer sentiment and spending, leaving the European business with near-term challenges, and that the group is watching how long the weakness lasts.

Put that next to the succession remarks and the logic becomes clear. What needs repair is not family honour but a thinning margin. European luxury retail carries high fixed costs, rent and inventory cycles, and demands fast judgement from management. When the top job arrives by birth, a board has little basis for asking why that particular person is the right one. Bringing in a manager who can be hired, assessed and dismissed restores the chain of accountability. It does not guarantee a turnaround; it merely gives failure someone to answer for.

## Four-year terms and a 66% threshold: this was never primogeniture

More striking is that Central's family governance is not the autocracy outsiders imagine. Thai media reported in November that the group has a family constitution under which leaders are elected every four years and need at least 66% support. The family now numbers hundreds, and not a single member has sold a share, despite there being no rule against it. Tos has also said that board decisions with 75–90% support rarely went wrong, while a close split of 60:40 or 51:49 was a reason to drop the issue.

Those numbers pull the "outsider CEO" story out of the culture-war folder and back into institutional continuity. A system that reauthorises its leader every four years and sets an explicit threshold has already converted inheritance into appointment. Moving from "choose among family members" to "choose among everyone" is one further step along the same path. Central is not suddenly Westernising; it is pushing rules it has written for decades to the one post they have not yet touched.

## An outsider CEO is not a family exit

The easiest mistake is to read an external chief executive as a family surrender. Look at the equity: hundreds of members have sold no shares, and control remains with the family. A change of surname at the top changes management, not ownership. Tos's long-held dual role as chief executive and executive chairman shows that the group has already separated "who runs the company" from "who sets the direction".

What the family gives up is the daily machine; what it keeps is the board, policy and long-term capital. That is a reallocation, not a retreat. For overseas Chinese businesses in Southeast Asia this matters: the usual framing asks whether a family firm can modernise, as if modernisation required the family to leave. Central's answer is more pragmatic. The family stays as owner and rule-maker and hands management to replaceable professionals. The surname stops being a condition of appointment while remaining an anchor of governance.

## Can the board govern a non-family CEO?

The question then becomes: who governs the person hired to govern? When a family chief fails, family opinion exerts pressure. When an outsider fails, there is no clan network to catch him, and perhaps not enough time to earn trust. Governance documents can specify elections and thresholds; they cannot specify whether a board will resist the urge to meddle in procurement, stores and appointments every week.

Two risks follow. First, a fixed four-year term can encourage short-term behaviour: turning around European department stores takes time, while an outsider's greatest fear is a lack of impressive numbers before the next vote. Second, the 66% threshold can work as a merit filter or as a factional veto. With hundreds of family members, the distribution of yes votes may matter more than a candidate's record. If the eventual choice is still the least offensive family member, the exercise was posture. The real test is not who arrives but whether the board can assess, renew or dismiss a non-Chirathivat chief by the same standard.

## The turn in the overseas Chinese business story: when trust networks become governance costs

The rise of Chinese family firms in Southeast Asia has usually been explained through kinship, native-place ties and chamber-of-commerce networks that lower transaction costs. That holds at the founding stage: a clan network can substitute for contracts, credit checks and courts. The same network starts charging fees at group scale — hundreds of shareholders with competing expectations, sentiment hard to separate from performance, board seats allocated by seniority rather than competence, and outside investors unable to see how decisions are made.

Central's case suggests that the shared values holding the family together are precisely what make professionalisation possible. Because no one sold shares and no one split the group, the family had room to outsource management; if equity itself were unstable, outsourcing would mean losing control. The familiar claim that Chinese family firms enjoy inherently low governance costs misses this condition: a trust network only avoids becoming a liability at scale when it can be translated into written rules. This is not a question of cultural virtue but of organisational stage.

## The real test: can rules speak after the founder stops talking?

So do not applaud meritocracy yet, and do not mourn tradition yet. Central has offered a governance option, not a completed handover. Three things deserve attention: whether the candidate pool genuinely opens outward; whether the 66% threshold is used to choose candidates rather than block them; and, if the first non-family chief underperforms, whether the board dares to remove him by the same rule.

Those answers determine the weight of Tos's remark. If they are affirmative, the Chirathivat legacy will be not malls and department stores but an authorisation mechanism that works beyond the family — harder to replicate than any single asset. If they are negative, the outsider becomes a respectable shield: good results prove the family's judgement in picking him, bad results prove he did not understand the business. Whether Southeast Asian Chinese capital has really crossed the threshold has never depended on the surname. It depends on whether the rules can still speak when the founder is no longer in the room.

## Further reading

**Family governance**: the rule system a family sets for ownership, board composition and the selection of leaders, turning kinship understandings into enforceable procedures.

**Separation of ownership and control**: an arrangement in which the family keeps equity and long-term direction while day-to-day management goes to assessable, replaceable professionals.

**Professional manager**: an executive hired on the basis of a verifiable record and performance targets rather than a family connection.
