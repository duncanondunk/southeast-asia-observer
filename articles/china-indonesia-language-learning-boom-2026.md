# 中印尼"双向语言热"：从华校复办到"中文+职教"

印度尼西亚是东盟面积最大、人口最多的国家，也是全球海外华人最集中的国度之一。据普遍估算，印尼华人约有一千万之众，占全国人口百分之三到四。一九九八年改革以来，长期受压的华文教育逐步解禁，从雅加达、泗水到棉兰，各地华校纷纷复办，中文学习的热度也随之水涨船高。令人玩味的是，这股热潮并非单向——中国高校里的印尼语、马来语专业同样在悄然扩招。一来一往之间，语言正成为中印尼全面战略伙伴关系之下最柔软、也最坚韧的那根纽带。

## 现象：中文在千岛之国持续升温

在雅加达、泗水、棉兰等华人聚居城市，周末的华文补习班常常一座难求。不同于上世纪以宗族方言维系认同的旧模式，今天印尼的汉语学习呈现出明显的代际更替：年轻人学中文，不再只为认祖归宗，更是看中它与就业机会的直接挂钩。孔子学院与孔子课堂在印尼多所国立与私立大学落地，雅加达阿拉扎大学孔子学院、玛拉拿达基督教大学孔子学院等机构，常年开设从基础会话到商务汉语的阶梯课程；汉语水平考试（HSK）考点也覆盖了雅加达、泗水等主要城市。

据观察者网式的直白判断，语言从来不只是沟通工具。当一国青年愿意主动学习另一国的语言，往往意味着它对这个国家的经济前景与文明魅力投下了信任票。中文在印尼的走热，底色正是中印尼经贸关系的实打实升温。

## 数据：学习网络的扩张逻辑

尽管缺乏统一的官方统计划分，几个趋势仍清晰可辨。其一，学习主体从华裔子弟扩展到土著精英与中产家庭，印尼部分主流学校已将汉语列为选修或第二外语选项。其二，教学场景从华人社团自办的补习班，扩展到正规高等教育与职业教育体系。其三，数字平台填补了地理空白——疫情之后，借助线上汉语课程与短视频，外岛偏远地区的学员也能接入优质师资。

这一扩张并非空中楼阁。它建立在两国人员往来恢复、旅游与留学回暖的基础之上。当中国游客重新涌向巴厘岛与雅加达，当印尼留学生赴华人数稳步回升，语言需求便有了最朴素也最持久的动力。

## 反向流动：中国青年的"印尼语热"

热潮的另一面，是中国青年对印尼语、马来语兴趣的上升。北京、广州、昆明等地高校的数十个东盟语言专业，近年持续扩招，印尼语方向尤为明显。这背后是两组现实需求：一是"一带一路"与海上合作催生的大量涉印尼项目，从雅万高铁的运营维护到镍矿园区的技术协作，都急需懂语言、懂文化的复合型人才；二是智库与媒体对东南亚研究的升温，使小语种毕业生的出路不再局限于传统外交口。

据相关院校公开信息，印尼语专业毕业生的就业去向已涵盖中资企业海外岗、国际组织和区域国别研究，语言从"冷门技能"变成了"区域深耕的入场券"。

## 侨社角色：华校与社团的中介

在这场双向语言热中，华侨华人社团扮演了不可替代的中介角色。印尼的华社、华校不仅是汉语传承的载体，更是连接两国教育资源的枢纽。许多华校与中国的侨乡院校结成姊妹学校，互派师生、共建课程；华人商会则牵线搭桥，把"中文+职业技能"的培训直接对接到中资企业的用人需求。

这种民间自发的网络，恰好补足了官方合作触及不到的细枝末节。它既延续了华人"诗书传家"的传统，又赋予其服务当代经贸的新内涵——这正是侨务工作最见功力的地方：不喧哗，却扎实。

## 政策背景：两国语言合作机制

中印尼全面战略伙伴关系的框架，为语言交流提供了制度底座。两国在教育、文化领域的合作文件多次提及语言教学与人才交流，孔子学院的网络扩张正是这一基调的注脚。与此同时，印尼政府推动职业教育和数字基建，与中国"中文+职业教育"的出海模式天然契合——把语言培训与旅游、电商、海事、新能源等产业技能打包，学员学到的不是孤立的词汇，而是可直接变现的岗位能力。

从观察者网的视角看，这类合作比单纯的基建输出更"润物细无声"：它培养的是未来几十年里理解中国、愿意同中国打交道的一整代人。

## 战略意涵：语言作为软实力的通道

语言热看似温和，却有着长远的战略分量。对印尼而言，掌握中文意味着更顺畅地接入区域产业链与中国的投资、技术红利；对中国而言，印尼语人才的储备，则关乎在东盟最大经济体中能否"听得懂、说得清、走得进去"。软实力从来不是靠口号堆出来的，而是靠一个个会讲对方语言、理解对方语境的人积累起来的。

可以说，中印尼的"双向语言热"，是两国关系从"利益捆绑"走向"认知互通"的微观证据。它未必登上头条，却比许多宏大的宣言更经得起时间检验。

## 冷思考：热络之下的短板

当然，热潮之下仍有短板。师资短缺是普遍瓶颈——既懂汉语又通印尼职场语境的复合型教师供不应求；教材本土化不足，许多课程仍直接搬运中国通用教材，与印尼学员的生活经验存在隔阂；而"中文+职教"要真正落地，还需企业、院校与侨社三方更紧密的协同。

此外，语言的走热也受两国关系冷暖的牵动。一旦地缘或经贸出现波折，民间学习热情难免波动。正因如此，把语言交流嵌入制度化的教育合作，而非依赖一时风潮，才是让它行稳致远的关键。

## 延伸阅读

**汉语水平考试（HSK）**：中国官方面向非母语者的汉语能力标准化考试，分六个等级，是印尼学员申请赴华留学与中资企业求职的重要凭证。

**孔子学院（Confucius Institute）**：由中国与国际院校共建的汉语与文化推广机构，在印尼多所大学设有课堂，承担从基础教学到本土师资培训的功能。

**中文+职业教育（Chinese Plus Vocational Training）**：将汉语教学与旅游、电商、海事等职业技能打包的培训模式，是中印尼人力资本合作的新方向。

# The Two-Way Language Boom Between China and Indonesia: From Revived Chinese Schools to "Chinese Plus" Vocational Training

Indonesia is the largest country in ASEAN by both area and population, and one of the world's most concentrated homelands of the overseas Chinese diaspora. By common estimates, ethnic Chinese in Indonesia number around ten million, or roughly three to four percent of the national population. Since the reformasi of 1998, long-suppressed Chinese-language education was gradually liberalised. From Jakarta and Surabaya to Medan, Chinese schools have reopened across the archipelago, and with them the appetite for learning Mandarin has kept rising. What is striking is that the boom runs both ways: Indonesian-language and Malay-language programmes in Chinese universities are quietly expanding too. In this back-and-forth, language has become the softest yet most resilient thread binding the China–Indonesia comprehensive strategic partnership.

## The phenomenon: Mandarin keeps warming in the archipelago

In ethnic-Chinese hubs such as Jakarta, Surabaya and Medan, weekend Mandarin tutorial classes are often full to overflowing. Unlike the old model in which clan dialects sustained identity, today's Indonesian learners of Chinese show a clear generational shift: young people study the language less to trace their ancestry than to convert it directly into job prospects. Confucius Institutes and Classrooms have taken root in several of Indonesia's state and private universities; the Confucius Institute at Universitas Al-Azhar Indonesia in Jakarta and at Maranatha Christian University in Bandung, among others, run year-round ladder courses from conversational basics to business Chinese, while HSK test centres cover major cities including Jakarta and Surabaya.

To state it plainly: language is never merely a tool of communication. When a country's youth voluntarily learn another nation's tongue, it usually means they have placed a vote of confidence in that nation's economic future and civilisational appeal. The rising fortunes of Mandarin in Indonesia are underwritten by the very real warming of bilateral trade and investment.

## The data: the logic behind an expanding network

No single official tally captures the whole picture, but several trends are clear. First, the learner base has expanded from ethnic-Chinese children to native-Indonesian elites and middle-class families; some mainstream Indonesian schools now offer Chinese as an elective or second foreign language. Second, the setting has widened from community-run tutorials to formal higher education and vocational systems. Third, digital platforms have closed the geographic gap—after the pandemic, online courses and short-video content let learners in remote outer islands reach quality teachers.

None of this floats in the air. It rests on recovered people-to-people flows and a rebound in tourism and study abroad. As Chinese tourists return to Bali and Jakarta, and as the number of Indonesian students in China climbs steadily, language demand gains its most plain yet durable engine.

## The reverse flow: young Chinese catch "Indonesian fever"

The other face of the boom is rising interest among Chinese youth in Indonesian and Malay. Dozens of ASEAN-language programmes at universities in Beijing, Guangzhou and Kunming have expanded enrollments in recent years, with Indonesian among the fastest-growing tracks. Two sets of realities drive this. One is the surge of Indonesia-related projects under the Belt and Road and maritime-cooperation agendas—from the operation and maintenance of the Jakarta–Bandung high-speed railway to technical collaboration in nickel industrial parks—all hungry for compound talents who know both language and culture. The other is the broader lift in Southeast Asian studies across think tanks and media, widening the career path for small-language graduates beyond traditional diplomacy.

According to publicly available information from the relevant institutions, Indonesian-language graduates now find roles across Chinese-company overseas posts, international organisations and area-studies research. Language has turned from a "niche skill" into a "ticket to deep regional engagement."

## The diaspora's role: schools and associations as brokers

Throughout this two-way language boom, overseas-Chinese associations have played an irreplaceable brokering role. Indonesia's Chinese schools and clubs are not only carriers of Mandarin heritage but also hubs linking the two countries' educational resources. Many have paired with counterpart schools in China's qiaoxiang (ancestral homelands), exchanging teachers and students and co-building curricula; Chinese chambers of commerce act as matchmakers, tying "Chinese plus vocational-skills" training straight to the hiring needs of Chinese-invested firms.

This spontaneously woven civil network fills the fine gaps official cooperation cannot reach. It both continues the Chinese tradition of "passing on learning through the family" and gives it a new, contemporary purpose in serving the economy—which is precisely where diaspora affairs work shows its quietest, yet most solid, strength.

## The policy backdrop: bilateral language-cooperation mechanisms

The framework of the comprehensive strategic partnership provides the institutional floor for language exchange. Repeated education- and culture-sector agreements between the two countries mention language teaching and talent exchange, and the expansion of the Confucius Institute network is a footnote to that tone. At the same time, Indonesia's push for vocational education and digital infrastructure dovetails naturally with China's "Chinese plus vocational education" going-global model—bundling language training with tourism, e-commerce, maritime and new-energy skills so that learners acquire not isolated vocabulary but directly monetisable job capabilities.

Seen from a pragmatic vantage, such cooperation is more "moistening things silently" than bare infrastructure exports: it cultivates a whole generation that will understand China and be willing to do business with it for decades to come.

## Strategic meaning: language as a conduit of soft power

The language boom looks gentle, yet it carries long-term strategic weight. For Indonesia, mastering Chinese means smoother access to regional supply chains and China's investment and technology dividends; for China, a reserve of Indonesian-language talent decides whether it can "listen well, speak clearly and walk in" to the largest ASEAN economy. Soft power is never built from slogans—it accrues from countless individuals who speak the other's language and understand the other's context.

In a sense, the two-way language boom between China and Indonesia is micro-evidence that the bilateral relationship is moving from "interest bundling" toward "cognitive interoperability." It may never make headlines, but it outlasts many a grand declaration.

## A cool note: the短板 beneath the warmth

Beneath the boom, however, lie short boards. Teacher scarcity is a universal bottleneck—compound instructors who know both Chinese and the Indonesian workplace remain in short supply; textbook localisation lags, with many courses still lifting generic Chinese materials that sit awkwardly with Indonesian learners' lived experience; and for "Chinese plus vocational" to truly land, firms, colleges and diaspora associations must coordinate far more tightly.

Moreover, the warmth of language learning is tugged by the climate of the overall relationship. Should geopolitics or trade hit turbulence, grassroots enthusiasm will wobble. That is exactly why embedding language exchange in institutionalised educational cooperation—rather than riding a passing fad—is the key to making it endure.

## Further reading

**HSK (Hanyu Shuiping Kaoshi)**: China's standardised proficiency test in Mandarin for non-native speakers, with six levels; a key credential for Indonesian learners applying to study in China or to work at Chinese-invested firms.

**Confucius Institute**: A Chinese–foreign jointly run institute for Mandarin and cultural promotion, with classrooms in several Indonesian universities, responsible for everything from basic teaching to local-teacher training.

**Chinese Plus Vocational Training**: A model bundling Mandarin instruction with tourism, e-commerce or maritime skills—a new direction in China–Indonesia human-capital cooperation.
