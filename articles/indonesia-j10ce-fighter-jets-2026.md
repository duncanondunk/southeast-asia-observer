# “等着就行”：印尼的42架歼-10CE，与东南亚天空的第一次

2026年10月5日，雅加达。印尼空军参谋长穆罕默德·托尼·哈乔诺被记者问及首批歼-10CE何时抵达时，微笑着回答了一句：“等着就行。”

据印尼《共和报》援引消息人士报道，这批绰号“猛龙”的中国战斗机预计在6个月内——也就是2027年4月左右——飞抵印尼。若成行，这将是中国高端战机首次进入东南亚。

## 印尼买了什么

印尼采购的是歼-10CE，即歼-10C的外贸型号，属于四代半多用途战斗机，由中国航空工业集团成都飞机工业集团研制。计划采购42架，核准预算约90亿美元（约648亿元人民币）。

从公开参数看，该型机装备有源相控阵雷达，可挂载射程约150公里的霹雳-15E远程空空导弹，具备超视距多目标攻击能力。对一支长期缺乏远程空中拦截手段的空军来说，这是实打实的补强。

## 打动印尼的两个理由

第一个理由是实战验证。

在2025年的印巴空中冲突中，巴基斯坦空军的歼-10CE被指取得了对印度“阵风”战机的空战战果，打破了外界对中国高端战机“缺少实战检验”的质疑。对一个正在做采购决策的空军来说，战场记录比宣传册更有说服力。

第二个理由是价格。歼-10CE的单价仅为“阵风”的三分之一到四分之一。对军费有限、又需要快速形成规模的国家而言，这个差距足以改变决策。

## 这不是“倒向中国”

把印尼的选择简单理解为“倒向中国”，会误读它的逻辑。

印尼已经订购42架法国“阵风”、48架土耳其“可汗”，还在与韩国合作研发KF-21。在这一串订单里加入歼-10CE，更像是大国平衡策略的升级：一方面补齐远程超视距空战的短板，另一方面避免主力战机全系来自西方、战时被“卡脖子”。

中国军售不附带政治捆绑、交付周期更短，对急需现代化的国家具有实际吸引力。这是市场逻辑，也是地缘逻辑。

## 战略含义：一次“零的突破”

可以确定的是，歼-10CE进入印尼，意味着中国高端战机在东南亚实现了零的突破。

过去，东南亚的先进战机市场长期由美、俄、欧三方分享。中国装备多集中在教练机、轻型战机与无人机领域。歼-10CE若落地，将第一次把中国产品放进该地区主力战斗机的比较清单里。

对澳大利亚、新加坡等地区空中力量而言，印尼空军获得能够对四代半机构成实质威胁的装备，可能迫使邻国重新评估自身的空中优势——以及是否需要加快自己的换装节奏。

## 三处变数

不过，这笔交易仍有变数。

其一，印尼国防部至今既未正式确认，也未否认采购细节，首批交付的具体配置和数量尚未公开。其二，此前有报道称印尼可能采购的是二手歼-10B而非全新CE型，两者在雷达与武器系统上差距明显。其三，有分析人士质疑采购的紧迫性，指出印尼已积压大量战机订单，后勤与飞行员培养体系能否同步跟上仍是问号。

还有一个不能忽略的交叉点：中国在纳土纳群岛海域问题上与印尼存在主权主张重叠。军购合作的深化，会把这层关系放进一个更复杂的框架中。

## 邻国怎么想

军购从来不只是买装备，它同时是一次外交表态。

如果印尼最终同时运营“阵风”、歼-10CE并推进KF-21，它将成为区域内少数同时与欧美、中国、韩国航空工业保持深度合作的国家。这种“多头下注”能带来议价空间，也会带来后勤与体系整合的难题——不同来源的战机意味着不同的数据链、弹药谱系和维护标准。

## 印尼空军的换装清单

把歼-10CE放进印尼的装备计划里看，它的位置会更清楚。

印尼空军现役主力包括美制F-16与俄制苏-27、苏-30，机龄普遍偏大，妥善率长期承压。为此，雅加达近年连续签署大单：42架“阵风”、48架“可汗”，并参与韩国KF-21项目。加上拟采购的42架歼-10CE，印尼正在用十多年时间完成一次跨度极大的机队更替。

多来源装备的好处是议价空间与供应安全，代价是后勤体系被切成几块。对一支需要在广阔群岛之间维持巡逻的空军来说，这本身就是一个不小的难题。

## 结语：军购作为语言

回到那句“等着就行”。它既是对记者的回应，也是一种姿态：不确认、不否认，让外界去猜。

在一个大国竞争加剧的区域里，这种模糊本身就是筹码。42架歼-10CE是否会如期掠过雅加达的天空，2027年4月会给出答案。但无论结果如何，印尼已经用一份采购清单，说完了它想说的话。

## 延伸阅读

**歼-10CE（J-10CE）**：中国成都飞机工业集团研制的歼-10C外贸型号，四代半多用途战斗机，配备有源相控阵雷达，可挂载霹雳-15E远程空空导弹。

**阵风（Rafale）**：法国达索公司研制的双发多用途战斗机，属四代半机型，是印尼已订购的主力战机之一。

**大国平衡（hedging）**：中小国家同时与多个大国发展安全与经济合作，以避免过度依赖单一方、保留战略回旋空间的策略。

# "Just Wait": Indonesia's 42 J-10CEs and a First for Southeast Asian Skies

Jakarta, October 5, 2026. Asked by reporters when the first J-10CEs would arrive, Indonesian Air Force chief of staff Muhammad Tony Harjono smiled and said: "Just wait."

Citing sources, Indonesia's Republika newspaper reported that the Chinese fighters — nicknamed the "Vigorous Dragon" — are expected to reach Indonesia within six months, around April 2027. If it happens, it would be the first time Chinese high-end fighters have entered Southeast Asia.

## What Indonesia is buying

Indonesia is purchasing the J-10CE, the export variant of the J-10C, a 4.5-generation multirole fighter developed by the Chengdu Aircraft Industry Group under the Aviation Industry Corporation of China. The plan covers 42 aircraft with an approved budget of about US$9 billion.

By open specifications, the type carries an active electronically scanned array radar and can mount PL-15E long-range air-to-air missiles with a range of about 150 kilometres, giving it beyond-visual-range, multi-target engagement capability. For an air force long lacking long-range interception, that is a concrete upgrade.

## Two reasons Indonesia was persuaded

The first is combat validation.

In the 2025 India-Pakistan air clash, Pakistan Air Force J-10CEs were credited with an air-combat result against India's Rafales, denting the argument that Chinese high-end fighters lacked combat testing. For an air force weighing a procurement decision, a battlefield record carries more weight than a brochure.

The second is price. The J-10CE's unit cost is one-third to one-quarter that of the Rafale. For a country with a limited defence budget that nonetheless needs to build scale quickly, the gap is decision-changing.

## This is not "tilting to China"

Reading Indonesia's choice simply as a tilt toward China misreads its logic.

Indonesia has already ordered 42 French Rafales and 48 Turkish Kaan fighters, and is co-developing the KF-21 with South Korea. Adding the J-10CE to that list looks more like an upgraded hedging strategy: filling the long-range beyond-visual-range gap while avoiding a fleet sourced entirely from the West that could be "choked" in a crisis.

Chinese arms sales come with no political strings and shorter delivery timelines — a real attraction for countries in a hurry to modernise. That is market logic as much as geopolitics.

## Strategic meaning: a first

What is certain is that the J-10CE's entry into Indonesia would mark China's first breakthrough in high-end fighters in Southeast Asia.

The region's advanced-fighter market has long been shared by the United States, Russia and Europe, with Chinese equipment concentrated in trainers, light fighters and drones. If the J-10CE lands, it would put a Chinese product on the region's main combat-aircraft comparison list for the first time.

For regional air forces such as Australia's and Singapore's, an Indonesian fleet able to pose a real threat to 4.5-generation aircraft could force neighbours to reassess their own air advantage — and whether to accelerate their own recapitalisation.

## Three variables

The deal is not settled, however.

First, Indonesia's defence ministry has neither confirmed nor denied the procurement details, and the configuration and numbers of the initial delivery remain undisclosed. Second, earlier reports suggested Indonesia might be buying second-hand J-10Bs rather than new CE models, a meaningful difference in radar and weapons systems. Third, some analysts question the urgency, noting Indonesia has a large backlog of fighter orders and that logistics and pilot training may not keep pace.

There is also an unavoidable intersection: China and Indonesia have overlapping sovereignty claims in the waters around the Natuna Islands. Deeper arms cooperation would place that relationship in a more complicated frame.

## What the neighbours think

Arms purchases are never only about equipment; they are also a diplomatic statement.

If Indonesia ultimately operates Rafales, J-10CEs and moves ahead with the KF-21, it will be one of the few countries in the region to maintain deep aviation cooperation with Europe, the United States, China and South Korea at once. Such multi-sided betting buys bargaining room, but it also brings logistics and systems-integration headaches — aircraft from different sources mean different data links, ammunition families and maintenance standards.

## Indonesia's air force shopping list

Placed within Indonesia's wider equipment plans, the J-10CE's position becomes clearer.

The Indonesian Air Force's current mainstays include US-made F-16s and Russian Su-27s and Su-30s, generally ageing with long-standing readiness pressures. To address this, Jakarta has signed a string of major orders in recent years: 42 Rafales, 48 Kaans, and participation in South Korea's KF-21 programme. Add the intended 42 J-10CEs, and Indonesia is undertaking an unusually wide fleet replacement over more than a decade.

Multi-source equipment buys bargaining room and supply security, at the cost of a logistics system split into several parts. For an air force that must patrol across a vast archipelago, that is a considerable problem in itself.

## Conclusion: procurement as language

Back to "just wait." It was a reply to reporters, and also a posture: neither confirm nor deny, and let others guess.

In a region of intensifying great-power rivalry, that ambiguity is itself a bargaining chip. Whether 42 J-10CEs will sweep over Jakarta's skies on schedule is a question April 2027 will answer. Either way, Indonesia has already said what it wanted to say — with a procurement list.

## Further reading

**J-10CE**: The export variant of China's J-10C, developed by the Chengdu Aircraft Industry Group. A 4.5-generation multirole fighter with an active electronically scanned array radar and the ability to carry PL-15E long-range air-to-air missiles.

**Rafale**: A twin-engine multirole fighter built by France's Dassault Aviation. A 4.5-generation type, it is one of the mainstay aircraft Indonesia has ordered.

**Hedging**: A strategy in which smaller and middle powers develop security and economic cooperation with several great powers at once, to avoid over-dependence on any one and preserve strategic room to manoeuvre.
