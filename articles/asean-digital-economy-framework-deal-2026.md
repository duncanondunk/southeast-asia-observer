# 互认的重量：东盟数字经济框架协议谈判里的不对等

东盟数字经济框架协议的谈判，常被放进“区域一体化又进一步”的叙事里：统一电商规则、打通跨境数据、让支付与发票互相承认，听起来是一张越织越密的网。但真正决定这张网能否承重的，不是条款写得多齐整，而是各国监管能力是否处在同一水平线上。谈判越往细处走，这个前提越显得脆弱。

数字规则的特别之处在于，文本上的“互认”需要现实中的等价作支撑。一国监管机构能否核验对方出具的合规证明、能否追查跨境数据流、能否在本地执行处罚，决定了它愿不愿意把准入交给别人代管。当能力差距明显时，互认往往不是双向的便利，而是一方开放市场、一方承担举证的不对称安排。这是理解谈判为何慢、也为何谨慎的起点。

## 一份框架协议的雄心：把分散的规则缝成一张网

东盟数字经济框架协议的定位，不是再签一份单纯的贸易便利化文件，而是为电子商务、数据治理、支付、电子签名与消费者保护等议题搭一套共同规则。区域内各国数字市场规模、语言、法律传统与监管机构的设置差别很大，同一种业务在不同口岸要重复满足不同的披露、许可与存证要求，成本最终落在企业头上。框架协议想做的，是把这些分散的门槛折算成彼此可对接的标准。

这类整合的价值不必夸大，也不必怀疑：规则可对接，跨境交易才可能从“逐国逐案”变成“一套材料走多国”。但网络越密，对节点的要求越高。一张互认的网，默认每个节点都有能力识别、记录并追责违规行为；只要有一个节点做不到，风险就会沿着最薄弱的环节回流到整个网络。框架协议的雄心因此天然带着一个未言明的前提——各国的监管能力大致齐平，而这在东盟恰恰不成立。

## 跨境数据流动与本地化存储：最难对齐的那道缝

跨境数据流动是谈判里最难对齐的一道缝。一边是数字业务的基本诉求：订单、物流、支付与客服数据需要在集团与平台之间自由流转，割裂存储会直接抬高延迟与成本；另一边是各国对数据本地化存储的坚持，理由涉及执法取证、隐私保护、税务稽查与所谓数据主权。两种诉求都不是借口，却指向相反的基础设施安排。

难点不在原则表述，而在例外怎么写。“允许跨境流动、但为公共政策目标保留限制”几乎是各方都能接受的措辞，可一旦落到“何种数据、何种情形、由谁认定、争议如何救济”，分歧立刻显形。更现实的问题是验证：即便一方承诺数据只在合规环境里流转，另一方若缺乏审计跨境数据流、读取日志、追溯二次转移的技术与法律手段，就无从判断承诺是否被兑现。于是允许流动与否，往往不取决于文本允不允许，而取决于有没有能力查得动。

## 电子支付与电子发票互认：从接口到信任的落差

电子支付与电子发票的互认，看起来是最技术、也最容易先出成果的一块：报文格式、字段标准、证书体系、身份识别，都可以由工作组逐项对齐。区域内近年确实在支付互联、二维码标准与电子单据上积累了不少可对接的经验，技术层面的障碍远小于制度层面。

但“接口通了”与“敢不敢认”是两回事。承认一张境外电子发票或一笔境外支付的效力，意味着本国税务、反洗钱与消费者保护机构愿意把核验责任部分交给对方系统：对方签发的身份是否可靠、留痕是否可追溯、出问题时能否跨境追责与退赔。这些判断依赖对彼此监管与司法执行的信任，而非接口文档。能力较强的一方往往要求对方先补齐可核验的存证、申诉与执法记录，能力较弱的一方则担心一旦开放，本地机构反而沦为单纯的数据接收端。互认在这里不是一次签字，而是一串持续的能力确认。

## 中小微企业的数字鸿沟：一体化红利到不了的地方

规则一体化的收益分布并不均匀。大型平台与出口企业有法务、合规与信息技术团队，能把新规则折算成内部流程，甚至参与标准讨论；中小微企业却常常连基本的电子记账、可追溯存证与数据分类都未建立。对它们而言，跨境合规不是一道可以优化的成本，而是一道是否进得去的门槛。

这带来一个反直觉的结果：越精致的规则，越可能把小企业挡在外面。电子发票、可追溯、数据分类分级、跨境传输评估，每一项单独看都合理，叠加起来却是一笔固定成本，不随企业规模缩小。区域谈判若只谈“规则对齐”而不谈“能力补齐”，一体化红利会先流向已经具备合规能力的头部企业，中小微企业反而被更清楚地标在了门外。这也是为什么能力建设、技术援助与过渡期安排，在文本里往往比市场准入条款更吃紧。

## 数据保护法成熟度与既有章节的重叠：多层规则之间

另一个绕不开的事实，是各国数据保护立法与执法机构的成熟度并不在同一阶段。有的已建立较完整的个人数据保护制度与独立监管机构，有的仍以部门规章与分散条款为主，跨境传输、数据主体权利、违规处罚的口径都不一致。框架协议要在这样的底座上谈互认，等于要求不同成熟度的制度彼此承认对方的合规判断。

叠加其上的是既有规则。全面与进步跨太平洋伙伴关系协定、区域全面经济伙伴关系协定以及诸多双边安排里，都已写入电子商务、数据流动与电子签名相关章节，各自措辞、例外与争端解决路径并不相同。框架协议并非从零起草，而是要处理与这些既有承诺的重叠：哪些沿用、哪些优先、冲突时以何者为准。多方观察认为，这类“规则叠加”本身就是谈判耗时的重要来源；而对企业来说，多套并存的标准若不能收敛，所谓一体化反而会变成更多套需要同时满足的清单。

## 互认为何变成单向让步：监管能力不对等下的执行难题

把前面几节收拢，核心论点就落在这里：区域数字规则的难点不在文本，而在各国监管能力不对等，使“互认”容易变成单向让步。互认的前提是双方都能替对方把好门；当一方具备完整的登记、审计、取证与处罚能力，另一方尚在搭建时，承认对方的合规结论，实质上是能力较弱一方把准入判断交了出去，却未必换得对等的准入回报。文本上是对称的义务，执行上是不对称的负担。

这也解释了谈判为何倾向于“先框架、后细则”“先自愿、后约束”，以及为何执行机制的讨论总比市场准入更艰难。框架可以先立，能力却无法一纸补齐；没有可核验的存证、可追溯的日志、可执行的处罚与可用的救济，任何互认条款都停在纸面。真正决定这份框架协议能走多远的，不是签约时的措辞有多漂亮，而是签署之后各国是否愿意投入长期成本，把监管能力这条底线慢慢垫平。在那之前，谨慎不是保守，而是对不对等现实的一种诚实回应。

## 延伸阅读

**数字经济框架协议（Digital economy framework agreement）**：为电子商务、数据治理、支付与消费者保护等议题建立共同规则的区域协定，通常先立框架、再以议定书或细则逐步推进。

**数据本地化（Data localisation）**：要求特定数据在本国境内存储或处理、限制其自由跨境流动的制度安排，理由多涉及执法取证与隐私保护。

**互认安排（Mutual recognition arrangement）**：各方相互承认对方合规认定、许可或标准效力的机制，其可行性取决于彼此监管能力是否大致对等。

# The Weight of Mutual Recognition: Asymmetry in the ASEAN Digital Economy Framework Agreement Talks

Talks on the ASEAN Digital Economy Framework Agreement (DEFA) are often folded into a familiar story of integration advancing another step: harmonise e-commerce rules, open up cross-border data, recognise payments and invoices across borders — a net woven ever tighter. Yet what decides whether that net bears weight is not how neatly clauses are drafted, but whether national regulators operate at comparable capability. The deeper the talks go, the more fragile that premise looks.

Digital rules are distinctive in that mutual recognition on paper needs real equivalence to hold. Whether a regulator can verify a foreign compliance certificate, trace a cross-border data flow and enforce penalties at home decides how willing it is to let someone else administer market access. When capabilities differ markedly, recognition is rarely a two-way convenience but an asymmetric arrangement: one side opens its market, the other carries the burden of proof. That is where understanding the slowness and caution begins.

## The ambition of a framework agreement: stitching scattered rules into one net

The agreement is not meant to be just another trade-facilitation document but a common set of rules for e-commerce, data governance, payments, electronic signatures and consumer protection. Across the region, digital markets, languages, legal traditions and regulators differ widely, so the same service must satisfy different disclosure, licensing and record-keeping requirements at each border, with the cost landing on firms. The framework tries to turn these scattered thresholds into standards that connect.

Such integration need be neither exaggerated nor doubted: only when rules connect can cross-border transactions move from case-by-case handling to one set of documents travelling across several markets. But the denser the net, the more it demands of each node. Mutual recognition assumes every node can identify, record and assign liability for violations; if one cannot, risk flows back along the weakest link. The ambition therefore carries an unspoken premise — that regulatory capabilities are roughly aligned, which is precisely what does not hold across ASEAN.

## Cross-border data flows and localisation storage: the seam hardest to align

Cross-border data flows are the hardest seam to align. On one side lies the basic demand of digital business: order, logistics, payment and customer-service data need to move freely within a group or platform, and fragmented storage raises latency and cost. On the other stands the insistence on local storage, justified by law-enforcement access, privacy, tax audit and data sovereignty. Neither is a pretext, yet they point to opposite infrastructure.

The difficulty is less in stating a principle than in drafting exceptions. Allowing cross-border flows while reserving restrictions for public-policy goals is wording nearly everyone accepts, but once it reaches which data, which circumstances, who decides and how disputes are remedied, disagreement surfaces. More practical still is verification: even if one side promises data will move only within compliant environments, the other, lacking the means to audit flows, read logs and trace onward transfers, cannot tell whether the promise is kept. Whether flows are permitted depends less on the text than on whether anyone can check.

## Mutual recognition of e-payments and e-invoices: the gap from interface to trust

Mutual recognition of e-payments and e-invoices looks like the most technical, most likely area to deliver early results: message formats, field standards, certificates and identity checks can be aligned item by item in working groups. The region has accumulated interoperable experience in payment linkage, QR standards and electronic documents, and the obstacles here are smaller at the technical than the institutional level.

But a working interface and a willingness to recognise are different things. Accepting a foreign e-invoice or payment as valid means a domestic tax, anti-money-laundering and consumer-protection authority hands part of its verification duty to a counterpart system: whether the identity issued is reliable, records traceable, and liability and refunds pursuable across borders. These judgements rest on trust in each other's regulation and enforcement, not interface documentation. The stronger side asks the weaker to build verifiable records, appeals and enforcement history first; the weaker fears its agencies will become mere data receivers. Recognition is not a signature but a continuing chain of capability confirmations.

## The digital divide among smaller firms: where integration gains do not reach

The gains from integration are not evenly spread. Large platforms and exporters have legal, compliance and IT teams able to fold new rules into internal processes, and may even shape standards; micro, small and medium enterprises often lack basic electronic bookkeeping, traceable records and data classification. For them, cross-border compliance is not a cost to optimise but a threshold deciding whether they enter at all.

This produces a counter-intuitive result: the more refined the rules, the more likely they keep small firms out. E-invoicing, traceability, data classification and transfer assessments each look reasonable alone, but together form a fixed cost that does not shrink with firm size. If talks address only rule alignment and not capability building, gains flow first to incumbents already able to comply, while smaller firms are marked even more clearly as outsiders. That is why capacity building, technical assistance and transition periods prove more contentious than market-access clauses.

## Uneven data-protection laws and overlapping chapters: layered rules

Another unavoidable fact is that national data-protection law and enforcement sit at different stages of maturity. Some countries have fairly complete personal-data regimes with independent regulators; others rely on sectoral rules and scattered provisions, with inconsistent definitions of cross-border transfer, data-subject rights and penalties. Negotiating mutual recognition on such a base asks systems of uneven maturity to accept one another's compliance judgements.

Layered on top are existing rules. The Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership, the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership and many bilateral arrangements already contain chapters on e-commerce, data flows and electronic signatures, each with different wording, exceptions and dispute routes. The framework is not drafted from a blank page but must manage overlap: which to retain, which takes precedence, what governs a conflict. Many observers see such layering as a reason the talks take time; and for firms, if standards do not converge, integration can become a longer list of requirements to satisfy at once.

## Why mutual recognition becomes a one-sided concession: enforcement under unequal capacity

Pull the sections together and the argument lands here: the difficulty of regional digital rules lies not in the text but in unequal regulatory capacity, which turns mutual recognition into a one-sided concession. Recognition presupposes both sides can guard the door for each other. When one has full registration, audit, evidence-gathering and sanctioning capacity and the other is still building it, accepting the former's findings means the weaker side hands over its entry judgement without gaining equivalent access. Obligations are symmetrical on paper; the burden is asymmetrical in execution.

That explains why the talks lean towards framework first, detail later, voluntary first, binding later, and why enforcement is harder to discuss than market access. A framework can be set up quickly; capacity cannot be supplied by a document. Without verifiable records, traceable logs, enforceable penalties and usable remedies, any recognition clause stays on paper. What decides how far DEFA can go is not polished signing language but whether governments invest, over the long run, in levelling that regulatory baseline. Until then, caution is not conservatism but an honest response to unequal reality.

## Further reading

**Digital economy framework agreement**: A regional accord establishing common rules for e-commerce, data governance, payments and consumer protection, usually setting a framework first and advancing through protocols or detailed rules later.

**Data localisation**: A regime requiring certain data to be stored or processed within a country and restricting its free cross-border movement, justified mainly by law-enforcement access and privacy protection.

**Mutual recognition arrangement**: A mechanism by which parties accept one another's compliance findings, licences or standards, workable only when their regulatory capacities are roughly comparable.
