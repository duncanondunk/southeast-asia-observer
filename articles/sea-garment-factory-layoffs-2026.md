# 订单少了一半：东南亚服装代工的裁员潮，从万隆烧到越南

在印度尼西亚西爪哇省，纺织业正在经历一场安静但迅速的收缩。

据该省纺织品商业协会介绍，自今年夏季以来，许多工厂接到的来自耐克等主要品牌的订单减少了一半。由于库存尚未售罄，品牌方还要求延迟发货。裁员从10月开始加速：截至目前，全省已有108家服装厂裁员约5.5万人，另有18家工厂倒闭，导致9500人失业。

## 订单为什么突然消失

表面看，这是一次典型的订单波动——品牌去库存、消费疲软、航运与关税的不确定性。但把时间轴拉长，问题不止于此。

过去几年，全球时尚品牌把产能大量转移到东南亚，看重的是廉价劳动力与关税优惠。这种转移的前提是"订单持续增长"。一旦欧美市场需求走弱，最先被削减的就是这些代工环节：工厂不是品牌的资产，而是成本项。

## 越南：世界工厂的另一面

同样的逻辑正在越南上演，而且规模更大。

据越南工贸部数据，单季度停业企业超过4.29万家，倒闭数量同比上升20.1%。曾经一地难求的工业用地开始闲置，部分工业园区昼夜轰鸣的流水线安静下来。

越南的处境有特殊性。它对外贸的依赖度极高，欧美是主要客户；当这些市场的消费能力下降，依赖出口的经济体首当其冲。

## 一组说明结构的数字

印尼纤维与长丝纱生产商协会（APSyFI）在10月13日公布的一组数据，把事情讲得更清楚。

据其统计，过去10年间，印尼纺织及纺织产品行业的进口比率从55%升至83%；同期，越南的这一比率反而从70%降至28%。也就是说，同样在承接全球订单，越南把上游配套做了起来，印尼却越来越依赖进口原料与成品。

另一组数字更直接：过去10年，印尼纺织业对国内生产总值的贡献从3.5%缩至0.97%；而越南纺织业的这一比重约为6%。

## 组装环节留不住订单

越南的案例常被当作"产业升级成功"的样本，但它也有脆弱的一面。真正稳固的制造能力需要本土配套——面料、纱线、辅料、印染；如果只做最后的剪裁与缝制，附加值低，抗风险能力弱。

这也是APSyFI呼吁的核心：印尼的问题不只是竞争力与生产效率，还包括"不合理价格的进口"与"非法成品服装"的冲击。该协会认为，对倾销与非法进口的宽容，已经损害了整个产业链的生态。

## 转内销能接住多少人

面对订单流失，印尼纺织品商业协会负责人杰米给出的方向是根本性改革：开拓新市场、转向面向国内消费的生产。

这个建议听上去合理，却有一道门槛。东南亚多数国家的内需市场规模有限，人均购买力不足以消化原本面向欧美的产能。转内销意味着从"按订单生产"变成"猜市场要什么"，对习惯了代工模式的工厂而言，这是一次能力重构，而不只是一次客户切换。

## 被裁的人怎么办

数字背后是具体的人。

印尼官方近期介入了一起港资服装企业裁员846人的案例。政府曾提供两套方案试图保住岗位：针对资金链问题可通过国有银行提供融资支持，若受阻于法规、原材料或外部市场，则协助对接资源。但企业最终仍选择裁员，核心原因是疫情后财务状况持续恶化，叠加洪涝冲击，资金链断裂难以缓解。

调查中还发现，该企业疑似强迫约200名员工签署补偿协议，仅按法定标准的0.5倍发放遣散费。官方明确表示，任何低于法定标准、受胁迫签署的协议均属无效，并已责令劳工部门彻查。劳工社保机构则加速发放失业与养老保障补贴，并开设技能培训课程。

## 品牌在算的那笔账

要理解订单为何消失得这么快，需要看品牌方的账本。

对全球时尚品牌而言，东南亚工厂是可变成本：旺季加单、淡季砍单，库存压力可以迅速转嫁给供应端。近两年欧美需求走弱、库存高企，品牌的第一反应就是减少下单并要求延迟交货——这不是针对印尼或越南，而是这套采购模式的内置动作。

问题在于，代工方承担了几乎全部风险，却很少有议价权。订单的波动被放大成工厂的生死，这是由东南亚制造在价值链上的位置决定的。

## 结语：出口导向模式的再校准

把万隆与越南放在一起看，这场裁员潮并非意外，而是出口导向模式的一次再校准。

过去20年的产业转移，让东南亚获得了就业与外汇，也让它把命运系在了别人的订单上。当订单收缩，真正的分水岭不是谁的成本更低，而是谁的产业链更完整、谁的内需更厚实。

对那些只承接组装环节的经济体来说，眼下这场裁员，或许是一次不得不接受的提醒。

## 延伸阅读

**代工（original equipment manufacturing, OEM）**：品牌方提供设计与订单，制造方按规格生产并贴牌出货的模式。代工环节附加值较低，订单随品牌方的库存与销售策略波动。

**进口比率（import ratio）**：一国某行业所用原料与半成品中来自进口的比重。比率越高，说明本土配套越薄弱，产业对外部供应链的依赖越强。

**产业配套（industrial supporting capacity）**：为终端制造提供面料、纱线、零部件、模具等上游供给的本土能力，是判断制造业是否"扎根"的关键指标。

# Orders Halved: Southeast Asia's Garment Layoffs, From Bandung to Vietnam

In Indonesia's West Java province, the textile industry is going through a quiet but rapid contraction.

According to the provincial textile business association, since this summer many factories have seen orders from major brands such as Nike cut in half. Because inventories have not yet sold through, brands have also asked for delayed shipments. Layoffs accelerated from October: so far 108 garment factories in the province have shed about 55,000 workers, while another 18 factories have closed, putting 9,500 people out of work.

## Why the orders vanished

On the surface this is a classic order fluctuation — destocking by brands, weak consumption, uncertainty over shipping and tariffs. Stretch the timeline out, though, and the problem runs deeper.

Over the past few years, global fashion brands moved large volumes of capacity into Southeast Asia, drawn by cheap labour and tariff advantages. That shift assumed orders would keep growing. Once demand in Europe and the United States weakens, the first thing cut is exactly this contract-manufacturing link: a factory is not a brand's asset but a cost line.

## Vietnam: the other side of the world's factory

The same logic is playing out in Vietnam, on a larger scale.

According to Vietnam's Ministry of Industry and Trade, more than 42,900 enterprises suspended operations in a single quarter, with closures up 20.1 percent year on year. Industrial land once fought over now sits idle, and production lines that once ran day and night in some parks have fallen quiet.

Vietnam's position is particular. Its economy depends heavily on foreign trade, with Europe and the United States as its main customers; when purchasing power in those markets falls, export-dependent economies take the first hit.

## A set of numbers that explains the structure

A set of figures published on October 13 by the Indonesian Fiber and Filament Yarn Producers Association (APSyFI) makes the picture clearer.

By its count, over the past decade the import ratio of Indonesia's textile and textile-product sector rose from 55 percent to 83 percent, while Vietnam's fell from 70 percent to 28 percent over the same period. In other words, both were taking on global orders, but Vietnam built up its upstream supporting industries while Indonesia grew ever more dependent on imported materials and finished goods.

Another pair of figures is starker still: over the past decade, the textile sector's contribution to Indonesia's GDP shrank from 3.5 percent to 0.97 percent, whereas in Vietnam it stands at about 6 percent.

## An assembly link cannot hold orders

Vietnam is often cited as a success story of industrial upgrading, but it has a fragile side too. Durable manufacturing capacity needs domestic supporting industry — fabric, yarn, trims, dyeing. If a country only does the final cutting and sewing, value added is low and resilience to shocks is weak.

This is the core of APSyFI's argument: Indonesia's problem is not only competitiveness and production efficiency but also the impact of "unreasonably priced imports" and "illegal finished garments." Tolerance of dumping and illegal imports, the association argues, has damaged the ecosystem of the entire supply chain.

## How many can domestic sales absorb?

Facing the loss of orders, the head of the Indonesian textile business association, Jemi, points to fundamental reform: opening new markets and shifting to production for domestic consumption.

The advice sounds reasonable, but there is a threshold. Most Southeast Asian economies have limited domestic markets, and per-capita purchasing power is not enough to absorb capacity once aimed at Europe and the United States. Switching to domestic sales means moving from producing to order to guessing what the market wants — for factories used to contract manufacturing, that is a rebuilding of capabilities, not just a change of customer.

## What happens to those laid off

Behind the numbers are people.

Indonesian authorities recently intervened in the case of a Hong Kong-invested garment firm cutting 846 jobs. The government offered two options to preserve the posts: financing support through state banks for cash-flow problems, or help matching resources if the obstacles lay in regulations, raw materials or external markets. The company nonetheless chose to proceed, the core reason being finances that had deteriorated since the pandemic and, compounded by flooding, a funding chain that could not be repaired.

The investigation also found the firm had allegedly pressured about 200 workers into signing compensation agreements paying only 0.5 times the statutory rate. Officials made clear that any agreement below the legal standard and signed under duress is void, ordering the labour ministry to investigate. The social security agency moved to speed up unemployment and pension payments and to open skills-training courses.

## The math brands are doing

To understand why orders vanished so fast, look at the brands' ledger.

For global fashion brands, Southeast Asian factories are variable costs: add orders in peak season, cut them in the trough, and pass inventory pressure quickly onto suppliers. With demand in Europe and the United States weakening and inventories high over the past two years, brands' first response was to order less and ask for later delivery — not a move aimed at Indonesia or Vietnam, but a built-in reflex of this sourcing model.

The problem is that manufacturers bear almost all the risk while having little bargaining power. Order volatility is magnified into a matter of survival for factories, and that is set by where Southeast Asian manufacturing sits in the value chain.

## Conclusion: recalibrating the export-led model

Seen together, Bandung and Vietnam suggest this wave of layoffs is not an accident but a recalibration of the export-led model.

Two decades of industrial relocation brought Southeast Asia jobs and foreign exchange, and also tied its fate to other people's orders. When those orders contract, the real dividing line is not who is cheaper but whose industrial chain is more complete and whose domestic demand is thicker.

For economies that only take on assembly, this round of layoffs may be a reminder they have little choice but to accept.

## Further reading

**Original equipment manufacturing (OEM)**: A model in which the brand supplies design and orders while the manufacturer produces to specification and applies the brand's label. Value added is relatively low, and orders swing with the brand's inventory and sales strategy.

**Import ratio**: The share of an industry's raw materials and semi-finished inputs that come from abroad. The higher the ratio, the weaker the domestic supporting base and the greater the industry's dependence on external supply chains.

**Industrial supporting capacity**: The domestic ability to supply upstream inputs — fabric, yarn, components, moulds — for final manufacturing. It is a key indicator of whether manufacturing has truly taken root.
