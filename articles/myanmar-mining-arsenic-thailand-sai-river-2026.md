# 砷从矿场流进水龙头：泰国北部两万户家庭的跨境水账

一条河把两个国家的治理缺口连在了一起。据法新社报道，泰国北部清莱府美塞县的自来水厂检出砷含量超标30倍，当局自11月起暂停向14个村庄供水，超过2万户家庭陷入用水危机。政府副发言人拉莉达11月18日说，首相阿努廷已在17日晚下令寻找替代水源。水可以靠水车临时运，但问题不会随水车开走——砷从哪里来、谁该为此负责，才是这场危机的真问题。

## 30倍：从自来水管里流出的砷

先看清数字。美塞县自来水厂砷超标30倍，湄公河支流赛河未经处理的河水报出超标25倍；另一组检测则显示赛河原水砷浓度达每公升0.3毫克，是泰国地表水标准的30倍。数字略有出入并不奇怪——不同点位、不同时间，本就不该期待一个整齐的答案。真正刺眼的是“超标”本身：砷俗称砒霜，是明确的致癌物，煮沸去不掉，长期饮用会在体内累积中毒。

更值得追问的是覆盖的盲区。泰国人民党领袖纳塔蓬指出，当局主要检查地方自来水管理局的供水系统，而这套系统只占整体用水量约15%。也就是说，其余八成以上的村落自建供水，可能根本没进检测网。参议员南塔娜批评官员视察时对着镜头喝过滤水作秀，2套反渗透设备远远不够供应2万多户，“我们需要专业部长，而不是把自己当成内容创作者的人”。这话刻薄，却戳中要害：危机管理如果只剩镜头前的表演，数字再漂亮也落不到水龙头上。

## 溯源：卫星建模指向缅甸金矿

这一次没有停留在“疑似”。泰国纳黎萱大学研究人员上星期公布，化学分析和卫星建模结果显示，赛河含砷沉积物的主要来源可追溯至缅甸境内的金矿开采。清莱府尹11月9日召集会议时提到，缅甸上游有逾20座矿场，影响范围不止2万户用水，还有6万余莱农田。土壤和农作物同样在吸收砷——纳塔蓬说，部分地区连稻米都检出了污染物。

这是典型的跨境外部性：开采在缅甸，收益流向矿场，成本和风险却顺着赛河下泄到泰国。上游没有约束，下游就得买单。法新社报道里那句“缅甸旷日持久的内战导致自然资源遭到无序开采”，其实把病因说透了一半——不是技术做不到清洁开采，而是战乱状态下没人有能力和动力去管。

## 内战把矿山变成“无主生意”

缅甸的资源开采长期与冲突绑在一起。掸邦是重灾区，金矿和稀土矿在同一片山坡上被反复开挖，而稀土元素正是智能手机、风力涡轮机和电动车的关键原料。全球绿色转型与电子供应链对矿产的饥渴，撞上一个国家的治理崩塌，结果就是“要矿不要命”的开采竞赛。

其中的结构相当冷酷：军政府需要外汇，地方武装需要经费，中间商需要利润，各方都想在最短时间内把矿挖出来运走，环保与劳工标准成了最先被砍掉的成本。对下游邻国来说，这不是“别人的内政”——矿山废料不会停在国境线上。当一个国家的内部冲突可以被外部化为另一个国家的公共健康危机，所谓不干涉内政的原则就撞上了自己的边界。

## 中资矿场：一场绕不开的问责

泰国官方明确表示，由于缅甸许多采矿作业由华人或中资经营，政府已就此事与北京方面进行了讨论。掸邦人权基金会此前指控，有中国采矿人员在赛河上游沿岸开采黄金。参议员南塔娜援引数据称，缅甸掸邦稀土开采造成的砷污染最高达安全标准28倍，并呼吁政府直接与被指涉的中国投资者交涉。

这里要把话说清楚：指出中资矿场的责任，不等于把环境问题变成地缘指控。企业注册在哪里、资本来自哪里，就应当在哪里被追问环境义务——这是通行的商业伦理，不是政治站队。去年泰国向北京提出污染问题时，中国驻泰大使馆支持开展科学调查，这是建设性的起点。但“支持调查”之后要有动作：矿产溯源、供应链尽责、对违规企业的约束，缺一不可。泰国学者提出的五项措施里，就包括暂停缅甸矿产进口及对华出口、建立矿产溯源机制。这些建议能否落地，考验的是“企业出海”叙事里环境问责的成色。

时机也微妙。中国国务院总理李强11月13日至14日访泰，反对派议员帕达拉蓬敦促阿努廷借此请北京协助追踪矿产运输。据泰国中文社梳理，阿努廷事后表示已把泰北河流污染问题通报中国与缅甸。外交渠道是通的，剩下的问题是意愿与执行。

## 工作组的悖论：跟军政府谈环保

泰国的应对看起来有条不紊：副首相兼外长西哈萨11月12日在内比都会见缅总统敏昂莱，提出郭河、赛河和鲁阿克河的污染问题；两国同意成立水质问题工作组，预计11月底举行首次会议；泰国还寻求缅方批准，在境内的河流上游安装水质监测设备。

但这套安排有一个绕不过去的悖论。要让工作组拿到真实数据、让监测设备真正运行，前提是上游矿场所在地——一个正在打内战的军政府——愿意且能够约束矿主。一个连辖区都未必完全控制的政权，有多少能力执行环保承诺，要打个问号。更现实的问题是，那些矿场往往与地方武装或军方关联，触动它们等于触动冲突各方的钱袋子。

换句话说，技术性安排解决不了政治性病因。工作组能做的是记录污染、协调预警，做不到的是让采矿停下来。如果把“成立了工作组”当作危机处理的终点，两万户家庭等来的，可能只是一份更精确的污染报告。

## 湄公河下游：一条河的公共账

外界最担心的，是污染顺流而下扩散至湄公河干流。湄公河流经多个国家，沿岸数以百万计的人口依赖这条河饮水、灌溉和捕鱼。赛河只是支流，但河流是连通的——今天在美塞的超标砷，明天可能出现在更下游的取水口。

这暴露了区域环境治理的一处空白。澜湄合作机制运行多年，围绕航运、水电与防洪有不少协调，但面对矿山污染这类非传统安全议题，约束力明显不足。人民党领袖纳塔蓬呼吁把问题带上东盟峰会和亚太经济合作组织会议，方向是对的：跨境污染本就该在跨境机制里解决。但多边场合的声明能否变成上游的实地整改，需要的是可核查的监测、可追溯的供应链和明确的问责主体，而不是又一份原则性共识。

危机当前，水车能解一时之渴。真正要算清的，是一条河的公共账：谁排放，谁治理，谁买单。这笔账如果一直悬着，砷就不会只停在赛河。

## 延伸阅读

**砷污染（Arsenic contamination）**：砷及其化合物进入水体与土壤造成的污染，具致癌性，煮沸无法去除；长期饮用受污染水可致皮肤、神经与心血管损害，是东南亚部分矿区常见的环境健康风险。

**跨境外部性（Transboundary externality）**：一国或一地经济活动产生的成本外溢至他国，而排放方未承担相应代价的现象；跨境河流污染是典型案例，通常需双边或多边机制协调治理。

**供应链尽责（Supply-chain due diligence）**：企业对其采购与投资链条中的环境和社会风险进行识别、预防和补救的义务；在矿产领域常体现为溯源、审核与对违规供应商的约束。

# From mine to tap: the cross-border water bill facing 20,000 Thai households

A single river has stitched together the governance gaps of two countries. According to AFP, the water plant in Mae Sai, Chiang Rai, detected arsenic at 30 times the permitted level, prompting authorities to suspend tap water to 14 villages from November and leave more than 20,000 households without a safe supply. Deputy government spokesperson Lalida said on 18 November that Prime Minister Anutin had ordered a search for alternative sources the previous evening. Water trucks can cover the immediate shortfall, but they do not drive away the problem. Where the arsenic came from and who should answer for it are the questions that matter.

## Thirty times the limit: arsenic from the tap

Start with the numbers. The Mae Sai water plant registered arsenic 30 times above the standard; untreated water in the Sai River, a Mekong tributary, was reported at 25 times; another set of tests put arsenic in the raw river water at 0.3 milligrams per litre, or 30 times Thailand's surface-water limit. The discrepancies are unremarkable — different points, different moments, no reason to expect a tidy figure. What is jarring is the excess itself. Arsenic is a confirmed carcinogen that boiling cannot remove and that accumulates in the body with prolonged exposure.

More revealing is the blind spot in coverage. Natthaphong Ruengpanyawut, leader of the People's Party, noted that inspections focus on the local water authority's network, which accounts for only about 15 per cent of total water use. The other eighty-plus per cent of villages rely on self-supplied systems that may never enter the testing grid. Senator Nantana Nantavaropas criticised officials who drank filtered water in front of cameras during site visits, and said two reverse-osmosis units were nowhere near enough for more than 20,000 households. "We need professional ministers, not people who see themselves as content creators," she said. Cutting, perhaps — but it lands on the nerve: if crisis management amounts to a camera-side performance, handsome numbers never reach the tap.

## Tracing the source: satellite models point to Myanmar's gold mines

This time the suspicion hardened into evidence. Researchers at Naresuan University said last week that chemical analysis and satellite modelling traced the main source of arsenic-bearing sediment in the Sai River to gold-mining operations inside Myanmar. When the Chiang Rai governor convened a meeting on 9 November, officials pointed to more than 20 mines upstream and an impact extending beyond 20,000 households to over 60,000 rai of farmland. Soil and crops are taking up arsenic too; Natthaphong said contaminants had been detected in rice in some areas.

This is a textbook case of transboundary externality: extraction happens in Myanmar, revenue flows to the mines, and the cost runs downhill into Thailand. Without restraint upstream, the bill lands downstream. The AFP report's observation — that Myanmar's protracted civil war has produced unregulated extraction of natural resources — states only half the cause. The problem is not that clean extraction is technically impossible; it is that in a war zone nobody has the capacity or the incentive to enforce it.

## Civil war turns mines into a no-man's business

Myanmar's resource extraction has long been bound up with conflict. Shan State is the epicentre, where gold and rare-earth deposits are dug and re-dug on the same slopes; rare-earth elements are essential to smartphones, wind turbines and electric vehicles. The global hunger for minerals driven by the green transition and electronics supply chains has collided with a state in governance collapse, producing a race to dig fast and ship out faster, with environmental and labour standards the first costs to be cut.

The structure is grimly simple. The junta needs foreign exchange, local armed groups need funding, middlemen need margins. Everyone wants the ore out in the shortest possible time, and remediation becomes an expense nobody wants to book. For downstream neighbours, this is not "someone else's internal affair" — mine waste does not stop at the border. When one country's internal conflict can be exported as another country's public-health crisis, the principle of non-interference meets its own limit.

## Chinese-funded mines: an accountability question that won't go away

Thailand has said explicitly that, because many mining operations in Myanmar are run by ethnic Chinese or Chinese-funded interests, it has raised the matter with Beijing. The Shan Human Rights Foundation has previously accused Chinese miners of panning for gold along the upper Sai River. Citing data, Senator Nantana said arsenic from rare-earth mining in Shan State reached up to 28 times the safety standard, and called on the government to deal directly with the Chinese investors involved.

Let us be precise. Naming the responsibility of Chinese-funded mines is not a geopolitical accusation dressed up as environmental concern. Where a company is registered and where its capital originates are precisely the places it should be asked about its environmental obligations — that is ordinary business ethics, not taking sides. When Thailand raised the pollution with Beijing last year, the Chinese embassy supported a scientific investigation, a constructive start. But support must be followed by action: traceability, supply-chain due diligence, and constraints on non-compliant firms. Among the five measures proposed by a Thai academic were a suspension of Myanmar mineral imports and exports to China, and a traceability regime. Whether such proposals land is a test of how much substance sits inside the "going global" narrative.

The timing was delicate. Chinese Premier Li Qiang visited Thailand on 13–14 November, and opposition MP Padarapong urged Anutin to seek Beijing's help tracing mineral shipments. According to a Thai Chinese-language news portal, Anutin said afterwards that Thailand had notified both China and Myanmar about the contamination. The diplomatic channel is open; what remains is will and enforcement.

## The working-group paradox: negotiating environmental protection with a junta

Thailand's response looks orderly on paper. Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister Sihasak met President Min Aung Hlaing in Naypyidaw on 12 November and raised pollution in the Kok, Sai and Ruak rivers. The two sides agreed to set up a water-quality working group, expected to hold its first meeting by the end of November, and Thailand sought Myanmar's approval to install monitoring equipment upstream.

But this arrangement contains a stubborn paradox. For the working group to obtain real data and the monitors to function, the authority that controls the upstream mining zone — a junta fighting a civil war — must be both willing and able to rein in mine operators. A regime that may not fully control its own territory cannot be assumed to have the capacity to enforce environmental commitments. More to the point, those mines are often linked to local armed groups or military interests; touching them means touching the finances of the very parties in the conflict.

Technical arrangements, in short, cannot cure a political aetiology. A working group can document pollution and coordinate warnings; it cannot make the digging stop. If "we have set up a working group" becomes the endpoint of crisis management, the 20,000 households may receive only a more precise report on the contamination.

## Downstream Mekong: a river's public ledger

The wider fear is that the contamination will travel downstream into the Mekong mainstream. The river crosses several countries, and millions depend on it for drinking water, irrigation and fishing. The Sai is a tributary, but rivers are connected: arsenic recorded today in Mae Sai may surface at a downstream intake tomorrow.

This exposes a gap in regional environmental governance. The Lancang–Mekong Cooperation mechanism has run for years, with considerable coordination on shipping, hydropower and flood control, but its grip on non-traditional security issues such as mine pollution is visibly weak. Natthaphong's call to raise the matter at the ASEAN summit and the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation forum is directionally right: cross-border pollution belongs in cross-border institutions. Yet whether multilateral declarations become on-the-ground remediation depends on verifiable monitoring, traceable supply chains and identifiable accountability — not another statement of principles.

Two water trucks can quench an immediate thirst. The harder task is to settle a river's public ledger: who discharges, who remediates, who pays. Until that account is balanced, the arsenic will not stay in the Sai.

## Further reading

**Arsenic contamination**: pollution of water and soil by arsenic and its compounds; carcinogenic and not removable by boiling, it can cause skin, neurological and cardiovascular damage with chronic exposure, and is a recurring environmental-health risk near mines in Southeast Asia.

**Transboundary externality**: a cost generated by economic activity in one country that spills into another without the polluter bearing the price; river pollution is the classic case and usually demands bilateral or multilateral governance.

**Supply-chain due diligence**: the obligation of firms to identify, prevent and remedy environmental and social risks in their sourcing and investments; in minerals it typically means traceability, auditing and sanctions on non-compliant suppliers.
