# 中美竞争挤压东盟政策空间 不选边站要靠经济多元化

在中美战略竞争持续加剧的当下，东南亚正被两股力量同时拉扯。新加坡尤索夫伊萨东南亚研究院（ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute）客座资深研究员奥尔森（Stephen Olson）9月25日在新加坡"慧眼中国环球论坛"（FutureChina Global Forum）一场讨论会上直言：美国和中国都在加强对东南亚国家的压力，东盟过去在两大国之间左右逢源的做法，正变得"越来越困难"，可腾挪的政策空间也在收窄。这番话戳中的，是许多东南亚决策者的心头之患——当两头都不敢得罪，中间的回旋余地却越来越小，平衡术还走得通吗？

据联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）报道，奥尔森给出的药方并不复杂：东南亚国家应根据自身国家利益作决定，不要屈服于压力而偏向任何一方；同时积极实现贸易和经济关系多元化，以降低任何一个大国施加影响和制约的能力。这话听起来像是老生常谈，却也道出了一个朴素道理——与其在大国之间赌边站，不如先把自家篮子里的鸡蛋分开放。

## 平衡术越来越难走

必须承认，奥尔森口中的"越来越困难"并非危言耸听。过去二十年，东盟的生存之道正是"大国平衡"：在中美之间保持等距，两头获利。可当华盛顿把贸易、技术、供应链乃至人工智能算力统统塞进国家安全框架，当北京因西方市场受阻而将过剩产能向外疏导，东盟那种"脚踩两只船"的从容，正被实实在在的压力挤压。

问题是，压力来自两边，却不是对称的。美国的压力多体现在规则和准入上——关税、技术封锁、友岸外包；中国的压力则更多体现在市场与产能上——价格优势明显的商品涌进马来西亚、印度尼西亚等市场，当地一些工厂已因竞争而倒闭。两种压力叠加，东盟国家既要防住西方的"脱钩"大棒，又要接住东方的"溢出"浪潮，平衡的难度自然水涨船高。

## 亚洲内部贸易仍在快速增长

不过，地缘政治的紧张并没有掐断亚洲内部的经贸联系，这或许是东盟最该抓住的锚点。PSA国际港务集团东北亚区域总裁林锦川指出，尽管面对关税和地缘政治紧张，亚洲内部贸易近年仍快速增长。随着供应链日趋复杂，一件产品在生产过程中可能多次跨境流动，反而为区域创造了更多贸易机会。

这其实点出了一个常被忽视的事实：中美在"上面"博弈，亚洲在"下面"做生意。当全球层面的规则之争陷入僵局，区域内产业链的韧性反而成了避风港。对东盟而言，这意味着不必被动等待两大国发落，而可以主动深耕亚洲内部市场，把"区域全面经济伙伴关系协定"（RCEP）这类机制用足用好。

## 从"制造基地"到"增长引擎"：中企出海的转向

华侨银行（OCBC）大中华区总裁及香港首席执行官王克提供了一个耐人寻味的观察：中国企业赴东南亚投资的供应链布局，已从单纯把生产基地从中国搬走，逐步扩展到采购、制造、分销乃至服务环节。换句话说，东盟如今"不只是中国企业的制造基地"。

凭更年轻的人口、较高的数码化程度与不断增长的消费市场，东盟正成为中国企业的国际扩张增长引擎。王克还点出，地缘政治本身就是推动部分中企转向东盟的动因之一——东盟可能成为许多产品的最终组装地，再销往欧美市场。这对东盟是机会，也是考验：机会在于产业升级与就业，考验在于如何避免在"承接转移"中沦为单纯的组装车间，乃至被过剩产能反噬。

## 两大趋势：美国政策走向与中国产能过剩

展望未来三至五年，奥尔森认为有两大趋势值得关注。其一是美国政策走向。他判断美国不太可能回到战后推动全球化和自由贸易的传统角色，未来美国总统是否延续特朗普对全球化及贸易的态度，将深刻影响区域局势。其二是中国过剩产能的外溢。奥尔森警告，若东盟国家因国内产业压力而采取反击措施，中国可能作出强烈回应，进一步加剧区域紧张。

这两点合起来，勾勒出一幅并不轻松的图景：一边是保护主义回潮的不确定性，一边是产能外溢引发的本土产业阵痛。东盟若处理不当，很容易从"左右逢源"滑向"左右挨打"。奥尔森的潜台词其实很清楚——多元化不是选择题，而是必答题。

## 人员往来：被忽视的稳定器

在宏大叙事之外，携程集团（Trip.com）首席执行官孙洁与安美德集团（Amata Corporation）首席执行官邱威功不约而同地把目光投向了人。孙洁希望更多国家扩大免签安排、开通直飞航班，鼓励年轻人跨国学习生活；邱威功则愿世人把彼此看作"一家人"，少看外貌衣着与饮食习惯的差异。

这看似柔软，却可能是最实在的缓冲垫。经贸摩擦会升温，但民间往来织起的相互理解，往往是危机时刻防止关系决裂的最后一道防线。对夹在两大国之间的东盟而言，把"人"的连接做厚，或许比在谈判桌上多争几个百分点更为长久。

说到底，东盟不愿做棋盘，也做不起棋盘。奥尔森们的提醒值得听进去：在大国竞争的时代，小国最大的底气，从来不是选对边，而是把自己的路子走宽。

# US–China Rivalry Squeezes ASEAN's Policy Space; Diversification, Not Alignment, Is the Answer

As strategic competition between the United States and China intensifies, Southeast Asia is being pulled in two directions at once. Stephen Olson, a visiting senior fellow at Singapore's ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute, put it bluntly at a panel of the FutureChina Global Forum on 25 September: both Washington and Beijing are tightening pressure on Southeast Asian states, and the old ASEAN habit of balancing between the two powers is becoming "increasingly difficult," with the room to manoeuvre steadily narrowing. His remark lands on a genuine anxiety among regional policymakers — when neither side can be offended yet the middle ground keeps shrinking, can the balancing act still work?

According to Lianhe Zaobao, Olson's prescription is straightforward enough: Southeast Asian countries should decide on the basis of their own national interests rather than yield to pressure and lean toward either side, and they should actively diversify their trade and economic relationships to reduce any single great power's ability to influence or constrain them. It sounds like a platitude, yet it captures a plain truth — rather than betting on which giant to side with, it is wiser to spread one's eggs across several baskets first.

## The balancing act is getting harder

To be fair, Olson's "increasingly difficult" is no alarmism. For two decades ASEAN's survival formula has been "great-power balancing" — keeping equal distance from Washington and Beijing and profiting from both. But when Washington folds trade, technology, supply chains and even AI computing power into a national-security frame, and when Beijing, blocked in Western markets, channels excess capacity outward, the comfortable "two-boat" posture is being squeezed by real pressure.

The pressure, however, is not symmetrical. American pressure shows up mostly in rules and access — tariffs, tech controls, friend-shoring. Chinese pressure arrives more through markets and capacity — goods with strong price advantages flood into Malaysia, Indonesia and other markets, and some local factories have already shut down under competition. Stacked together, ASEAN must fend off the West's "decoupling" stick while absorbing the East's "spillover" wave; the balancing act naturally grows harder.

## Intra-Asia trade keeps growing

Yet geopolitical tension has not severed intra-Asian economic ties — and that may be the anchor ASEAN should cling to. Lim Jin Chuan, Northeast Asia regional president of PSA International, noted that despite tariffs and geopolitical strain, intra-Asia trade has grown rapidly in recent years. As supply chains grow more complex, a single product may cross borders several times during production, creating additional trade opportunities for the region.

This points to an often-overlooked fact: while the US and China joust "up top," Asia keeps doing business "down below." When rule-making at the global level stalls, the resilience of regional supply chains becomes a safe harbour. For ASEAN, it means not waiting passively for the two giants to dictate terms, but proactively deepening the Asian internal market and making full use of mechanisms such as the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP).

## From 'factory floor' to 'growth engine'

Wang Ke, Greater China head and Hong Kong CEO of OCBC Bank, offered a telling observation: Chinese firms' supply-chain footprint in Southeast Asia has expanded from simply relocating production bases out of China to encompass procurement, manufacturing, distribution and even services. In other words, ASEAN is now "not just a manufacturing base for Chinese companies."

With younger populations, higher digitalisation and growing consumer markets, ASEAN is becoming a growth engine for Chinese firms' international expansion. Wang Ke also noted that geopolitics itself is one reason pushing some Chinese companies toward ASEAN — the region may serve as the final assembly point for goods later sold to Europe and America. This is both an opportunity and a test for ASEAN: the opportunity lies in industrial upgrading and jobs; the test is how to avoid being reduced to a mere assembly workshop in the "relocation" cycle, or being backfired on by excess capacity.

## Two trends to watch

Looking three to five years ahead, Olson flags two trends. The first is the direction of US policy. He judges that America is unlikely to return to its postwar role of championing globalisation and free trade, and whether a future president sustains Trump's stance on globalisation and trade will profoundly shape the region. The second is the spillover of Chinese excess capacity. Olson warns that if ASEAN states retaliate under domestic industrial pressure, China may respond forcefully, further escalating regional tension.

Together, the two sketch an uncomfortable picture: on one side, the uncertainty of resurgent protectionism; on the other, the domestic pain of capacity spillover. Handle it poorly and ASEAN could slide from "profiting from both sides" to "caught in the crossfire." Olson's subtext is clear enough — diversification is not an option but a necessity.

## People-to-people: the overlooked stabilizer

Beyond the grand narrative, Jane Sun, CEO of Trip.com, and Vikrom Kromadit, CEO of Amata Corporation, both turned their gaze to people. Sun hopes more countries will expand visa-free arrangements and open direct flights, and encourages young people to study and live across borders; Kromadit wishes the world would see one another as "one family," looking past differences in appearance, dress and diet.

This may sound soft, yet it could be the most practical cushion. Economic frictions heat up, but the mutual understanding woven through grassroots exchange is often the last line of defence against a total breakdown in relations during crises. For ASEAN caught between two giants, thickening the human connection may prove more durable than haggling over a few extra percentage points at the negotiating table.

In the end, ASEAN neither wants nor can afford to be a chessboard. Voices like Olson's are worth heeding: in an era of great-power competition, a small state's greatest strength has never been picking the right side, but widening its own path.
