# 当机房撞上社区：东南亚数据中心热遭遇“社会许可证”危机

人工智能（AI）基础设施的狂飙突进，正在东南亚撞上一堵资本砌不起来的墙——社区的同意。据《日经亚洲》报道，横跨亚洲的“红火热”数据中心建设潮正引发日益强烈的公众反弹：从澳大利亚到印度、韩国、马来西亚与泰国，居民开始走上街头抵制自家后院里拔地而起的服务器农场。对一门把“土地便宜、电价低廉”当作招商口号的生意而言，这记回马枪来得很突然，却也合乎逻辑。

## 数字：柔佛州的一场 5.8 吉瓦豪赌

最尖锐的矛盾集中在马来西亚柔佛州（Johor）。这里紧邻新加坡，自新加坡在 2019—2022 年因土地与水资源吃紧冻结新批数据中心后，国际运营商把规划容量成片搬过海峡，令柔佛一跃成为东南亚增长最快的枢纽。如今该州在运与规划中的数据中心容量已逼近 5.8 吉瓦。问题在于，一套 100 兆瓦的机房每天仅冷却就可能耗水约 400 万升；有州官员称超大规模设施的人均取水约为传统工业用户的 200 倍。一份估算把数据中心的承诺用水量推高至每日逾 8 亿升，而现有基础设施据称每天只能供给约 1.4 亿升。缺口摆在那里：若再批新项目而不补供水，增长的代价就得由家庭、其他产业与公共水务来背。

## 从欢迎红毯到警觉

两年前，地方还在为“睡城边的棕榈庄园终于迎来巨资”而兴奋；如今话风陡转。2026 年 8 月 20 日，吉隆坡近郊哥打白沙罗（Kota Damansara）一个数据中心项目的开发商撤回了建筑许可申请——起因是一份线上请愿在一周内集得逾 6000 个签名，居民担心噪音与缺水。柔佛州南部顶点格朗巴亥（Gelang Patah）的居民也曾聚到工地旁，抗议未来的供水前景。在人口最多也最富有的雪兰莪州（Selangor），围绕社区负担的争论已经政治化。咨询机构仲量联行（JLL）的克里斯·霍华德说得直白：人人都想要更快的网速，却强烈抵触机房落在自家社区，理由无非是电价上涨、耗水，或者“就是栋丑建筑”。业内的新口头禅变成了“社会许可证”（social licence）——拿不到它，再多的资本也开不了工。

## 泰国按下暂停键：49 个停建、117 个冻结

泰国给出了更硬的一课。2026 年 9 月，曼谷方面叫停了约 49 个在建数据中心，并冻结了约 117 个待批项目，直到全国层面的电力、用水与选址规则在约一个月内厘清。讽刺的是，此前曼谷一座机房竟被“仓库”名义分类、贴着医院获批，以此绕开若干审查要求——这正是公众怒火的燃料。泰国此前为战略产业开了审批快速通道，如今却要回过头补上“本地效益”与资源账。可见，招商时的油门踩得越猛，回调时的刹车声就越响。

## 电费账单终究要付

比水更根本的约束是电。马来西亚央行数据显示，数据中心用电目前约占半岛总电力的 7%，到 2035 年可能升至约 31%。电网的“最后一公里”——变电站、输电线、备用容量——才是真正的瓶颈，而非有没有投资。整个区域都在同一道坎上：新加坡、柔佛、曼谷、大雅加达、马尼拉与巴淡岛预计到 2030 年还需新增 35 至 45 太瓦时的电力，而许多地方的配电网络本是为更慢、更分散的增长设计的。印尼爪哇—巴厘电网本就逼近实用极限，煤电仍占约六至六成五，企业的净零承诺因此被打了折扣。越南以低成本吸金，却也年年夏天拉闸。一句话：主机可以跨国搬，电网搬不动。

## “记分卡”取代空白支票

马来西亚已经开始改打法。财政部秘书总长公开承认，即便巨额资本也不能想当然带来足够的外溢。评估逻辑正从“看投资金额”转向“记分卡”——既算经济放大器，也算对能源与水资源压力的冲击。柔佛州去年底直接禁掉两类极耗水的数据中心（单日需水高达 5000 万升，相当于 20 个奥运泳池）；雪兰莪虽不硬性封杀，却要求项目满足全球能效与水效标准，并把 30% 的本地含量（如集成电路设计、冷却系统）写入门槛。显见的纠偏还包括：国家水务委员会在雪兰莪、柔佛与森美兰受理的 808 百万升日申请中，只批了不到 18%。

## 对东盟人工智能之赌意味着什么

这场反弹提醒人们一个被忽视的真相：承接算力，不等于掌握人工智能经济。机房带来土建、工程就业、能源投资与税收，但人工智能的高价值环节——软件、模型、研发、专业服务——大多不在这栋楼里。若东盟只做“算力房东”，便可能重演电子组装大国“有产能、无设计”的老剧本。更棘手的是治理仍各自为政：一家企业把服务器铺满多国，却要在每个市场分别应付数据治理、网络安全、牌照与跨境数字服务的不同规则，碎片化可能跑赢协调。社区反弹、资源红线与监管碎片化三股力叠加，正在把东南亚的人工智能基建从“抢跑”逼向“算账”。谁能把社会许可证、电网与本地价值链一起补齐，谁才真正握得住这张人工智能牌。

## 延伸阅读

**社会许可证（Social Licence）**：社区对工业项目非正式的“同意与容忍”，拿不到它，再大的资本也难落地，是资源型与基建项目的新门槛。

**记分卡评估（Scorecard Assessment）**：马来西亚评估外资数据中心的新的方法，从单纯看投资金额转向综合衡量经济外溢与能源、水资源压力，取代“空白支票”式招商。

**东盟电网（ASEAN Power Grid）**：旨在实现跨国电力交易的区域互联计划，老挝—泰国—马来西亚—新加坡电力集成项目是其早期样板，可缓解但无法消除数据中心的本地缺电。

# When Server Farms Hit the Neighbourhood: Southeast Asia's Data-Center Boom Runs Into a "Social Licence" Crisis

The artificial-intelligence infrastructure gold rush is running into a wall that capital alone cannot build — the consent of the community. Nikkei Asia reports that the "red-hot" data-center buildout sweeping Asia is fuelling intensifying public backlash: from Australia to India, South Korea, Malaysia and Thailand, residents are taking to the streets to block server farms rising in their backyards. For an industry that sold itself on "cheap land, cheap power," the boomerang is sudden but logical.

## The numbers: a 5.8-gigawatt gamble in Johor

The sharpest friction sits in Malaysia's Johor state, just across the strait from Singapore. After Singapore froze new data-center approvals between 2019 and 2022 over land and water stress, international operators shifted planned capacity en masse across the causeway, turning Johor into Southeast Asia's fastest-growing hub. Its operational and planned capacity has now surged toward 5.8 gigawatts. The catch: a 100-megawatt facility can burn roughly 4 million litres of water a day just for cooling, and state officials say hyperscale sites may consume about 200 times more water than a conventional industrial user. One estimate places committed data-center water demand above 800 million litres daily, while existing infrastructure reportedly delivers only about 140 million. The gap is stark — approve more without new supply, and households, other industries and public utilities foot the bill.

## From welcome mat to wariness

Two years ago, localities cheered as "sleepy palm estates" landed mega-capital. Today the tone has reversed. On 20 August 2026, a developer withdrew its building permit for a data center in Kota Damansara near Kuala Lumpur after an online petition gathered more than 6,000 signatures in a week, with residents fearing noise and water shortages. In Gelang Patah, at the southern tip of Johor's boom, residents gathered outside a construction site to protest future water availability. In the richest and most populous state of Selangor, the debate over community burden has been politicised. JLL's Chris Howard puts it bluntly: everyone wants faster data, yet acceptance collapses locally over perceived price hikes, water use, or simply "ugly buildings." The new industry keyword is the "social licence" — without it, no amount of capital breaks ground.

## Thailand hits pause: 49 halted, 117 frozen

Thailand delivered the harder lesson. In September 2026, Bangkok halted about 49 data centers under construction and froze roughly 117 pending approvals until national rules on power, water and siting are finalised within about a month. The irony is apt: a Bangkok facility had earlier been approved next to a hospital by classifying it as a warehouse, sidestepping several reviews — precisely the kindling for public anger. Having opened a fast-track for strategic investment, Thailand must now retro-fit a "local benefit" and resource ledger. The louder the accelerator during the pitch, the louder the brake on the rebound.

## The electricity bill comes due

The deeper constraint is power. Bank Negara data show data-center electricity use is about 7% of Peninsular Malaysia's total today and could reach roughly 31% by 2035. The real bottleneck is the grid's "last mile" — substations, transmission lines, reserve capacity — not the availability of investment. The whole region is on the same ledge: Singapore, Johor, Bangkok, Greater Jakarta, Manila and Batam are expected to need an extra 35 to 45 terawatt-hours by 2030, yet many distribution networks were built for slower, dispersed growth. Indonesia's Java-Bali grid was already near its practical limit, with coal still supplying some 60% to 65%, blunting corporate net-zero pledges. Vietnam lures capital on low costs yet faces recurring summer outages. Hosts can move across borders; the grid cannot.

## A "scorecard" replaces the blank cheque

Malaysia has begun changing its playbook. The treasury secretary-general conceded that even vast capital cannot be assumed to generate enough spillover. Evaluation is shifting from "size of investment" to a "scorecard" that weighs economic amplifiers against energy and water stress. Late last year Johor banned two categories of extremely water-intensive centers needing up to 50 million litres daily — the volume of 20 Olympic pools. Selangor does not outright ban but vets projects to global efficiency standards and writes a 30% local-content requirement — in chip design and cooling systems — into the threshold. The correction is visible: of 808 million litres a day requested across Selangor, Johor and Negeri Sembilan, the water commission approved under 18%.

## What it means for ASEAN's AI bet

The backlash exposes an overlooked truth: hosting compute is not the same as capturing the AI economy. Server halls bring construction, engineering jobs, energy investment and tax revenue, but the high-value layers — software, models, research, specialised services — mostly sit elsewhere. If ASEAN merely becomes "compute landlord," it risks replaying the old script of electronics assemblers: capacity without design. Governance compounds the problem: a firm spreading servers across countries still faces differing rules on data governance, cybersecurity, licensing and cross-border digital services in each market, and fragmentation can outrun coordination. Community pushback, resource red lines and regulatory fragmentation are together dragging Southeast Asia's AI buildout from a sprint into an audit. Whoever replenishes the social licence, the grid and the local value chain at once is the one who truly holds the AI card.

## Further reading

**Social Licence**: A community's informal "consent and tolerance" for an industrial project. Without it, even the largest capital struggles to break ground — the new gatekeeper for resource and infrastructure schemes.

**Scorecard Assessment**: Malaysia's revised method for vetting foreign data centers, shifting from raw investment size to a balanced measure of economic spillover against energy and water stress, replacing the blank-cheque approach to investment promotion.

**ASEAN Power Grid**: A regional interconnection plan enabling cross-border electricity trade; the Laos–Thailand–Malaysia–Singapore Power Integration Project is an early model that eases but cannot erase local data-center power shortages.
