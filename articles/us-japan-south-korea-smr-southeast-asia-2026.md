# 美日韩联手押注东南亚核电：一台反应堆背后的印太博弈

日美韩以小型模块化反应堆（SMR）为支点切入印尼，并剑指马来西亚与菲律宾，把核能出口写进了「自由开放印太」的战略脚本。表面是能源生意，内里却是一次围绕技术路线与规则主导权的地缘布局。

## 核能出口的「锁定时长」

联合早报10月26日援引共同社报道，日本、美国、韩国正联手推动下一代核电站「小型模块化反应堆」（SMR）在第三国的推广，印度尼西亚被列为最热门的候选地。表面看，这是一笔能源生意；往深里看，它更像是一次地缘布局。

为什么各国对出口核电站如此上心？关键在于「锁定时长」。一座核电站卖出去，收益远不止建设本身——后续的核燃料供应、运维服务、人员培训，会把出口国与进口国绑定数十年。也就是说，谁帮印尼建了反应堆，谁就长期握住了对方能源安全与产业标准的一角。这恰恰是日美韩把此事与「自由开放的印度太平洋」战略挂钩的原因：核能合作，是经济影响力与安全影响力的一次打包输出。

## 三国分工：各取所长的一盘棋

这次合作最聪明的地方，在于互补。美国在反应堆设计上领先，韩国擅长核电站的「交钥匙」总承包建设，日本则在关键部件制造与安全工程上见长。三家还考虑成立合资公司，把设计、融资、建造、运营串成一条完整链条。

这背后是现实的工业逻辑。美国本土核电项目的核管理委员会（NRC）审批周期长、资本回收慢，企业急需海外参照项目；而韩国与日本恰好补上了美国流失的重型锻造与设备制造能力。三者合体，才向新兴市场端出一份「完整、可融资、有交付记录」的项目包。换句话说，单打独斗谁都难赢中国或俄罗斯的国家队，抱团才有价格与信用的底气。

## 印尼为何是必争之地

印尼被日美韩视为跳板，不止因为它体量大。按照印尼2025至2034年电力规划，到2034年要上马500兆瓦核电，主要服务苏门答腊与加里曼丹的矿区——那里加工镍、铝土矿、铝，眼下靠高排放的自备燃煤电厂供电。一台能替代燃煤的小型模块化反应堆，几乎自带客户。

但印尼绝不是「非谁不可」。它已与俄罗斯国家原子能公司（Rosatom）签下浮动式核电站协议，美日企业也在做引进调查，竞争早已白热化。更微妙的是，日本在东南亚目前只与越南签有核能协定，要与印尼缔结转运核设备的协定，还得先创造政治条件。谁能先帮印尼把「矿区绿电」落地，谁就拿下了这场多厂商博弈的先手。

## 与中国、俄罗斯的正面交锋

日美韩的这套打法，明面上是商业，暗地里是对标。联合早报点得很直白：中国和俄罗斯正试图通过向印度太平洋地区出口核电站扩大影响力，日美韩的举动「似乎意在与之抗衡」。

中国的「华龙一号」、俄罗斯的俄原能公司（Rosatom）都是国家背书的出口主力，在价格、融资、工期上往往更灵活。日美韩的差异化筹码，写在合作备忘录的第三根支柱里——统一安全与非扩散标准。这套「西方标准」叙事，正是他们用来区隔中俄方案的卖点。问题在于，标准之争从来不只是技术之争，更是规则主导权之争；而东南亚国家要的，往往是那个出价合理、条款不绑手绑脚的选项。

## 东南亚的机遇与隐忧

对东南亚而言，小型模块化反应堆确实有现实吸引力。地区电力需求预计到2050年翻倍，电网却支离破碎、受群岛地形所限，50至300兆瓦级的小堆比吉瓦级大堆更对路；人工智能与数据中心带来的用电飙升，更让各国急着找低碳基荷。菲律宾目标2032年上1200兆瓦核电、泰国规划两座300兆瓦机组、越南同时审视大堆与小堆——需求是真实的。

隐忧也同样真实。这里是地震与海啸频发的环太平洋带，监管体系大多稚嫩，融资与公众接受度都是门槛；更关键的是，今天签下的技术路线，会锁定未来四五十年的燃料、运维与人才依赖。把能源安全托付给外部供应商，究竟是「多元化」还是新的「单点依赖」，各国决策层心里都得有本账。

## 结语：技术选择即战略选择

日美韩想把印尼当作跳板，再把业务拓展到马来西亚、菲律宾，这个「由点及面」的节奏，暴露了真正的目标不在一两座反应堆，而在整个印度太平洋的能源与规则版图。对东南亚来说，多方竞标从来不是坏事——它意味着议价空间。但技术选择从来不只是采购选择，它是一份写进未来半个世纪的战略契约。谁能真正帮这片土地把电点亮、把碳降下来、把主动权留在本土，谁才配得上这份信任。

## 延伸阅读

**小型模块化反应堆（Small Modular Reactor, SMR）**：单机容量约50至300兆瓦、可模块化制造与部署的新一代反应堆，因前期投资小、应急规划区小，被视为适配东南亚碎片化电网的低碳基荷选项。

**自由开放印太（Free and Open Indo-Pacific, FOIP）**：美日等国提出的区域战略框架，强调航行自由、规则秩序与基础设施的「高质量」供给；核能合作被纳入其经济与安全影响力的延伸工具。

**华龙一号（Hualong One）**：中国自主研发的三代压水堆核电技术，由国家核电巨头背书出海，是本区域与美日韩、俄罗斯方案竞争的主力机型之一。

# Washington, Tokyo and Seoul Bet on Southeast Asia's Nuclear Future

The United States, Japan and South Korea are using small modular reactors (SMRs) as a wedge into Indonesia, with Malaysia and the Philippines next in line, folding nuclear exports into the script of a "Free and Open Indo-Pacific." On the surface it is an energy deal; underneath, it is a geopolitical play over technology paths and rule-setting.

## The lock-in effect of nuclear exports

On 26 October, Lianhe Zaobao, citing Kyodo News, reported that Japan, the United States and South Korea are working together to promote the deployment of next-generation small modular reactors (SMRs) in third countries, with Indonesia named as the hottest candidate. On the surface this looks like an energy deal. Look closer, and it reads like a geopolitical move.

Why do governments care so much about exporting reactors? The answer lies in what one might call the "lock-in duration." Selling a nuclear plant is never just about construction. Fuel supply, maintenance, safety audits and workforce training bind exporter and importer together for decades. Whoever builds Indonesia's reactors acquires a lasting foothold in that country's energy security and industrial standards. That is precisely why Tokyo frames the push as deepening the "Free and Open Indo-Pacific" strategy: nuclear cooperation is economic and security influence, bundled and exported together.

## A division of labour: each plays to its strengths

The shrewd part of this trilateral play is complementarity. The United States leads in reactor design; South Korea brings turnkey construction expertise; Japan contributes key-component manufacturing and safety engineering. The three are even weighing a joint venture that would stitch design, financing, construction and operation into a single chain.

The industrial logic is real. Domestic licensing before the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission is slow and capital cycles are long, so American vendors urgently need overseas reference projects. South Korea and Japan happen to supply the heavy forging and equipment capacity that the United States has partly lost. Together they can offer emerging markets a complete, bankable package with a delivery record. Alone, none of them beats China's or Russia's state-backed teams on price and schedule. As a bloc, they gain leverage.

## Why Indonesia is the prize

Indonesia is treated as a springboard not merely because of its size. Under its 2025–2034 power plan, the country targets 500 MW of nuclear capacity by 2034, aimed mainly at the mining belts of Sumatra and Kalimantan, where nickel, bauxite and aluminium are processed using high-emission captive coal plants. An SMR that can replace that coal capacity arrives with a customer already in place.

But Indonesia is nobody's captive. It has already signed an agreement with Russia's Rosatom for floating nuclear plants, and American and Japanese firms are also scouting introductions, so competition is already fierce. Tellingly, Japan has signed a nuclear cooperation agreement in Southeast Asia only with Vietnam; reaching one with Indonesia still requires building political conditions first. Whoever helps Indonesia actually deliver "green power for the mines" first seizes the upper hand in this multi-vendor contest.

## Going head-to-head with China and Russia

The trilateral design is commercial on its face and competitive underneath. Zaobao states it plainly: China and Russia are trying to expand influence by exporting nuclear plants across the Indo-Pacific, and the Japan–US–Korea move "seems intended to counter" them.

China's Hualong One and Russia's Rosatom are both state-backed export champions, often more flexible on price, financing and schedule. The trilateral edge is written into the third pillar of their memorandum of cooperation: harmonised safety and non-proliferation standards. That "Western standards" narrative is the pitch used to distinguish their offer from the Chinese and Russian ones. The catch is that standards disputes are never only technical; they are contests over who sets the rules. And what Southeast Asian buyers often want is simply the option with reasonable terms and the fewest strings attached.

## Southeast Asia's opportunity — and its anxieties

For Southeast Asia, SMRs do have genuine appeal. Regional electricity demand is expected to double by 2050, yet grids are fragmented and constrained by archipelagic geography, so 50–300 MW units fit far better than gigawatt-scale giants. The power surge from AI and data centres only sharpens the hunger for low-carbon baseload. The Philippines aims for 1,200 MW of nuclear by 2032, Thailand plans two 300 MW units, and Vietnam is weighing both large reactors and SMRs. The demand is real.

So are the anxieties. This is the seismically active Pacific Ring, regulatory systems are mostly young, and financing and public acceptance remain hurdles. More fundamentally, the technology path chosen today locks in fuel, maintenance and talent dependence for the next four to five decades. Entrusting energy security to an external supplier — is that diversification, or a new single point of failure? Every regional cabinet ought to keep that scorecard close.

## Conclusion: a technology choice is a strategic choice

Washington, Tokyo and Seoul want Indonesia as a springboard and then Malaysia and the Philippines — the point-by-point rhythm betrays the true target, which is not one or two reactors but the energy and rules map of the entire Indo-Pacific. For Southeast Asia, multiple bidders are never a bad thing; they mean bargaining room. But a technology choice is never merely a procurement choice. It is a strategic contract written into the next half-century. The partner who truly helps light up the grid, cut the carbon and keep agency at home is the one who earns that trust.

## Further reading

**Small Modular Reactor (SMR)**: A new generation of reactors, typically 50–300 MW per unit, built and deployed in modular form. Lower upfront cost and smaller emergency-planning zones make them a candidate low-carbon baseload suited to Southeast Asia's fragmented grids.

**Free and Open Indo-Pacific (FOIP)**: A regional strategy advanced by the United States and Japan emphasising freedom of navigation, rules-based order and "high-quality" infrastructure; nuclear cooperation is folded into its extension of economic and security influence.

**Hualong One**: China's indigenous third-generation pressurised-water reactor, backed by state nuclear champions and a leading competitor to US–Japan–Korea and Russian offerings in the region.
