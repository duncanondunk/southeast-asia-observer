# 一箭七星入轨：银河航天整星出口泰国与商业航天出海的新范式

2026年8月25日11时21分，太原卫星发射中心。长征六号丙遥一运载火箭腾空而起，一箭将7颗卫星送入预定轨道。其中一颗体量不大却分量不轻——银河航天研制的「灵知09泰国立方星」，用户是泰国地理信息与空间技术发展局（GISTDA）。这是中国商业航天企业首次面向东南亚国家完成整星研制、发射及在轨交付，也是银河航天首次实现「整星+地面系统+能力培养」一体化方案在「一带一路」共建国家的落地应用。一颗立方星的入轨，折射出的不仅是技术能力的输出，更是一种新型航天外交的轮廓正在成型。

## 从卖卫星到交钥匙：整星出口的范式跃迁

此次交付的核心创新，在于模式本身。传统航天合作多停留在硬件买卖或单项技术服务层面——你买我的卫星，我帮你发射，合同即止。但银河航天为GISTDA提供的是一套「整星+地面系统+能力培养」的一体化解决方案：泰方人员先赴中国接受集中培训，随后银河航天团队赴泰协助地面站建设与调试。用该公司解决方案部总经理张鹏的话说，这次合作让泰方从「使用卫星」走向「理解卫星」。

这一转变的意义不容小觑。过去数十年间，中国在东南亚的基础设施外交以铁路、港口、电站为主轴，其逻辑是「我建你用」。而此次灵知09项目的逻辑则是「我建、我教、你学会自己运维」——从产品交付升级为体系输出。北京理工大学宇航政策与法律研究所副所长杨宽此前指出，中国商业航天企业正从「出口产品」转向「出口服务与生态」，灵知09正是这一转向在东南亚的首个落地案例。

## 遥感立方星的实用逻辑：国土监测与教育双线并行

灵知09并非通信卫星，而是一颗遥感立方星（CubeSat），主要承担两项任务：一是为泰方收集国土规划、农业生产、生态监测等方向的地表遥感数据；二是在教育层面支撑泰国高校开展卫星设计研制、航天测控、遥感应用等方向的实践教学。GISTDA空间技术副执行主任披·楚斯里（Phee Choosri）评价称，这颗卫星的意义不仅在于一项技术成就，更是人才能力建设、技术研发与知识交流的重要载体。

这种「实用+教育」双线并行的设计，精准契合了东南亚中等收入国家对空间技术的真实需求。这些国家既需要卫星数据服务来支撑国土管理和灾害预警等公共职能，又希望培育本土航天人才以减少对外依赖。一颗同时满足两种需求的立方星，比单纯推销高轨道通信卫星更容易被接受，也更具政治安全性——它不直接触及大国竞争敏感的低轨互联网频谱资源。

## 发射成本的结构性差距：猎鹰9号与中国火箭的算账

然而，中国商业航天出海面临的挑战同样清晰。最核心的一项是发射成本。据行业测算，太空探索技术公司（SpaceX）的猎鹰9号火箭近地轨道发射成本约为每公斤1.8万元人民币，而中国一次性商业火箭的发射成本仍在每公斤5万至15万元区间。换言之，同等载荷下，中国的发射报价大约是SpaceX的3至8倍。

这一差距源于可重复使用技术的成熟度差异。猎鹰9号一级火箭已实现数十次复用，单箭边际发射成本被压至极低水平；而中国民营火箭的可回收技术虽在加速追赶——蓝箭航天（LandSpace）朱雀三号已于2026年成功完成一级助推器陆地回收——但从单次成功到规模化复用、再到成本曲线实质性下探，仍需时间。对银河航天这类卫星制造商而言，发射成本占星座建设总成本的30%至40%，上游降本乏力会直接压缩其在国际市场的价格竞争力。

## 东南亚的太空选择：在星链与中资方案之间

从地缘视角看，灵知09落地泰国恰逢东南亚太空格局加速重构的关键窗口。埃隆·马斯克的星链（Starlink）已在部分东南亚国家推进落地，其优势在于成熟的全球覆盖和相对低廉的终端价格；但星链的数据流经美国本土服务器，引发不少国家关于数据主权和网络安全的顾虑。中国商业航天企业的差异化竞争力恰恰在此：不仅交付卫星，还帮助客户建设自主可控的地面系统和数据处理能力。

GISTDA卫星集成测试与发射准备部门负责人奇特查诺·猜春乔布透露，过去几个月双方工程团队密切协作，共同参与系统设计、卫星集成、测试及任务准备等各阶段。这种深度参与式的合作模式，与星链「终端即服务」的标准化产品路线形成鲜明对比。对泰国而言，选择哪条路径不仅是技术采购决策，更涉及数据治理自主性和长期产业发展的战略取向。

## 数据主权与标准依赖：能力建设的深层政治

更深层次的问题在于，「卫星—地面—人才」全链条输出所塑造的，可能是一种基于技术标准的长期依赖关系。当泰国的工程师习惯了中国卫星的操作界面、数据处理流程和频谱协调规范，未来若要切换到其他国家的卫星系统或地面设备，转换成本将是巨大的。这类似于操作系统层面的「锁定效应」——一旦生态系统形成，迁移的门槛远高于初次采购的价格差。

银河航天联合创始人刘畅表示，公司未来将重点开拓「一带一路」共建国家和地区市场，稳步拓展海外产业布局。目前，垣信卫星（SpaceSail）、时空道宇（Geespace）等中国企业已在巴西、马来西亚和中亚推进类似项目。如果这一模式在更多国家复制开来，中国商业航天构建的将不仅是一个海外客户网络，更是一套以中国技术标准为底层逻辑的区域空间基础设施体系。

## 商业航天的国际化命题：独角兽能否跑通全球市场

作为中国首家商业航天独角兽企业——估值超过10亿美元（约71亿元人民币）——银河航天的国际化尝试具有行业标杆意义。该公司迄今已成功发射自主研制的49颗卫星，其中合成孔径雷达（SAR）卫星超过10颗，订单量在商业航天企业中位居前列。公司正筹备A股上市，海外业务扩张是其向资本市场讲述增长故事的重要一章。

但独角兽身份不等于全球竞争力。在发射成本尚未追平SpaceX、国际政治环境日趋复杂的背景下，中国商业航天企业的出海之路注定不会是一条坦途。灵知09的成功证明了中国企业具备全链条交付能力，但能否将单点突破转化为可持续的全球市场份额，取决于三个变量：火箭复用技术的降本进度、目标国对数据主权的敏感程度，以及中国企业在海外运营中能否真正实现「本地化」而非简单的「技术倾销」。这三个问题的答案，将在未来三五年内逐渐清晰。

## 延伸阅读

**立方星（CubeSat）**：一种标准化的微型卫星规格，通常以10厘米为基本单元（1U），体积小、成本低、研发周期短，广泛用于科研教学、技术验证和对地观测等场景，是近年来商业航天和高校航天教育的主流平台之一。

**合成孔径雷达（SAR）**：一种主动式微波成像遥感技术，不受云层和光照条件限制，可实现全天候全天时对地观测，广泛应用于农业监测、灾害应急、海洋监视和国防安全等领域，是当前商业遥感卫星的核心载荷类型之一。

**商业航天（Commercial Spaceflight）**：指由非政府主体投资和运营的航天活动，涵盖卫星制造、运载火箭发射、在轨服务等产业链环节。中国商业航天自2014年政策放开以来快速发展，已涌现出多家具备整星研制和火箭发射能力的民营企业。

# Seven Satellites, One Rocket: GalaxySpace's Full-Satellite Export to Thailand and a New Paradigm for China's Commercial Space Going Global

At 11:21 a.m. Beijing time on 25 August 2026, a Long March 6C rocket lifted off from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Centre, placing seven satellites into their designated orbits. Among them was a small but consequential payload — the Lingzhi-09 Thailand CubeSat developed by GalaxySpace, commissioned by Thailand's Geo-Informatics and Space Technology Development Agency (GISTDA). This marked the first time a Chinese commercial space company had completed full-satellite development, launch and in-orbit delivery for a Southeast Asian country, and also GalaxySpace's first export of an integrated "satellite plus ground system plus capacity-building" package under the Belt and Road Initiative. The orbital insertion of a single cubesat reveals not merely the export of technical capability, but the emerging contours of a new form of space diplomacy.

## From selling satellites to turnkey delivery: a paradigm shift in export mode

The core innovation of this delivery lies in the model itself. Traditional space cooperation has largely stopped at hardware sales or standalone technical services — you buy my satellite, I launch it for you, and the contract ends there. But what GalaxySpace delivered to GISTDA was an integrated solution covering "the complete satellite, ground system and talent training": Thai personnel first travelled to China for intensive training, followed by on-site deployment by GalaxySpace engineers who assisted with ground-station construction and commissioning. In the words of Zhang Peng, GalaxySpace's general manager of solutions, this cooperation enabled the Thai side to move from "using satellites" to "understanding satellites."

This shift carries significant weight. Over the past several decades, China's infrastructure diplomacy in Southeast Asia has centred on railways, ports and power stations, following the logic of "I build, you use." The Lingzhi-09 project, by contrast, follows the logic of "I build, I teach, you learn to operate independently" — an upgrade from product delivery to system export. Yang Kuan, deputy director of the Institute of Aerospace Policy and Law at Beijing Institute of Technology, has observed that Chinese commercial space firms are shifting from "exporting products" to "exporting services and ecosystems." Lingzhi-09 is precisely the first such case to land in Southeast Asia.

## The practical logic of a remote-sensing cubesat: dual-track land monitoring and education

Lingzhi-09 is not a communications satellite but a remote-sensing CubeSat tasked with two missions: collecting Earth-observation data for Thai use in land-use planning, agriculture and ecological monitoring; and supporting practical teaching at Thai universities in satellite design, spacecraft tracking and control, and remote-sensing applications. Phee Choosri, GISTDA's deputy executive director for space technology, remarked that the satellite's significance lies not only in a technical achievement but as a vehicle for talent capacity-building, technology R&D and knowledge exchange.

This dual-track design of "utility plus education" aligns precisely with the real needs of middle-income Southeast Asian countries for space technology. These states require satellite data services to support public functions such as land management and disaster early-warning, while also hoping to cultivate domestic aerospace talent to reduce external dependence. A cubesat that serves both needs simultaneously is more readily accepted than simply pitching high-orbit communications satellites, and it carries lower political risk — it does not directly touch on sensitive low-Earth orbit internet spectrum resources that are caught up in great-power competition.

## Structural cost gaps: the Falcon 9 versus Chinese rocket arithmetic

Yet the challenges facing China's commercial space sector going overseas are equally clear. The most fundamental one is launch cost. Industry estimates put SpaceX's Falcon 9 launch cost to low Earth orbit at around RMB 18,000 per kilogram (roughly US$2,500), whereas Chinese expendable commercial rockets still operate in the RMB 50,000–150,000 per kilogram range (US$7,000–21,000). In other words, for equivalent payload mass, Chinese launch quotes run approximately three to eight times higher than SpaceX's.

This gap stems from differences in reusable-technology maturity. The Falcon 9 first stage has achieved dozens of reuses, driving marginal per-launch costs to extremely low levels; Chinese private-sector rockets are accelerating their catch-up — LandSpace's Zhuque-3 successfully recovered its first-stage booster on land in 2026 — but moving from a single successful landing to scaled reuse and then to a meaningful downward bend in the cost curve will take time. For satellite manufacturers like GalaxySpace, launch costs account for 30% to 40% of total constellation construction expenditure; upstream cost-reduction stagnation directly compresses price competitiveness in international markets.

## Southeast Asia's space choices: between Starlink and Chinese offers

From a geopolitical perspective, Lingzhi-09's arrival in Thailand coincides with a critical window when Southeast Asia's space landscape is undergoing rapid restructuring. Elon Musk's Starlink has been advancing into select Southeast Asian countries, its advantages lying in mature global coverage and relatively affordable terminal pricing; yet Starlink's data traffic routes through US-based servers, raising data-sovereignty and cybersecurity concerns across the region. The differentiated competitiveness of Chinese commercial space firms lies precisely here: they deliver not just satellites but also help clients build autonomous, controllable ground systems and data-processing capabilities.

Chitchanok Chaichanop, head of GISTDA's satellite integration, test and launch-preparation division, revealed that engineering teams from both sides had worked closely over recent months, jointly participating in system design, satellite integration, testing and mission preparation. This deeply participatory cooperation model stands in sharp contrast to Starlink's standardised "terminal-as-a-service" product route. For Thailand, choosing which path is not merely a technology procurement decision; it touches on data-governance autonomy and long-term industrial-development strategy.

## Data sovereignty and standards dependence: the deeper politics of capacity building

A deeper question is that the full-chain export of "satellite–ground–talent" may be shaping a long-term dependency relationship rooted in technical standards. Once Thai engineers become accustomed to Chinese satellite operating interfaces, data-processing workflows and spectrum-coordination procedures, the switching cost of migrating to another country's satellite system or ground equipment would be enormous. This resembles a "lock-in effect" at the operating-system level — once an ecosystem takes shape, the barrier to migration far exceeds the initial procurement price differential.

Liu Chang, co-founder of GalaxySpace, stated that the company will focus on opening markets in Belt and Road countries and steadily expand its overseas industrial layout. At present, Chinese firms including SpaceSail and Geespace are advancing similar projects in Brazil, Malaysia and Central Asia. If this model replicates across more countries, what Chinese commercial space builds will be not merely an overseas client network but a regional space infrastructure ecosystem whose underlying logic rests on Chinese technical standards.

## The internationalisation proposition for commercial space: can a unicorn go global?

As China's first commercial-space unicorn — valued at over US$1 billion — GalaxySpace's internationalisation attempt carries benchmark significance for the industry. To date, the company has successfully launched 49 self-developed satellites, including more than 10 synthetic-aperture-radar (SAR) satellites, with an order book ranking among the top in the commercial space sector. The company is preparing for an A-share listing, and overseas business expansion forms an important chapter in its growth narrative for capital markets.

But unicorn status does not equal global competitiveness. With launch costs yet to match SpaceX's and the international political environment growing more complex, the path abroad for Chinese commercial space firms will not be smooth. Lingzhi-09 proves that Chinese companies possess full-chain delivery capability, but whether a single breakthrough can be converted into sustainable global market share depends on three variables: the pace of cost reduction through rocket reusability, target countries' sensitivity to data sovereignty, and whether Chinese firms can achieve genuine "localisation" rather than simple "technology dumping" in their overseas operations. Answers to these three questions will become clear over the next three to five years.

## Further reading

**CubeSat**: A standardised microsatellite format, typically using a 10-centimetre cube as the basic unit (1U). Characterised by small size, low cost and short development cycles, CubeSats are widely used in research, education, technology demonstration and Earth observation, and have become a mainstream platform for commercial space ventures and university aerospace programmes.

**Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR)**: An active microwave imaging remote-sensing technology unaffected by cloud cover or illumination conditions, enabling all-weather, day-and-night Earth observation. SAR is extensively applied in agricultural monitoring, disaster response, maritime surveillance and national security, and represents a core payload type for current commercial remote-sensing satellites.

**Commercial Spaceflight**: Space activities invested in and operated by non-governmental entities, spanning satellite manufacturing, launch vehicle services and in-orbit operations. Since policy liberalisation in 2014, China's commercial space sector has developed rapidly, giving rise to multiple private firms capable of full-satellite development and rocket launches.
