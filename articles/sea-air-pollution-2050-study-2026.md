# 气候把雾霾"养肥"了：研究测算东南亚到2050年恐因空气污染多掏约4.2万亿元人民币

一项新研究发出警告：若不采取更有力的行动，到2050年，东南亚因空气污染导致的早逝人数可能上升近一成，区域损失将超过1万亿美元（约7.1万亿元人民币）。这并非遥不可及的推演——在今年这场烟霾季里，账单已经摆在眼前。

据《南华早报》报道，这项发表于《环境国际》（Environment International）的研究由南洋理工大学（Nanyang Technological University, NTU）气候变化与环境健康中心主任任思勇（Steve Yim）领衔，按三种温室气体排放情景，模拟了过早死亡人数与经济损失。最刺眼的一处反差在于：正是气候变化本身，正在通过改变天气系统，让空气污染变得比预想更难治理。

## 研究说了什么

研究的基线是2019年：当年东南亚有114万7259人因空气污染过早离世，代价是4320亿美元（约3.07万亿元人民币）。到了2050年，在高位排放情景（更暖、更干）下，区域损失将达到1.023万亿美元（约7.26万亿元人民币），较2019年增加5910亿美元（约4.2万亿元人民币），早逝人数上升近一成。

中位排放情景则是最乐观的一版：经济损失仅增加2460亿美元（约1.75万亿元人民币），早逝人数反而较2019年下降1.8%——因为该情景下稍暖且降水略增，湿润条件有助于冲刷污染物。

任思勇对《南华早报》表示，气候变化预计将大幅重塑天气系统，并为东南亚带来更多极端天气事件；空气污染是该地区最紧迫的环境问题之一，已构成沉重的健康负担。换言之，减排与治污是同一道题的两面。

## 数字：到2050年的代价

分国别看，印尼与泰国在三种情景下都是受损最重的国家。印尼的损失增量在高、低位排放情景下分别达3070亿美元（约2.18万亿元人民币）与2130亿美元（约1.51万亿元人民币）；泰国则为1210亿美元（约8590亿元人民币）与1010亿美元（约7170亿元人民币）。

重灾集中在南苏门答腊、南泰国等次区域：人口密集，降水偏少，污染物容易堆积。一个看似矛盾的结果是，研究测算区域细颗粒物（PM2.5）浓度整体可能下降，但在印尼与泰国的热点地带，污染水平仍会上升，并持续推高健康与经济负担。

换句话说，总量改善掩盖了局部恶化——这正是政策最容易失焦的地方。

## 为什么印尼和泰国最受伤

答案藏在地理与气候里。印尼每年旱季的林火、泥炭地与油棕种植园的烧芭，是跨境烟霾的源头；南部人口稠密，静风少雨，污染物难以扩散。泰国的季节性焚烧、农作物秸秆处理与城市工业排放，则在南北两端叠加。

这些都不是偶然事故，而是结构性的排放格局。只要烧芭与露天焚烧的激励不变，气候越干热，火势越猛，烟霾就越久。研究点名的"热点"，本质上是一张被气候放大的既有风险地图。

## 气候与污染的双向缠绕

气候变化与空气污染的关系，不是单向的"污染导致变暖"，而是相互纠缠。更暖、更干的天气会催生更多林火、更多静稳天气，削弱部分地区的污染物冲刷；可一旦降水增多，反而有助于清除颗粒物。这种非线性，意味着单靠全球减排并不能自动解决东南亚的雾霾。

本地行动必须跟上：管控烧芭、升级工业与交通排放、推广清洁炉灶。否则，全球气候议程再漂亮，区域天空仍会按时变灰。

## 医疗短板放大经济损失

研究的共同作者、南洋理工大学李光前医学院院长沈祖尧（Joseph Sung）点出一个被忽视的维度：发展中国家承受的经济负担更重，而它们的医疗体系仍有很大改善空间。并发症患者康复更慢，甚至难以康复，经济冲击因此远高于发达国家。

世界卫生组织早已明确，长期暴露于污染物会提升心脏病、中风与癌症的风险。当医疗体系薄弱时，同样浓度的细颗粒物（PM2.5），在雅加达或曼谷造成的伤亡与误工，比在东京或新加坡更贵。

说到底，空气污染的经济账，最后是由最脆弱的人群和最薄弱的公共卫生系统一起买单。

## 区域协作的缺口

东盟并非没有跨境烟霾治理协定，但执行长期松软，烟霾年年卷土重来。这项研究相当于一记警钟：经济账单正在抬升，而邻国之间的协同却跟不上。

可行的抓手包括：跨境空气质量联合监测、协同防火、清洁炊具与交通替代、以及对公共卫生系统的定向投入。研究的乐观情景已经证明，若路径得当，早逝人数不升反降——1万亿美元（约7.1万亿元人民币）的代价，是一个选择，而非宿命。窗口还开着，但正在收窄。

## 延伸阅读

**细颗粒物（PM2.5）**：直径小于2.5微米的悬浮颗粒物，可深入肺部与血液，是空气污染致早逝的核心指标。

**跨境烟霾（Transboundary Haze）**：由印尼等地的林火与烧芭引发、随季风扩散至邻国的区域性空气污染，是东南亚细颗粒物（PM2.5）的周期性峰值来源。

**《环境国际》（Environment International）**：聚焦环境与健康交叉领域的国际同行评审期刊，本研究的发表载体。

# How climate change is feeding Southeast Asia's air-pollution bill: a study puts the 2050 cost at US$1 trillion

A new study warns that, without stronger action, deaths from air pollution in Southeast Asia could rise nearly 10 per cent and cost the region more than US$1 trillion by 2050. This is no distant projection — the bill is already visible in this year's haze season.

The research, published in Environment International, was led by Steve Yim of Nanyang Technological University's Centre for Climate Change and Environmental Health. It modelled premature deaths and economic losses across three greenhouse-gas emissions scenarios. The most unsettling twist: climate change itself, by reshaping weather systems, is making air pollution harder, not easier, to control.

## What the study says

The 2019 baseline shows 1,147,259 premature deaths in Southeast Asia at a cost of US$432 billion. By 2050, under a high-emissions scenario (warmer and drier), the regional loss reaches US$1.023 trillion — a US$591 billion increase — with deaths up nearly 10 per cent.

The medium-emissions scenario is the most optimistic: economic loss rises only US$246 billion and premature deaths actually fall 1.8 per cent from 2019, because slightly warmer temperatures with modestly higher rainfall help wash pollutants out of the air.

Yim told the South China Morning Post that climate change is expected to substantially reshape weather systems and bring more extreme events to Southeast Asia, and that air pollution is among the region's most urgent environmental problems, imposing a significant health burden. In short, cutting emissions and cleaning the air are two sides of one problem.

## The numbers: the cost by 2050

Broken down by country, Indonesia and Thailand suffer the largest losses under every scenario. Indonesia's increase stands at US$307 billion (high) and US$213 billion (low); Thailand's at US$121 billion and US$101 billion.

The damage concentrates in subregions such as southern Sumatra and southern Thailand: densely populated, with less rainfall, so pollutants accumulate. A seeming paradox emerges — the study projects that regional PM2.5 concentration may decline overall, yet pollution keeps rising in Indonesian and Thai hotspots, sustaining the health and economic burden.

In other words, an aggregate improvement masks local deterioration — precisely where policy tends to lose focus.

## Why Indonesia and Thailand suffer most

The answer lies in geography and climate. Indonesia's annual dry-season forest fires, peatland burning and oil-palm clearing are the source of transboundary haze; the densely populated south, with calm winds and little rain, traps the pollution. Thailand's seasonal burning, crop-residue disposal and urban-industrial emissions stack up at both its northern and southern ends.

None of this is accidental — it is a structural emission pattern. As long as the incentives for clearance fires and open burning persist, hotter and drier weather means bigger fires and longer haze. The "hotspots" the study names are essentially an existing risk map amplified by climate.

## Climate and pollution: a two-way tangle

The relationship between climate change and air pollution is not the one-way "pollution causes warming" story, but a tangle. Warmer, drier weather fuels more fires and more stagnant air, weakening pollutant washout in some places; yet more rainfall can help clear particles. This non-linearity means global emissions cuts alone will not automatically solve Southeast Asia's haze.

Local action must follow: regulating clearance fires, upgrading industrial and transport emissions, and promoting clean cookstoves. Otherwise, however polished the global climate agenda looks, the regional sky will still turn grey on schedule.

## Weak health systems amplify the loss

Co-author Joseph Sung, dean of NTU's Lee Kong Chian School of Medicine, points to a neglected dimension: developing countries bear a heavier economic burden, and their health-care systems still have much room for improvement. Patients with complications recover more slowly, or not at all, so the economic shock runs far higher than in developed countries.

The World Health Organization has long established that long-term exposure raises the risk of heart disease, stroke and cancer. When health systems are weak, the same concentration of PM2.5 costs Jakarta or Bangkok more in lives and lost work than it does Tokyo or Singapore.

Ultimately, the economic bill for air pollution is paid jointly by the most vulnerable people and the frailest public-health systems.

## The gap in regional cooperation

ASEAN is not without an agreement on transboundary haze, yet enforcement has long been soft and the smog returns year after year. This study is a warning bell: the economic bill is climbing while coordination among neighbours lags.

Practical levers include joint cross-border air-quality monitoring, coordinated fire prevention, clean cookstoves and transport alternatives, and targeted investment in public health. The study's optimistic scenario already shows that, with the right path, deaths can fall rather than rise — the US$1 trillion cost is a choice, not a destiny. The window is open, but narrowing.

## Further reading

**Fine particulate matter (PM2.5)**: airborne particles smaller than 2.5 microns that penetrate deep into the lungs and bloodstream, the core metric behind pollution-linked premature deaths.

**Transboundary haze**: the periodic regional smog from agricultural and forest fires — above all in Indonesia — that drifts across neighbours on the monsoon and drives Southeast Asia's PM2.5 peaks.

**Environment International**: the peer-reviewed journal at the intersection of environment and health where this study was published.
