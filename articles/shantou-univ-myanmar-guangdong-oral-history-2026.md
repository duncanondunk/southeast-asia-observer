# 记录南洋：一所广东高校如何为缅甸华侨华人建档

近日，汕头大学区域国别与华侨华人研究院的一支小队飞抵仰光。带队的是院长郑慕强教授，随行有徐成志老师与研究生陈泳翰。他们此行的任务听上去朴素——走访、录音、录像，把一群缅甸粤籍老人的故事留下来。但把这桩"小事"放进更大的坐标里看，它其实是一次对离散记忆的"抢救性"打捞：当亲历"下南洋"那一代人逐渐老去，一所来自侨乡本土的高校，正试图在他们沉默之前，把声音存进档案。

## 一次"抢救性"的乡音记录

据中国侨网（China Qiao Wang）报道，这支团队联合缅甸广东工商总会与缅甸潮州会馆，对近20位缅甸粤籍杰出华侨华人做了深度访谈。受访者里既有缅甸广东工商总会会长陈修桐、常务副会长魏子波这样的商界领袖，也有香港缅中友好协会主席王锦彪、澳门缅华总会会长洪金乐这样横跨港澳与缅甸的"桥接者"。他们讲述的，是各自在缅甸创业、融入与回望祖乡的故事。

为什么要做得这么细？因为口述史要补的，恰恰是统计数字与官方档案补不了的空缺。一个家族何时离乡、第一桶金怎么挣、方言如何在第三代人嘴里变淡——这些"软事实"不会出现在任何一份贸易报表里，却真正定义了一群人一百年的命运。把录音录像留下来，等于给一个正在失语的社群按下"保存键"。

## 第六站为何是缅甸

这支团队此前的足迹已经覆盖泰国（Thailand）、马来西亚（Malaysia）、新加坡、老挝、菲律宾（Philippines）。缅甸是第六站。选择缅甸，并非偶然。缅甸华侨华人社会中，粤籍尤其是潮汕籍群体历史深厚、网络致密。仰光至今矗立着拥有200余年历史的缅甸广东公司、120余年历史的缅甸潮州会馆——这些是比许多现代国家还年长的离散机构，也是乡情与慈善传统的实体载体。

但缅甸又是最"难"的一站。自1962年军人执政以来，当地华人几度经历身份收紧：1967年的反华骚乱、此后的同化压力、再到2021年政变后的整体动荡，都让记忆变得脆弱而敏感。越是动荡，越需要有人把"正常年代"的故事先记下来——这恰恰是学术团队能做的、也最该做的。

## 粤籍与潮汕：一张被低估的离散网络

我们习惯把"华侨华人"当成一个整体，但细看下去，"粤籍"二字分量很重。广东与福建是两大侨乡，而潮汕人（潮州帮）在泰国、马来西亚、新加坡、柬埔寨，以及缅甸，都织就了异常紧密的方言—宗亲—商会网络。潮州会馆不是同乡会那么简单：它是信用体系、是互助网络、也是一种跨国身份的根基节点。

汕头大学本身就在潮汕大地。由一所"侨乡自己的大学"来记录"粤籍同胞"的离散史，这件事本身就有象征意义：它把叙事权从遥远的中央，交还到了出发地的手边。郑慕强团队的国别系列，本质上是用学术方法，把一张被长期低估的粤籍离散网络，一针一线地缝回地图。

## 在动荡中保存记忆

这次调研还顺带走访了第二届全缅广东乡亲大团圆庆典。这类庆典看似热闹，实则是一种"记忆的仪式"——它让散落在各地的粤籍后代，每年有一次重新确认"我们是谁、从哪里来"的机会。在缅甸这样一个族群政治复杂、华人身份长期微妙的国家，这种仪式感不是多余的装饰，而是社群存续的锚。

但也要清醒：口述史能保存的声音，终究是少数"杰出"受访者的。近20位访谈对象里，多是会长、主席、企业家。普通劳工、女性、沉默的大多数，他们的故事仍停留在档案的空白处。真正完整的缅甸粤籍史，需要把麦克风也递给那些没有头衔的人。

## 学术档案，还是侨务叙事

最后一个问题值得直说：这类"记录与保存"工程，到底有多"纯学术"？无可否认，它服务于一种更大的文化寻根与侨务议程——让海外侨胞"记得从哪里来"，从来也是软实力的一环。但与此同时，它确实在抢救真实的人间记忆，填补了官方叙事与冷冰冰数据之间的巨大空洞。

我的看法是：二者并不必然冲突。只要方法诚实、受访者声音不被修剪，一份扎实的口述史，既可以是学术档案，也可以成为离散族群自我理解的材料。问题从来不在"记"，而在"谁来记、记给谁看、记完之后谁来读"。汕头大学的这份缅甸卷，值得期待，也值得被认真对待。

## 延伸阅读

**缅甸华人（Myanmar Chinese）**：指定居缅甸、多祖籍闽粤滇桂的华裔群体，历史上以经商与市镇生活为主，在1967年反华骚乱后转入低调。

**潮州会馆（Teochew Association）**：潮汕籍华侨在东南亚（Southeast Asia）各地建立的同乡组织，兼具互助、慈善与身份凝聚功能，是离散网络的核心节点。

**下南洋（Migration to Nanyang）**：19世纪末至20世纪中叶中国东南沿海大规模赴东南亚谋生的移民潮，构成今日东南亚华侨华人的主体来源。

# Recording the Nanyang: How a Guangdong University is Archiving the Myanmar Chinese Story

Recently, a small team from Shantou University's Institute of Area Studies and Overseas Chinese landed in Yangon. The group was led by Professor Zheng Muqiang, dean of the institute, and included lecturer Xu Chengzhi and graduate student Chen Yonghan. On paper, their task sounded modest: visit, record, film, and preserve the stories of a group of elderly Guangdong-born Chinese in Myanmar. But placed in a larger frame, this is a "rescue" salvage of diaspora memory. As the generation that lived through the great "Nanyang" migration ages and falls silent, a university from the emigrant homeland itself is trying to capture their voices before they fade.

## A "rescue" recording of native-place voices

According to China Qiaowang, the team worked with the Myanmar Guangdong Chamber of Commerce and Industry and the Myanmar Teochew Association to conduct in-depth interviews with nearly twenty prominent Guangdong-origin overseas Chinese in Myanmar. The interviewees ranged from business leaders such as Chen Xiutong, president of the Myanmar Guangdong Chamber, and its executive vice-president Wei Zibo, to bridge figures spanning Hong Kong, Macau and Myanmar — including Wang Jinbiao, chairman of the Hong Kong Myanmar–China Friendship Association, and Hong Jinle, president of the Macau Myanmar Chinese Association. What they shared were stories of building businesses, assimilating into local society, and their emotional ties to the ancestral homeland.

Why go to such lengths? Because oral history fills precisely the gap that statistics and official archives cannot. When a family left home, how the first fortune was made, how the dialect thins in the mouths of the third generation — these "soft facts" appear in no trade report, yet they define a community's century of fate. To record audio and video is, in effect, to hit "save" on a community that is losing its voice.

## Why Myanmar is the sixth stop

The team's previous fieldwork has already covered Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, Laos and the Philippines. Myanmar is the sixth country. The choice is no accident. Within Myanmar's ethnic-Chinese society, the Guangdong-origin — and especially the Teochew — community has deep roots and a dense network. Yangon still hosts the Myanmar Guangdong Company, with a history of over two hundred years, and the Myanmar Teochew Association, over one hundred and twenty years old. These are diaspora institutions older than many modern states, and they are the physical vessels of native-place sentiment and charitable tradition.

Yet Myanmar is also the hardest stop. Since the military took power in 1962, the local Chinese have several times faced tightening identity pressures: the 1967 anti-Chinese riots, the assimilationist push that followed, and the broader turbulence after the 2021 coup have all made memory fragile and sensitive. The more turbulent the times, the more urgent it becomes for someone to record the "normal years" first — and that is exactly what an academic team can and should do.

## Guangdong origin and Teochew: an underrated diaspora network

We are used to treating "overseas Chinese" as a monolith, but look closer and the word "Guangdong-origin" carries real weight. Guangdong and Fujian are the two great qiaoxiang, emigrant homelands, and the Teochew people — the "Teo-chew bang" — have woven exceptionally tight dialect–clan–chamber networks across Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, Cambodia and Myanmar. A Teochew association is far more than a hometown club: it is a credit system, a mutual-aid network, and a root node for a transnational identity.

Shantou University itself sits on Teochew soil. That a university "of the qiaoxiang itself" records the diaspora history of "Guangdong compatriots" is symbolic: it returns the power of narration from a distant centre to the hands of the place of departure. Professor Zheng's country-by-country series is, in essence, an academic method of stitching a long-underestimated Guangdong-origin diaspora network back onto the map, thread by thread.

## Preserving memory amid turbulence

The fieldwork also took in the second All-Myanmar Guangdong Compatriots Reunion Celebration. Such gatherings look festive, but they are in fact rituals of memory — they give Guangdong descendants scattered across the country one chance each year to reconfirm "who we are and where we come from." In a country as complex in ethnic politics as Myanmar, where Chinese identity has long been delicate, this sense of ritual is not superfluous decoration but the anchor of the community's survival.

Yet we should be sober: the voices oral history can preserve are, in the end, those of a minority of "eminent" interviewees. Of the nearly twenty interviewed, most are presidents, chairmen and entrepreneurs. Ordinary labourers, women, the silent majority — their stories still sit in the blank spaces of the archive. A truly complete history of Guangdong-origin Chinese in Myanmar requires passing the microphone also to those without titles.

## Academic archive, or diaspora-affairs narrative

One last question deserves to be stated plainly: how "purely academic" is this kind of "record and preserve" project? There is no denying it serves a broader cultural root-seeking and diaspora-affairs agenda — reminding overseas compatriots "where they came from" has always been part of soft power. At the same time, it genuinely rescues real human memory and fills the yawning gap between official narrative and cold data.

My view is that the two are not necessarily in conflict. So long as the method is honest and interviewees' voices are not trimmed, a solid oral history can be both an academic archive and material for a diaspora community's self-understanding. The question was never "to record or not," but "who records, for whom, and who reads it afterwards." Shantou University's Myanmar volume is worth anticipating — and worth taking seriously.

## Further reading

**Myanmar Chinese**: Ethnic Chinese settled in Myanmar, mostly tracing ancestry to Fujian, Guangdong, Yunnan and Guangxi; historically oriented to commerce and town life, they turned low-profile after the 1967 anti-Chinese riots.

**Teochew Association**: Hometown organizations established by Teochew-origin overseas Chinese across Southeast Asia, combining mutual aid, charity and identity cohesion; a core node of the diaspora network.

**Migration to Nanyang**: The mass migration from China's southeast coast to Southeast Asia from the late nineteenth to the mid-twentieth century, forming the main source of today's Southeast Asian overseas Chinese.
