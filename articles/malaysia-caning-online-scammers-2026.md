在吉隆坡的写字楼与乡间小镇之间，一道源自殖民时代的刑罚正被重新擦亮。当网络诈骗从街头的骗局搬进每个人的手机屏幕，马来西亚政府放出一个信号：要把长期留作"底牌"的鞭刑，伸向屏幕后的骗子。

## 一道被"封存"的刑罚，被重新擦亮

鞭刑在马来西亚并非陌生事物。它源自英国殖民时期，如今仍列于《刑事法典》，多用于强奸、贩毒、非法入境等重罪。但在反诈战场，它长期被"封存"——司法体系更习惯以罚款与监禁应对经济犯罪。据联合早报报道，马来西亚政府近期明确表态，考虑将鞭刑纳入对网络诈骗罪犯的量刑选项，以更强硬的手段震慑猖獗的骗局。

## 诈骗为何逼出一个"古早"手段

数字诈骗在东南亚正呈燎原之势。马来西亚警方数据显示，二〇二五年全国因网络诈骗损失约四十三亿林吉特（约六十九亿元人民币），受害面从退休老人延伸到城市白领。骗局手法不断翻新：假冒官员、冒充亲友、投资杀猪盘、窃取银行验证码，背后往往串联起跨境的"呼叫中心"与洗钱网络。当罚款对职业骗子形同"营业成本"，监禁又常因跨境取证困难而难以执行，政府开始把目光投向那道最具痛感的刑罚。

## 鞭刑能打到痛点吗

支持者认为，鞭刑的羞辱性与身体痛感，是罚款和短期监禁都难以替代的震慑。新加坡正是前车之鉴：这个城市国家长期对抢劫、非法涂鸦等罪行施以鞭刑，其低犯罪率常被归因于"痛感记忆"。马来西亚若效法，等于把反诈从"经济账"升级为"身体账"。但质疑声同样尖锐：鞭刑是否过度？它能否触及躲在境外、远程操控的幕后主脑？对基层"跑分"人员施以肉刑，会不会遮蔽了跨境产业链这一真正的病灶？

## 新加坡的镜子，与东盟的难题

新加坡的经验给了马来西亚底气，却也照出东盟的共同困境。诈骗网络往往横跨多国——呼叫中心设在某国、洗钱通道经手另一国、受害者遍布区域内外，单一国家的刑罚再重，也难以斩断整条链条。马来西亚的鞭刑动议，更像一面镜子，映出整个东南亚在数字经济时代面对跨境犯罪时的力不从心：法律边界追不上资金与数据的流动。

## 一场给数字时代的镜鉴

马来西亚的尝试并不孤例。当诈骗裹着技术的外衣、以更低成本、更广半径收割普通人，传统的罚款与监禁正在失灵。鞭刑能否成为那根"最后稻草"仍待观察，但有一点已清晰：在屏幕之后的犯罪，需要的不只是更重的刑罚，更是跨国的数据共治与源头打击。疼痛可以震慑个人，却堵不住流动的漏洞。

## 延伸阅读

**鞭刑（Caning）**：源自殖民法律的肉体刑罚，以藤条鞭打臀部，常见于马来西亚、新加坡、文莱等国的重罪量刑，具强烈羞辱与震慑意味。

**杀猪盘（Pig-butchering scam）**：以恋爱或投资为饵、长期"养熟"受害者后再卷款消失的诈骗模式，是东南亚跨境诈骗的典型形态。

**呼叫中心诈骗（Call-centre fraud）**：假借客服、公检法或金融机构名义远程行骗的团伙作案，常依托境外园区形成产业链。

In Kuala Lumpur's offices and rural towns alike, a punishment from the colonial era is being polished anew. As online scams moved from street corners into every phone screen, Malaysia's government has signaled it may extend caning—long kept as a "trump card"—to the fraudsters behind the screen.

## A Punishment "Sealed Away," Polished Anew

Caning is no stranger in Malaysia. Inherited from British colonial rule, it remains in the Penal Code, mostly for rape, drug trafficking and illegal entry. On the anti-fraud front, however, it was long "sealed away"—courts preferred fines and prison for economic crime. According to Lianhe Zaobao, Malaysian authorities recently stated they are considering caning as a sentencing option for online-fraud offenders, to deter a rampant scam epidemic with a harsher hand.

## Why Scams Forced Out an "Old" Tool

Digital fraud is spreading like wildfire across Southeast Asia. Police data show Malaysia lost about 4.3 billion ringgit (roughly US$900 million) to online scams in 2025, hitting victims from retirees to urban professionals. Tactics keep evolving—fake officials, impersonated relatives, investment "pig-butchering," stolen bank codes—often wired into cross-border "call centres" and laundering networks. When fines are merely a scammer's "cost of business" and prison terms stall on cross-border evidence, the government is turning to the most visceral penalty.

## Can the Cane Hit the Pain Point?

Supporters argue caning's shame and physical sting are a deterrence fines and short jail terms cannot match. Singapore is the precedent: the city-state has long caned offenders for robbery and vandalism, its low crime rate often credited to that "memory of pain." If Malaysia follows, it upgrades anti-fraud from a financial to a physical reckoning. But doubts are sharp: is caning excessive? Can it reach the masterminds hiding abroad? Caning foot soldiers may obscure the real disease—the cross-border industry chain.

## Singapore's Mirror, ASEAN's Dilemma

Singapore's record emboldens Malaysia yet exposes ASEAN's shared trap. Scam networks sprawl across borders—call centres in one country, laundering through another, victims everywhere. No single state's penalty, however harsh, severs the chain. Malaysia's caning proposal is a mirror reflecting the region's helplessness before cross-border crime in the digital age: legal boundaries lag behind the flow of money and data.

## A Lesson for the Digital Age

Malaysia's move is not alone. As scams hide in technology and harvest ordinary people at lower cost and wider reach, traditional fines and prison are failing. Whether the cane becomes the "last straw" remains to be seen, but one thing is clear: crime behind the screen needs more than harsher punishment—it needs cross-border data governance and source-level strikes. Pain deters individuals, but cannot plug the moving leaks.

## Further reading

**Caning**: A corporal punishment from colonial law, administered with a rattan cane on the buttocks, common in Malaysia, Singapore and Brunei for serious crimes; intensely shameful and deterrent.

**Pig-butchering scam**: A fraud that lures victims via romance or investment, "fattens" them over time, then vanishes with the money—a hallmark of cross-border scams in Southeast Asia.

**Call-centre fraud**: Syndicate scams impersonating customer service, police or banks remotely, often built into industrial parks abroad.
