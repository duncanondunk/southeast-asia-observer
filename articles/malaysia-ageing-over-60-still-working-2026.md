# 年过六十仍在岗：马来西亚老龄化的劳动力之问

2026年8月29日，新加坡《联合早报》（Lianhe Zaobao）援引马来西亚社险机构（PERKESO / 马来西亚社会保障机构）数据报道：截至2026年7月，马来西亚（Malaysia）年满60岁仍在缴纳社险的活跃就业者达43万3563人。法定退休年龄是60岁，而43万3563名长者仍在工薪名单之上，这一数字本身已经把政策划线与社会现实的落差，摆到了桌面上。吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）茨厂街一带的食档、社区杂货铺与加油站里，六旬以上仍在记账、打包、看店的身影并不鲜见。问题于是变成：他们继续工作，究竟是出于自主选择，还是养老金不足以支撑余生的无奈？

## 60岁的退休线，还框得住现实吗？

马来西亚的法定退休年龄为60岁。这条线来自《2012年最低退休年龄法令》，旨在为雇员提供可预期的退出时点，也为企业的人力更替提供节奏。然而法令挡不住需求：当家庭开支、医疗账单、子女教育或房贷仍在进行时，60岁并不意味着劳动义务的自动解除。

社险机构的数据显示，2023年60岁及以上的活跃在职缴纳者为41万5445人，到2026年7月增至43万3563人，两年多增加约1万8118人。增幅看似温和，却发生在劳动力年龄结构整体下移、青年就业竞争加剧的背景下。但社险数字只覆盖正规就业体系内被雇主登记、缴纳社险的劳动者；大量在统计边缘的家庭帮工、摊贩、宗教学校杂工、郊区农贸从业人员未被纳入。60岁的法定退休线因此更像一条行政边界，而不是经济生活的终点。

社险机构副首席执行员（策略及企业事务）锺百煌向英文《星报》指出，年满60岁不一定意味着停止工作；许多马来西亚人即便年过60岁，依然保持健康、高效，并继续为经济贡献技能和经验。

## 社险数字的增长，不只是长寿的故事

长寿当然是一个原因。医疗条件改善、营养水平提升，使得马来西亚人在60岁之后仍有相当体力和认知能力继续工作。2023年至2026年7月，这一群体的年均增幅不足1%，说明这并非短期劳动力短缺造成的一次性井喷，而是一种结构性慢涨。

然而，结构背后藏着两层压力。第一层是家庭财务：在社会保障覆盖不足、子女赡养功能弱化的城市家庭，长者继续工作是维持家庭现金流的重要方式。第二层是制度缺口：马来西亚的养老金体系以雇员公积金（EPF）和社险为核心，前者要求长期稳定缴费，后者依赖雇主登记。对于那些职业生涯断续、长期从事低薪或非正规就业的群体而言，退休账户余额往往低于维持基本生活所需的水平。2025年一项调查指出，接近半数的马来西亚55岁以上公积金会员，其账户余额低于5万令吉（约8万3500元人民币），在通胀与医疗支出的夹击下撑不过几年。

因此，43万3563人继续缴纳社险，既代表「仍能工作」，也折射「仍需工作」。

## 雇主-雇员二元框架的盲区

马来西亚社会保障制度的运行逻辑，长期围绕传统的雇主与雇员关系展开。雇员由企业代扣代缴公积金和社险，雇主承担部分缴费责任；自雇人士、平台从业者、临时帮工则处于制度外围。锺百煌的提醒切中要害：随着国民年龄增长，社会保障制度不能只着重于传统雇佣关系，也需日益关注他们继续谋生的不同方式。

在马来西亚，Grab、Foodpanda 等网约车与外卖平台的司机、送餐骑手、电商仓储分拣员中，年长者的比例并不低。他们或许有雇主，却不一定被视为「雇员」；他们或许有收入，却没有稳定的社保缴纳记录。当年龄跨过60岁，平台算法的派单偏好、保险覆盖的年龄上限、工伤赔偿的资格门槛都会悄然收紧。一位65岁的送餐骑手遇到交通事故时，他的保障来自哪里？

制度若仍以「雇主-雇员」作为唯一入口，大量老年劳动者就会被排斥在统计与保障之外。社险机构的数据只捕捉到了冰山浮在水面的部分；水面之下，是自雇与零工经济中更庞大的灰色老年劳动力。

## 养老金、家庭储蓄与外籍劳工的三角关系

马来西亚的养老金替代率长期处于中等偏低水平。以最低月薪1500令吉（约2505元人民币）计算，雇员公积金雇主缴纳12%、雇员缴纳11%，假设连续工作35年且工资不变，退休时账户余额可能仅相当于数年工资。若职业生涯曾中断、曾从事非正规工作，或曾在低薪岗位长期停留，实际余额会更少。

家庭储蓄本应成为补充，但马来西亚的家庭债务截至2026年3月底已相当于国内生产总值的84.4%，在东南亚（Southeast Asia）仅次于泰国，年长者在子女教育、住房贷款、医疗开销中往往已耗尽积蓄。于是，继续工作成为一种家庭财务策略，而非个人职业规划。

与此同时，马来西亚经济对外籍劳工的依赖又构成另一层张力。从建筑业、制造业到服务业，大量低薪岗位由印尼（Indonesia）、孟加拉国、尼泊尔和缅甸劳工填补。他们的存在压低了整体工资水平，也间接压缩了本地年长劳动者重返正规就业市场的空间。

## 邻国不会等待：新加坡与泰国的路径

比较视角有助于判断马来西亚所处的位置。新加坡没有设定统一的法定退休年龄，而是通过再就业至67岁的法定框架与中央公积金（CPF）制度，把年长员工留在岗位上，同时要求雇主提供再就业。政府还以渐进式工资模式补贴低薪年长工人的收入。结果是，新加坡65至69岁居民的劳动参与率超过四成，制度上把「继续工作」设计为可预期的老年生活阶段。

泰国（Thailand）的模式则不同。泰国已经步入老龄化社会，劳动力市场以非正规就业为主，社会保障覆盖率低。政府近年推动的「银色经济」与社区养老，更多依赖家庭网络和地方志愿组织，国家层面的收入替代功能有限。老年贫困与城乡差距因此成为泰国老龄化的突出问题。

马来西亚介于两者之间：它既不具备新加坡那样的财政实力和强制性再就业机制，也不像泰国那样完全依赖家庭。要在两者之间找到自己的路径，需要的是制度创新，而不是简单地宣布「欢迎老年人继续工作」。

## 「积极老龄化」：赋权话语与生存现实

「积极老龄化」（active ageing）是近年来马来西亚政策文件中的热词。它强调健康、参与和保障三重维度，倡导长者继续参与社会和经济活动。

但话语与现实之间有一道鸿沟。如果社会没有足够的灵活岗位、合理薪酬、职业培训和无障碍环境，「积极老龄化」很容易变成「不得不积极」。43万3563名60岁以上社险缴纳者的存在，既可以被解读为老龄人力资源的成功开发，也可以被理解为养老金体系尚未提供体面退出机制的证据。两种解读并不互斥，但政策制定者往往只庆祝前者，而回避后者。

真正的积极老龄化，应当让继续工作成为一种选择，而不是一种必须。它要求政策制定者同时回答两个问题：第一，如何让想要工作的长者获得安全、体面、有保障的岗位；第二，如何让不想或不能工作的长者，依靠制度获得足以维持尊严的收入。只回答第一个问题，是回避第二个问题。

## 当老龄化社会进入倒计时

马来西亚统计局预测，马来西亚将在2036年成为老龄化国家，届时60岁及以上人口占总人口超过15%。目前60岁及以上人口估计为420万人，占全国3440万人口的12.3%。从12.3%到15%，中间只有十年。这意味着劳动力市场、社保体系、城市规划、医疗资源配置都需要在相对短的时间内完成调整。

43万3563名60岁以上仍在缴纳社险的劳动者，是这场调整的一个缩影。他们提醒我们：老龄化不是遥远的2036，而是已经发生、正在加速的日常现实。退休年龄可以依法划定，但劳动与生活的真实节奏不会因此静止。马来西亚要做的，不是用一句「年龄只是数字」轻描淡写，而是让制度追上数字背后的人口真相。有三项改革比呼吁更有用：一是让保障权益可以跨雇主、乃至转入自雇后继续携带；二是让缴费型制度真正触及零工与平台就业；三是让再就业规定的效力延伸到正规部门之外。马来西亚距离15%这一门槛大约还有十年。检验的标准是，这十年换来的是制度重构，还是又一轮关于「优雅老去」的漂亮话。

## 延伸阅读

- **雇员公积金（Employees Provident Fund, EPF）**：马来西亚强制性退休储蓄计划，雇主与雇员按月缴费，是多数受雇者退休收入的主要来源。
- **老龄化国家（Ageing Nation）**：按照联合国标准，65岁及以上人口占比超过7%（或60岁及以上超过10%）即可称为老龄化社会；马来西亚统计局采用60岁及以上超过15%作为本国老龄化国家门槛。
- **积极老龄化（Active Ageing）**：世界卫生组织提出的政策框架，强调通过健康、参与和保障，延长老年人的社会与经济参与年限。

# Still on the Clock: Malaysia’s Ageing Workforce and the Sixty-Year Line

On 29 August 2026, Singapore’s *Lianhe Zaobao* cited data from Malaysia’s Social Security Organisation (PERKESO/Socso): as of July 2026, 433,563 Malaysians aged 60 and above were still active contributors to the social-insurance scheme. The statutory retirement age is 60, yet 433,563 older workers remain on the payroll. That single figure captures the tension between policy and lived reality. In Kuala Lumpur’s Petaling Street and in sundry shops and petrol stations across the country, people past sixty are still tallying bills, packing goods and minding storefronts. The question is whether they keep working by choice, or because their pensions cannot carry them through the rest of their lives.

## Does the age-60 line still describe the labour market?

Malaysia’s statutory retirement age is 60, set by the Minimum Retirement Age Act 2012. The law gives employees a predictable exit point and gives employers a timetable for workforce renewal. But it cannot override household economics: when medical bills, children’s schooling, mortgages or everyday expenses persist, turning sixty does not automatically end the need to earn.

Socso’s figures show that active contributors aged 60 and above rose from 415,445 in 2023 to 433,563 in July 2026, an increase of about 18,118 over roughly two-and-a-half years. The rise is modest in percentage terms, but it comes at a time when younger workers face their own employment pressures. More importantly, Socso’s numbers cover only formal employees registered and counted by the state. They exclude family helpers, market vendors, religious-school caretakers and rural trading hands who fall outside the formal net. The age-60 line is therefore an administrative boundary more than an economic endpoint.

Edmund Cheong Peck Huang, Socso’s deputy chief executive (strategy and corporate), told *The Star* that turning 60 does not have to mean stopping work; many Malaysians remain healthy, productive and able to contribute skills and experience beyond that age.

## The rise in contributors is not only a longevity story

Longevity is part of the explanation. Better health care and nutrition mean that many Malaysians still have the physical and cognitive capacity to work after sixty. The annual increase in older contributors is below one per cent, so this is not a one-off surge caused by temporary labour shortages; it is a slow, structural climb.

Still, the trend reflects two deeper pressures. The first is household finance. In households where social protection is thin and inter-generational support is weakening, older workers remain a crucial source of cash flow. The second is institutional gaps. Malaysia’s retirement pillars — the Employees Provident Fund (EPF) and Socso — assume long, stable, formally registered employment. Those with interrupted careers, low wages or years in informal work often retire with balances too small to sustain basic living. By one estimate, roughly half of Malaysian EPF members aged 55 and above have balances below 50,000 ringgit (about $12,400), which does not last long once health costs and inflation are factored in.

So the 433,563 older contributors represent both the ability to work and the need to work.

## The blind spot of the employer–employee model

Malaysia’s social-protection architecture has long revolved around the conventional employer–employee relationship. The employer deducts and remits EPF and Socso contributions; the worker receives coverage. The self-employed, platform workers and casual helpers sit at the margins. Edmund Cheong Peck Huang’s warning is apt: as the population ages, the system cannot focus only on traditional employment; it must also pay attention to the different ways people continue to earn a living.

In Malaysia, platform companies such as Grab and Foodpanda engage large numbers of drivers, delivery riders and warehouse sorters, and older workers are not absent from these ranks. Yet many are classified as partners or contractors rather than employees. They may earn an income, but they do not necessarily have a steady contribution record. Once they cross sixty, algorithmic dispatch preferences, insurance age limits and accident-compensation thresholds quietly tighten. If a 65-year-old delivery rider is injured in a road accident, what protection does he have?

If the system continues to use the employer–employee relationship as its only gateway, large numbers of older workers will remain invisible to both statistics and protection. Socso’s 433,563 is the visible tip; beneath the surface lies a much larger grey labour force in self-employment and gig work.

## Pensions, household savings and the foreign-labour triangle

Malaysia’s pension replacement rate is modest by regional standards. At the minimum monthly wage of 1,500 ringgit (about $372), with employer contributions of 12 per cent and employee contributions of 11 per cent, an uninterrupted thirty-five-year career would yield a balance equivalent to only a few years of wages. Anyone with career breaks, spells of informal work or long periods in low-wage jobs would end up with far less.

Household savings should provide a buffer, but Malaysian household debt stood at 84.4 per cent of GDP at the end of March 2026, second in Southeast Asia only to Thailand. Many older workers have already depleted their savings on children’s education, housing and medical costs. Continuing to work therefore becomes a household financial strategy rather than a personal career plan.

At the same time, Malaysia’s economy depends heavily on foreign workers from Indonesia, Bangladesh, Nepal and Myanmar to fill low-wage jobs in construction, manufacturing and services. Their presence suppresses overall wage levels and narrows the formal re-entry opportunities for older local workers.

## Neighbours are not waiting: Singapore and Thailand

A comparative view clarifies where Malaysia stands. Singapore has no single statutory retirement age; instead, it uses a re-employment framework up to age 67 and the Central Provident Fund to keep older workers attached to the labour market. Employers must offer re-employment, and the government supplements the incomes of low-wage older workers through a progressive wage model. The result is a labour-force participation rate above 40 per cent among residents aged 65 to 69, with longer working lives treated as a planned stage of life.

Thailand follows a different path. Already an ageing society, it has a large informal labour market and limited social-security coverage. Government initiatives under the "silver economy" and community-based elderly care rely heavily on family networks and local volunteers; national income-replacement is weak. Elderly poverty and rural–urban disparity are therefore prominent features of Thailand’s ageing challenge.

Malaysia sits between the two. It has a more developed provident-fund system than Thailand, but it also lacks Singapore’s fiscal muscle and compulsory re-employment rules. The right response is institutional innovation, not a simple declaration that older workers are welcome to stay longer.

## "Active ageing": empowerment or dressed-up necessity?

"Active ageing" has become a favourite term in Malaysian policy documents. It stresses health, participation and security, and encourages older people to remain engaged in social and economic life.

But there is a gap between discourse and reality. Without flexible jobs, fair pay, retraining and age-friendly workplaces, "active ageing" can easily become "compulsory activity." The 433,563 contributors aged 60 and above can be read as evidence of successful older-worker mobilisation, or as proof that the pension system still fails to provide a decent exit. The two readings are not mutually exclusive, yet policy makers tend to celebrate the first while ignoring the second.

Genuine active ageing would make continued work a choice, not a requirement. It would ask two questions together: how to give those who want to work safe, dignified and protected jobs, and how to give those who cannot or do not want to work an income sufficient for a decent life. Answering only the first is a way of evading the second.

## The countdown to an ageing nation

Malaysia’s Department of Statistics projects that Malaysia will become an ageing nation in 2036, when people aged 60 and above exceed 15 per cent of the total population. At present, the 60-and-over population is estimated at 4.2 million, or 12.3 per cent of the country’s 34.4 million people. The jump from 12.3 per cent to 15 per cent leaves only about a decade to adjust labour-market institutions, social-security design, urban planning and health-care capacity.

The 433,563 older workers still contributing to Socso are a preview of that adjustment. They remind us that ageing is not a distant event in 2036; it is already unfolding in everyday life. The retirement age can be fixed by law, but the real rhythm of work and survival does not stop at the statutory line. What Malaysia needs is not a slogan that age is just a number, but institutions that catch up with the demographic truth behind the number. Three pieces would do more than exhortation: benefits that stay portable across employers and into self-employment, a contributory scheme that actually reaches gig and platform work, and re-employment rules with force beyond the formal sector. Malaysia has roughly a decade before the 15 per cent threshold arrives. The test is whether that decade buys institutional redesign, or only a great deal more talk about ageing gracefully.

## Further reading

- **Employees Provident Fund (EPF)**: Malaysia’s mandatory retirement-savings scheme, financed by monthly contributions from employers and employees, and the main income source for most formal-sector retirees.
- **Ageing nation**: The United Nations conventionally classifies a society as ageing when people aged 65 and above exceed 7 per cent of the population, or when those aged 60 and above exceed 10 per cent; Malaysia’s Department of Statistics uses a national threshold of 15 per cent aged 60 and above.
- **Active ageing**: The World Health Organisation framework that promotes health, participation and security to extend older people’s social and economic engagement.
