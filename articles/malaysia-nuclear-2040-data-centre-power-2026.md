# 2% 的核电填不上 21% 的数据中心：马来西亚把核能写进 2040 年的账本

## 一份书面答复里的两条曲线

据《南洋商报》报道，马来西亚能源及水务转型部 11 月 26 日在国会书面答复议员提问时，摊开了一份直到 2050 年的电力账本：半岛电力需求将从 2027 年的 22.6 吉瓦增至 2050 年的 49.8 吉瓦，复合年增长率约 3.5%；数据中心占半岛总用电的比重，将从 2027 年的 11% 升至 2050 年的 21%。同一份答复还写着一个更抓眼球的数字：政府计划从 2040 年起分阶段引入核能，到 2050 年形成约 2 吉瓦规模，约占发电结构的 2%。

把这几组数字并排放，结论已经写在纸面上。一条曲线在翻倍，另一条从零起步、要等到 2030 年代的后半段才登场。据马新社报道，能源部在答复中把核能与天然气并列为取代煤炭、承担基载发电的策略选项，但真正装机多少，仍要等马来西亚电力机构主导的可行性研究。换句话说，核电在这份文件里首先是一个被预留的位置，而不是一份已经兑现的答案。

## 2% 的口径，比 2% 的数字更值得追问

2% 究竟是什么的 2%？按装机算，2 吉瓦放在 49.8 吉瓦的需求盘子里大约是 4%；按发电量算，核电机组利用率远高于光伏，实际贡献还会更高。官方文本写的是「发电占比约 2%」，同时又说「约 2 吉瓦」，两个口径之间的缝隙没有填平。这不是吹毛求疵：能源规划里，装机、发电量、可调度容量是三笔不同的账，混着写，最后吃亏的是掏电费的那一方。

更要紧的是量级。数据中心到 2050 年要吃掉 21% 的电，核能届时贡献约 2%。就算把核电全额算作新增的清洁基载，也填不上数据中心新增需求的一个零头。把这两件事放在同一份答复里宣布，容易制造一种「问题已经有解法」的印象；实际上，剩下那十几个百分点靠什么发出来，答复并没有说清。真正需要公开辩论的，恰恰是这段被 2% 轻轻带过的空白。

## 资本先到，电网后到

马来西亚这几年一直想把柔佛推成区域数据中心枢纽，甲骨文、亚马逊、谷歌陆续落子；其中谷歌宣布未来两年投入 150 亿美元（约 1080 亿元人民币）扩建数据中心与云业务。资本按下的是快进键：一座机房从签约到通电，按年计；一条输电线路、一座变电站，按十年计；一座核电站从可行性研究到并网，按二十年计。三条时间轴叠在一起，2040 年这个起点就不显得遥远，反而显得过于乐观。

本月马来西亚还宣布，将为数据中心等大型用电户单独开设批发电力市场，理由是把居民和工厂的供电安全隔离开来。这一步其实比核电表态更值得关注：它承认了大用户与普通用户之间存在真实的成本冲突，并选择用市场分层来隔离冲突。隔离不是消灭。当电价、水价和土地价格各自往上走，谁来承担「枢纽」这个称号的账单，迟早会回到选民手里。

## 柔佛的水账：1.05% 是一条什么线

据《中国报》报道，柔佛州务大臣翁哈菲兹 11 月 27 日在州议会总结 2027 年财政预算案辩论时说，全州已有 19 座数据中心投入运作，业者原本申请每日 7300 万公升净水，最终获批 2100 万公升；全州平均每日净水产量为 20 亿 3400 万公升，供给数据中心的水量占 1.05%。他同时强调，州政府不会为了吸引投资牺牲人民福祉，水与电是绝不能妥协的底线。

1.05% 是个精确数字，但精确的是审批口径，不是社会承受的上限。把申请量砍掉七成，说明州政府确实在算水账；可当数据中心继续增加，分母不动、分子往上走，1.05% 这条线是继续守，还是随投资规模重画？核电解决的是电的问题，柔佛眼下更紧的是水。数据中心是「用电大户」，也是「用水大户」，而核电机组本身同样需要大量冷却水。把两笔账分开算，规划才算得漂亮；推进起来，它们在同一片土地上抢的是同一种资源。

## 核能不是新话题，是一份旧档案重启

马来西亚讨论核电已经不是第一次。据行业回顾，早在 2009 年，政府就曾设想 2030 年让核电占到全国供电的近 18%；2011 年福岛事故后计划放缓；2018 年政权更迭，时任首相马哈迪以核废料处理没有成熟方案为由，解散了马来西亚核能机构。此后多年，核能留在文件里，不留在电网里。

转向发生在最近几年。2024 年，安华在国会提呈第十三大马计划时明确提到探讨核能作为替代方案；2025 年，副首相兼能源及水务转型部长法迪拉宣布委任马来西亚电力机构担任核能计划执行机构，按国际原子能机构的「核能发展里程碑方法」补制度、补法律、补监管、补公众沟通。同年 11 月，科学、工艺及革新部长郑立慷回应质疑说，探讨核能并非临时起意，而是从 2008 年延续至今的议题。这段话是可信的：马来西亚确实讨论了很多年。问题是，讨论的年数和建成的机组数之间，从来不成正比。

## 监管要先于反应堆落地

真正决定核电能不能建成的，不是反应堆型号，而是监管能不能先把话说硬。核能计划执行机构的四项任务——基础设施、监管框架、公众参与、国际标准——没有一项是工程问题，全部是制度问题。法迪拉把原则概括为安全、透明与永续，并提到要提升原子能执照局的能力、通过国际合作培养人才。这些都是正确的顺序：先有能说不的监管者，再有能开工的业主。

核废料是这套顺序的试金石。郑立慷此前表示，只有合作方愿意负责回收乏燃料，马来西亚才会考虑合作，并点名俄罗斯、中国、法国等国表达过意愿。这个条件看似把风险外包，实际上把长期责任写进了合同谈判；能否落实，要看条款而不是表态。区域经验也不乐观：周边国家里，公众对核电的接受度差异很大，新加坡约 31.4% 的受访者接受核能，居东盟之首，却也没有因此开工。接受度不是科研结论，是信任存量，而信任无法从邻国进口。

## 光伏账本里的另一道算术

同一场国会答复还给出另一组值得对照的数字：从大规模太阳能计划第一期到第五期+，登嘉楼、吉兰丹、玻璃市三州共获批 25 个项目、1531 兆瓦，占全国该计划 6028 兆瓦总容量的 25.4%。对企业可再生能源供应计划，能源部重申「愿意买、愿意卖」的原则，并强调电网使用应缴纳系统接入费，避免成本被不公平地转嫁给其他用户。

这道算术与核电并不矛盾，反而互为补丁。光伏解决的是白天与容量，核电解决的是夜间与基载；太阳能每吉瓦占地远大于核电，核电每千瓦的前期资本开支又远高于光伏。真正的问题不是选谁，而是谁付钱。接入费那一句「不让成本转嫁给其他用户」，放在核电语境里同样成立：如果大型数据中心拿到了专属市场与专属电源，普通家庭和中小工厂的账单上就不该出现隐含交叉补贴。这条原则写进了可再生能源，就该写进核电。

## 2040 之前那一段，谁来兜底

按官方口径，煤炭作为基载的角色预计到 2035 年大幅下降，核电却要到 2040 年才分阶段进入。中间那五年，加上从现在到 2035 年的十年以上，靠什么顶住翻倍的需求？答案是天然气、进口燃料和更多可再生能源，以及把大用户单独放进批发市场。也就是说，核电被摆在一条需求曲线的末端，用来回应一个从今天就开始的问题。

这不是说核电不该研究。对一个进口燃料依赖高、又承诺 2050 年净零排放的半岛电网而言，保留基载选项本是常识。但该说的是：把 2040 年写进政策，等于承认此前的问题另有答案，而那个答案目前既不完整，也没有经过同等强度的社会讨论。天然气价格波动、输气通道安全、储备成本，每一项都比一座尚未立项的核电站更紧迫。把远期选项当成近期安慰，是能源政治里最省事、也最昂贵的做法。

## 延伸阅读

**基载电力（Baseload power）**：指满足最低持续负荷的发电能力，要求机组可长时间稳定运行；煤电退出后由谁接棒，是半岛电网的核心问题。

**核能发展里程碑方法（Milestones Approach）**：国际原子能机构提出的核电项目分阶段框架，覆盖法律、监管、基础设施与公众沟通，强调先建制度再建机组。

**系统接入费（System access charge）**：大型用户或分布式电源使用公共电网时承担的成本分摊机制，目的是避免成本转嫁给其他用户。

# Two percent of nuclear cannot power 21 percent of demand: Malaysia's 2040 ledger

## Two curves in a written parliamentary reply

According to Nanyang Siang Pau, Malaysia's Ministry of Energy Transition and Water Transformation told Parliament in a written reply on 26 November that electricity demand in Peninsular Malaysia will rise from 22.6 gigawatts in 2027 to 49.8 gigawatts by 2050, a compound annual growth rate of about 3.5 per cent. Data centres' share of that demand is projected to climb from 11 per cent in 2027 to 21 per cent in 2050. The same reply offered a more eye-catching number: the government plans to introduce nuclear power in phases from 2040, reaching roughly 2 GW by 2050, or about 2 per cent of the generation mix.

Put side by side, the numbers tell their own story. One curve doubles; the other starts from zero and only arrives in the second half of the 2030s. Bernama reports that the ministry identified nuclear and gas as strategic options to replace coal for baseload generation, while stressing that the actual capacity depends on a comprehensive feasibility study led by MyPOWER Corporation. Nuclear, in other words, is first a reserved slot in a planning document, not a delivered answer.

## What exactly is two percent of?

Is 2 per cent a share of capacity, of generation, or of dispatchable output? Two gigawatts against a 49.8 GW demand pool is roughly 4 per cent by capacity; measured by generation, nuclear's high load factor would lift its contribution further. The official wording says "about 2 per cent of the generation mix" while also citing "about 2.0 GW", leaving the gap between the two metrics unfilled. This is not pedantry. Capacity, generation and firm capacity are three different ledgers, and the person who eventually pays the tariff is the one who absorbs the confusion.

The more serious issue is scale. Data centres are forecast to consume 21 per cent of electricity by 2050; nuclear is expected to supply about 2 per cent. Even if every nuclear kilowatt-hour were counted as new clean baseload, it would not cover a fraction of the additional demand from data centres. Announcing both figures in one reply creates an impression that the problem has a solution. It does not. Where the remaining double-digit percentage points come from is left unsaid, and that blank space — smoothed over by the phrase "2 per cent" — is precisely what deserves public argument.

## Capital arrives first; the grid arrives later

Malaysia has spent years trying to make Johor a regional data-centre hub, with Oracle, Amazon and Google committing capital. Google alone announced a US$15 billion investment over two years to expand its data centres and cloud business there. Capital runs on a clock measured in years: a facility can be signed, built and energised quickly. Transmission lines and substations run on a decade-long clock. A nuclear plant, from feasibility study to grid connection, runs on a twenty-year clock. Stack the three timelines and 2040 stops looking distant; it starts looking optimistic.

This month Malaysia also announced a separate wholesale electricity market for large users such as data centres, explicitly to protect household and industrial supply. That move deserves more attention than the nuclear statement. It concedes that large users and ordinary consumers have a real conflict of cost, and chooses market segregation as the tool. Segregation is not elimination. As power, water and land prices climb, the bill for being a "hub" will eventually return to the ballot box.

## Johor's water ledger: what kind of line is 1.05 percent?

According to Sin Chew Daily, Johor Menteri Besar Onn Hafiz told the state assembly on 27 November, while winding up the 2027 budget debate, that 19 data centres were already operating in the state. Operators had applied for 73 million litres of treated water a day and were approved 21 million. The state produces about 2.034 billion litres a day, so data centres account for 1.05 per cent of output. He pledged that the state would not sacrifice public welfare for investment, calling water and electricity non-negotiable red lines.

One point zero five per cent is a precise figure, but what it measures is an approval ceiling, not society's tolerance. Cutting the requested volume by roughly seven tenths shows the state is doing the arithmetic. Yet as more centres arrive, with the denominator fixed and the numerator rising, will that line hold — or be redrawn to fit the investment pipeline? Nuclear addresses electricity; Johor's tighter constraint today is water. Data centres are large consumers of both, and nuclear plants need substantial cooling water as well. The two ledgers can be kept apart on a planning slide. On the ground they compete for the same resource.

## Nuclear is not a new topic; it is an old file reopened

This is not Malaysia's first nuclear debate. Industry retrospectives recall that as early as 2009 the government envisioned nuclear supplying nearly 18 per cent of national electricity by 2030. The plan slowed after Fukushima in 2011, and in 2018 then-prime minister Mahathir Mohamad dissolved the Malaysian Nuclear Power Corporation, citing the absence of a mature solution for nuclear waste. For years afterwards, nuclear survived in documents rather than in the grid.

The turn came recently. In 2024 Anwar Ibrahim explicitly mentioned exploring nuclear as an alternative when tabling the Thirteenth Malaysia Plan. In 2025 Deputy Prime Minister and Energy Minister Fadillah Yusof appointed MyPOWER as the Nuclear Energy Programme Implementing Organisation, tasked with strengthening institutions, law, regulation and public engagement under the IAEA's Milestones Approach. That November, Science and Technology Minister Chang Lih Kang insisted the exploration was not impromptu but an issue tracked since 2008. He is probably right. Malaysia has discussed nuclear for many years. The trouble is that years of discussion and reactors built have never been proportional.

## Regulation must arrive before the reactor

What determines whether a nuclear plant gets built is less the reactor model than whether the regulator can say no with authority. NEPIO's four tasks — infrastructure, regulatory framework, public engagement and international standards — are not engineering problems; they are institutional ones. Fadillah framed the principles as safety, transparency and sustainability, and pledged to strengthen the Atomic Energy Licensing Board and train specialists through international cooperation. That is the right order: a regulator able to refuse before an owner able to build.

Spent fuel tests that order. Chang has said Malaysia would consider cooperation only if the partner takes responsibility for recycling spent fuel, naming Russia, China and France as countries that had expressed willingness. The condition looks like an outsourcing of risk, but it actually writes long-term liability into contract negotiation. Whether it survives contact with a signed deal remains to be seen. Regional experience is not reassuring: in a nearby survey, about 31.4 per cent of Singaporean respondents accepted nuclear power, the highest share in ASEAN, and no reactor followed. Acceptance is not a research finding; it is a stock of trust, and trust cannot be imported from a neighbour.

## The other arithmetic in the solar ledger

The same parliamentary reply contained a useful counterpoint. Across large-scale solar programmes LSS 1 through LSS 5+, Terengganu, Kelantan and Perlis secured 25 projects totalling 1,531 MW, or 25.4 per cent of the national LSS capacity of 6,028 MW. For the Corporate Renewable Energy Supply Scheme, the ministry reiterated a "willing buyer, willing seller" principle and said grid use should attract appropriate system access charges so that costs are not unfairly transferred to other electricity users.

That arithmetic does not compete with nuclear; it patches the same hole from another side. Solar supplies daylight and capacity, nuclear supplies night-time and baseload. Per gigawatt, solar needs far more land; per kilowatt installed, nuclear demands far more upfront capital. The real question is who pays. The line about not shifting costs onto other users, written for renewable energy, should apply equally to nuclear. If large data centres receive a dedicated market and a dedicated power source, cross-subsidies should not quietly appear on household and small-factory bills. A principle good enough for solar is good enough for reactors.

## Who carries the gap before 2040?

Coal's baseload role is expected to fall sharply by 2035, while nuclear enters only in phases from 2040. What carries demand through those five years — and through the longer stretch from today to 2035? The answer is gas, imported fuel, more renewables, and a separate wholesale market for large users. Nuclear is placed at the end of a demand curve meant to answer a problem that began today.

None of this argues against research. For a peninsula heavily dependent on imported fuel and committed to net-zero emissions by 2050, keeping a baseload option open is common sense. But writing 2040 into policy is an admission that the earlier gap has another answer, and that answer is neither complete nor subjected to the same intensity of public scrutiny. Gas price volatility, route security and reserve costs are all more urgent than a plant that has not been approved. Treating a distant option as immediate comfort is among the cheapest tricks — and the most expensive habits — in energy politics.

## Further reading

**Baseload power**: The minimum level of demand that must be met continuously, requiring plant that can run steadily for long periods; who replaces coal as baseload is the peninsula's central grid question.

**Milestones Approach**: The IAEA's phased framework for nuclear power programmes, covering legal, regulatory, infrastructure and public-communication prerequisites before any reactor is ordered.

**System access charge**: A cost-allocation mechanism charged to large users or distributed generators for using the public grid, intended to prevent costs from being shifted onto other consumers.
