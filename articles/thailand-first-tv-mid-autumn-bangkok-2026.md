# 泰国首届电视中秋晚会登陆曼谷：当团圆夜走上主流荧屏

9月13日晚，曼谷泰国第五电视台的剧场里亮起中秋的灯。由泰国第五电视台、泰国中华总商会与泰国潮州会馆联合主办，泰国首届电视中秋晚会在这座拥有庞大华侨华人群体的城市录制完成，并定于9月25日中秋之夜在第五台播出。表面看，这不过是一台应景的文化晚会；但若把它放进泰国华社数十年的文化脉络里，你会发现，这是华侨华人的传统节日第一次真正意义上走上泰国的国家主流荧屏。本文想说清楚一件事：这台晚会不是孤立的文艺活动，而是中华文化在东南亚落地、生根、并与在地社会相互交融的一个切片。

## 一台晚会的象征意义

一台中秋晚会被搬上“电视”，意义远不止多了一个播出渠道。在泰国，中秋节长期是华侨华人社区内部的节俗——在华人学校、宗亲会馆、同乡社团里张灯、赏月、吃月饼。它温暖，却也相对封闭。如今，这台晚会由泰国第五电视台这样的全国性媒体承制并播出，意味着中秋叙事第一次被放进泰语主流受众的客厅。据中国侨网报道，晚会还特意由中泰两国主持人共同主持，节目里融入泰国歌舞。换句话说，主办者从一开始就不只想“自娱自乐”，而是要借国家电视台的公共性，把“团圆”“思乡”这些华人情感，翻译成泰国社会也能共情的语言。这一步看似温和，却恰恰是文化软实力的题中应有之义：不是强行输出，而是让对方愿意坐下看。

## 谁在搭台：侨社与主流媒体的合流

值得玩味的，是这台晚会的“搭台者”组合。主办方里既有泰国中华总商会、泰国潮州会馆这类深耕泰国社会数十年的华侨华人组织，也有泰国第五电视台这样的国家主流媒体。中华总商会的代主席李桂雄在致辞里说，中秋寄托着对团圆、和谐与美好生活的向往，希望通过这台晚会“让更多泰国朋友感受中华传统文化的独特魅力”。注意这里的措辞——重点不是“我们华侨自己过节”，而是“让泰国朋友感受”。这背后是一种成熟的侨社自觉：华人文化要在这个国家长期存续，就不能只停留在族裔内部，必须主动进入公共空间。第五台的加入，则给了这种自觉一个放大器。侨社提供内容与人脉，主流媒体提供渠道与合法性，二者合流，才把一场社区节庆变成了一次国家级的文化呈现。

## 潮籍底色：泰国华社的方言地图

细看主办与演出阵容，你会发现一个鲜明的底色：潮籍（潮州）力量贯穿始终。泰国潮州会馆是主办方之一，担纲演出的泰中文化人联合会同样是华人文化团体。这并不偶然。泰国华侨华人中，潮籍裔占绝对多数，从早期“下南洋”的移民潮到今天曼谷的街市与商行，潮汕方言与饮食早已渗入泰国日常。据中国侨网报道，泰国前副总理功·塔帕朗西在开幕致辞中主动提及，自己的祖上来自中国广东澄海——澄海正是潮汕重镇。一个有意思的观察是：在东南亚，华社内部的“方言地图”往往比国境线更清晰，而在泰国，这张地图的主色，正是潮州红。理解这一点，才能理解为何这台中秋晚会从策划到演出，都带着浓厚的潮汕文化气质。

## 建交51年与“泰中一家亲”

晚会的时间点也耐人寻味。2026年适逢泰中建交51周年。功·塔帕朗西在致辞中把中秋节与这个节点并置，称半个多世纪以来两国“携手同行”，“泰中一家亲”的情谊历久弥新。把一台华人节庆晚会，安放在两国关系的周年叙事里，是一种典型而有效的文化外交手法：它不谈利益、不列条款，只用“团圆”“一家亲”这种最朴素的人情，去润滑国家关系的宏大叙事。对于中国而言，泰国是东盟里对华最友好的国家之一；对于泰国而言，借华社的文化活动向北京释放善意，成本低、观感好。中秋晚会因此既是情感的，也是政治的——只是它把政治裹在了月饼和歌声里，让人乐于接受。

## 双重身份的范本：从政要到艺人

泰国华人最值得研究的，是他们“双重身份”的从容。功·塔帕朗西是前副总理，却坦然以“祖上来自澄海”自况；泰国中华总商会、潮州会馆的领袖，既是泰国商界巨擘，也是华社精神纽带。这种身份不冲突、不遮掩，反而互为加持。对比东南亚另一些国家曾出现的族群张力，泰国提供了一个相对顺滑的范本：华人不需要在“做泰国人”和“做华人”之间二选一。晚会上，中泰主持人同台、泰中文联的演员以中国歌舞亮相主流电视台，正是这种从容的具象化。它说明，当一套文化符号能被国家高层与民间共同认领，离散群体的身份焦虑就会大幅稀释。

## 从会馆到荧屏：文化记忆的代际传递

把镜头拉远，这台晚会触及一个更现实的命题：文化记忆怎么传给年轻人。泰国的新生代华人里，许多人已不会讲华语，与宗亲会馆的情感连接也在变淡。过去，中秋的记忆靠的是长辈在会馆里张罗、孩子跟着凑热闹；如今，这样的现场在萎缩。把晚会搬上第五台，等于用一种年轻人熟悉的媒介——电视与晚会形式——去重新包裹传统。策划人吴小菡说，晚会主打“团圆”“思乡”与“唯美”，意图正是“把中秋节文化推向泰国社会”。这未必能让年轻人立刻去读唐诗，但至少，它让“中秋”作为一个文化符号，继续活在公共视野里。文化记忆的延续，往往不靠说教，而靠一次次被看见。

## 双向融合，而非单向输出

需要澄清的是，这台晚会不是中华文化的单向倾销。据中国侨网报道，节目除中国歌舞、器乐外，还特别融合了泰国本土的歌舞元素，呈现“中泰文化相通、人心相通”的场景。也就是说，它被设计成一场双向的对话：华人演绎自己的传统，也邀请泰国观众的文化符号同台。这种“你中有我”的编排，恰恰呼应了“泰中一家亲”的主线。对一个多元社会而言，外来文化若想长久驻留，必须学会与在地文化共舞，而非凌驾其上。泰国华社显然深谙此道——这也是为何在泰国，中华文化既能保持本色，又不显得突兀。

## 柔性叙事，比硬性推广更稳

最后想落到一点判断：在东南亚推动文化交流，柔性的、由在地侨社自发组织的叙事，往往比自上而下的硬性推广更稳、更久。近年来，一些国家对中国文化机构持审慎态度，而泰国这台中秋晚会几乎听不到杂音，原因正在于它的“出身”——由本地扎根数十年的中华总商会、潮州会馆牵头，演员是本地华人，观众是泰国大众。它不是从外部空降的，而是从泰国社会内部长出来的。这样的文化呈现，自带合法性与亲和力。对于关心中华文化走出去的人，泰国样本值得记一笔：真正的落地，从来不是把节目送出去，而是让当地人愿意把它当成自己的事来办。

## 延伸阅读

**泰国中华总商会（The Thai-Chinese Chamber of Commerce）**：成立于20世纪初的泰国华商联合组织，长期扮演泰中经贸与民间交往的桥梁，是泰国华社最具影响力的机构之一。

**泰中一家亲（Thai-Chinese "as one family"）**：泰中关系中最常用的温情表述，强调两国地缘相近、血缘相亲、文化相通，常被用于高层与民间交往的叙事中。

**潮籍移民（Teochew diaspora in Thailand）**：指祖籍广东潮汕地区的泰国华侨华人；潮籍裔在泰国华社中占多数，深刻影响了泰国的商业、饮食与方言生态。

# Thailand's First Televised Mid-Autumn Gala in Bangkok: When Reunion Night Reaches Prime Time

On the evening of 13 September, the theatre of Thailand's Channel 5 in Bangkok lit up for the Mid-Autumn Festival. Co-hosted by Channel 5, the Thai-Chinese Chamber of Commerce and the Teochew Association of Thailand, the country's first televised Mid-Autumn gala was filmed in a city with a very large overseas-Chinese population, and is scheduled to air on Channel 5 on 25 September, Mid-Autumn night. On the surface this is simply a seasonal variety show. But placed against decades of Thai-Chinese cultural life, it is the first time an overseas-Chinese festival has genuinely reached Thailand's national prime-time screen. The point of this article is straightforward: the gala is not an isolated performance, but a snapshot of how Chinese culture lands, takes root and blends into local society in Southeast Asia.

## Why a televised gala matters

Putting a Mid-Autumn gala "on television" means far more than adding a broadcast channel. In Thailand, the festival has long been an intra-community custom of the overseas Chinese — lanterns, moon-viewing and mooncakes inside Chinese schools, clan halls and hometown associations. Warm, but relatively enclosed. Now, produced and aired by a national broadcaster like Channel 5, the Mid-Autumn narrative enters the Thai-language living rooms of the mainstream public for the first time. According to China Qiaowang, the show was co-hosted by Chinese and Thai presenters and folded Thai songs and dance into the programme. In other words, the organisers never intended mere self-entertainment; they used the publicness of national television to translate "reunion" and "homesickness" — Chinese emotions — into a language Thai society can share. Gentle as it looks, this is exactly what cultural soft power should be: not forced export, but getting the other side to sit down and watch.

## Who built the stage: diaspora groups meet national television

What is interesting is the mix of those who "built the stage." Among the organisers are institutions with decades of roots in Thai society — the Thai-Chinese Chamber of Commerce and the Teochew Association — alongside a national mainstream outlet, Channel 5. Li Guixiong, acting chair of the Chamber, said in his remarks that the festival carries hopes for reunion, harmony and a good life, and that the gala should let "more Thai friends feel the unique charm of traditional Chinese culture." Note the wording: the aim is not "we overseas Chinese celebrate alone," but "let Thai friends feel." Behind it lies a mature diaspora instinct: for Chinese culture to endure in this country, it cannot stay inside the ethnic fold; it must enter public space. Channel 5 supplies the amplifier. The associations provide content and networks; mainstream media provides reach and legitimacy. Only together do they turn a community festival into a national cultural event.

## A Teochew hue: the dialect map of Thai-Chinese society

Look closely at the organisers and performers and a clear colour emerges: the Teochew thread runs through everything. The Teochew Association of Thailand is a co-host, and the performing Thai-Chinese Cultural Federation is likewise a Chinese cultural body. This is no accident. Among Thailand's overseas Chinese, those of Teochew origin form an absolute majority; from the early "Nanyang" migration waves to today's markets and firms in Bangkok, the Teochew dialect and cuisine have seeped into daily Thai life. According to China Qiaowang, former Thai deputy prime minister Korn Dabbaransi noted in his opening speech that his ancestors came from Chenghai in Guangdong — a Teochew stronghold. An interesting observation: across Southeast Asia, the "dialect map" inside ethnic-Chinese communities is often sharper than national borders, and in Thailand the dominant colour of that map is Teochew red. Understanding this explains why the gala, from planning to performance, carries such a strong Teochew cultural temper.

## Fifty-one years of relations and "Tai-Zhong yi jia qin"

The timing is also telling. The year 2026 marks the 51st anniversary of Thai-Chinese diplomatic relations. In his speech Korn placed the festival next to that milestone, saying the two countries have "walked together" for over half a century and that the bond of "Tai-Zhong yi jia qin" (Thai and Chinese as one family) only grows richer with time. Setting a Chinese festival within the anniversary narrative of bilateral relations is a typical and effective cultural-diplomacy move: it speaks of no interests and lists no clauses, only using the plainest human sentiments — reunion, one family — to lubricate the grand narrative of state relations. For China, Thailand is among the most friendly ASEAN states; for Thailand, signalling goodwill to Beijing through a diaspora cultural event is cheap and well received. The Mid-Autumn gala is thus both emotional and political — only it wraps the politics in mooncakes and song, so people accept it gladly.

## A model of dual identity: from statesmen to artists

What is most worth studying about Thai Chinese is the ease of their "dual identity." Korn is a former deputy prime minister yet speaks openly of "ancestors from Chenghai"; leaders of the Chamber and the Teochew Association are both Thai business titans and the spiritual ties of the Chinese community. The identities do not clash or hide; they reinforce each other. Compared with ethnic tensions seen in some other Southeast Asian countries, Thailand offers a relatively smooth template: ethnic Chinese need not choose between "being Thai" and "being Chinese." At the gala, Chinese and Thai hosts shared the stage and the Federation's artists presented Chinese song and dance on national television — the concrete face of that ease. It shows that when a set of cultural symbols can be claimed by both state elites and ordinary people, the identity anxiety of diaspora groups is greatly diluted.

## From clan hall to screen: passing cultural memory across generations

Zooming out, the gala touches a more practical question: how to pass cultural memory to the young. Among Thailand's new-generation Chinese, many no longer speak a Chinese language, and their bond with clan halls is fading. In the past, Mid-Autumn memory relied on elders organising inside associations while children tagged along; today such scenes are shrinking. Putting the gala on Channel 5 equals repackaging tradition in a medium the young know well — television and the variety-show form. Planner Wu Xiaohan said the show centred on "reunion," "homesickness" and "beauty," precisely to "push Mid-Autumn culture into Thai society." This may not send youngsters to read Tang poetry, but at least it keeps "Mid-Autumn" alive as a public symbol. The continuity of cultural memory rarely rests on preaching; it rests on being seen, again and again.

## Fusion, not one-way export

To be clear, the gala is not a one-way dump of Chinese culture. According to China Qiaowang, besides Chinese song, dance and instrumental pieces, the programme deliberately blended native Thai songs and dance, presenting "Chinese and Thai cultures communicating, hearts connecting." In other words, it was designed as a two-way conversation: ethnic Chinese perform their tradition, and invite Thai cultural symbols on stage too. This "you in me" arrangement echoes the "one family" main line. For a plural society, if an imported culture is to stay, it must learn to dance with the local culture, not tower above it. Thailand's Chinese community clearly knows this — which is why, in Thailand, Chinese culture keeps its character without seeming out of place.

## Soft storytelling beats hard promotion

One final judgement: in Southeast Asia, cultural exchange led by soft, locally initiated diaspora storytelling tends to be steadier and longer-lasting than top-down, hard promotion. In recent years some countries have grown cautious about certain Chinese cultural institutions, yet this Thai gala drew almost no noise — precisely because of its "origin": led by the locally rooted Chamber of Commerce and Teochew Association, performed by local Chinese, watched by the Thai public. It was not air-dropped from outside; it grew from inside Thai society. Such cultural presentation carries its own legitimacy and warmth. For anyone interested in Chinese culture going out, the Thai case deserves a note: real landing never means shipping programmes out, but getting locals to treat them as their own affair.

## Further reading

**The Thai-Chinese Chamber of Commerce**: A Thai ethnic-Chinese business federation founded in the early 20th century, long a bridge for Thai-Chinese economic and people-to-people ties, and one of the most influential institutions in the Thai-Chinese community.

**"Tai-Zhong yi jia qin" (Thai-Chinese as one family)**: The warmest stock phrase in Thai-Chinese relations, stressing geographic proximity, kinship and cultural affinity, routinely used in both high-level and grassroots narratives.

**Teochew diaspora in Thailand**: Ethnic Chinese in Thailand whose ancestral home is the Teochew region of Guangdong; Teochew-origin Thai Chinese form a majority of the community and have deeply shaped Thailand's commerce, cuisine and dialect ecology.
