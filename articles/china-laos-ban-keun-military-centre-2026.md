# 中国在老挝开设军事训练中心：安全存在从铁路延伸到天空

9月29日，中国国防部几乎是在"顺带一提"中宣布：位于老挝万象省班根（Ban Keun）机场的"中老联合保障与训练中心"和"中老友谊航空学院"已正式运行。官方给出的目标听起来很技术化、很克制：帮助老挝培训空军飞行员。但选址、措辞与时机，比这份新闻稿透露得更多。这绝不只是送几台飞行模拟器那么简单——它是北京正在中南半岛腹地悄然铺开的安全架构里，最新也最耐人寻味的一块砖。

## 为什么是班根，为什么是现在

班根机场坐落在万象以北约30公里，正处在向北直抵中国边境的走廊上。这条走廊并非偶然。2021年12月，全长1035公里、连接昆明与万象的中老铁路通车，耗资约60亿美元（约432亿元人民币），把东南亚唯一的内陆国老挝，从"陆锁国"变成了其规划者口中的"陆联国"。铁路是中国对老挝经济押注的招牌工程，而班根中心，则是这笔押注的安全注脚。

无论以什么标准衡量，老挝都是中国在区域内最可靠的伙伴。这个由老挝人民革命党执政的一党制社会主义国家，在2024年担任了东盟轮值主席国，也一贯在中国于东盟共识外交中需要"挡刀"时挺身而出。当北京想要一个不会就主权或透明度追问尴尬问题的朋友，万象总会接起电话。一个能加深两军日常惯性、训练节奏的安全中心，在北京眼里，是一笔低风险、高杠杆的投资。

## "联合保障中心"的措辞艺术

请注意这里的语言。中国没有把班根称为"基地"，而是叫它"联合保障与训练中心"，由双方共同维护运营，并且"不针对任何第三方"。同样的措辞艺术，也用在2025年4月开放的柬埔寨云壤海军基地设施，以及2017年设立的吉布提保障基地上。这种表述是刻意的：一旦叫"基地"，就会在整个高度重视"不结盟"叙事、珍视东道国主权规范的地区拉响警报；而叫"中心"，则保留了合理的推诿空间，也让东盟的外交机器不至于卡死。

中国军事学者对这笔买卖说得更直白。正如一位被《环球时报》引述的专家所言，老挝空军规模不大，长期缺乏系统性的飞行员、地勤与维修人员培训体系；该中心让万象可以在国内自主培养人才，而不必再把学员送出国门。这话既对，又没说在点上。中心同时把老挝空军的训练节奏、装备标准与演习日历，绑上了中国体系——恰恰是这样一种制度性锁定，比任何一届政府都更长久。

## 云壤与班根：一海一空的组合拳

把班根和云壤放在一起看，一条脉络就清晰了。云壤位于柬埔寨海岸，是守卫泰国湾与南海南部入口的海军支撑点；班根则是中南半岛腹地的一座内陆空中节点。一个管海，一个管天。两者合起来，把泰国夹在中间，框住越南的西侧翼，让中国从泰国湾一路深入到湄公河腹地，形成连续的安全存在。吉布提处于印度洋西口，是海外的原型；东南亚这些"中心"则是它在区域政治尺度上的近亲。

批评者会指出这种不对称。美国在全球约设有750处海外军事设施，而中国的海外足迹不过寥寥数个后勤与训练点。柬埔寨否认有中国军队驻扎云壤，北京也坚称班根中心不设独立的中国驻军。这些否认应当被认真对待，但不必天真。真正的问题不在于今晚是否有中国士兵睡在班根，而在于：老挝对本国空军训练的"主权"，在事实上是否已外包给一个既修了铁路、又建了水坝、还兜着预算的伙伴。

## 东盟的平衡还能撑多久

对东盟而言，这个中心是"中心性"与"战略自主"这副常被挂在嘴边的门面，又一道细小的裂缝。老挝与柬埔寨，作为区域内最贫困、对外援依赖最深的成员国，最愿意接纳更深的中国安全合作——而它们这样做，正把区域的重心进一步拉向北京。越南作为老挝东邻、历来对中国在高原地带的影响力保持警惕，自会紧盯班根。其余东盟成员不愿在中美博弈中选边，却只能面对一个内部天平正在悄然倾斜的东盟。

华盛顿照例对中国的军事存在扩张表示担忧，从云壤到班根无一例外。但美国对柬埔寨试过、且大体失败的制裁与说教剧本，在万象同样施展不开。中国的打法——先基础设施、后安全合作、主权话术始终在场——更慢，却更黏。它不要求结盟，只培育依赖。

## 延伸阅读

**一带一路（Belt and Road Initiative）**：中国的跨国基础设施与投资战略，中老铁路即其在大陆东南亚的旗舰项目。

**陆联国战略（Land-linked strategy）**：老挝将“陆锁”地理转化为联通中国、泰国乃至更远市场的枢纽的国家方略。

**云壤海军基地（Ream Naval Base）**：柬埔寨海岸设施，经中方出资扩建，现设中柬联合保障与训练中心。

# China Opens a Military Training Centre in Laos — Quietly Extending Its Security Footprint Inland

On 29 September 2026, the Chinese Ministry of National Defense announced, almost in passing, that a "China–Laos Joint Support and Training Centre" and a "China–Laos Friendship Aviation Academy" at Ban Keun airport in Vientiane Province are now operational. The stated aim is modest and technocratic: to help Laos train its air force pilots. But the location, the wording, and the timing say more than the press release lets on. This is not merely a gift of flight simulators. It is the latest, and most revealing, brick in a security architecture that Beijing is quietly laying across mainland Southeast Asia.

## Why Ban Keun, and why now

Ban Keun sits roughly 30 kilometres north of Vientiane, on the same corridor that runs north to the Chinese border. That corridor is no accident. In December 2021, the China–Laos Railway — 1,035 kilometres of standard-gauge track linking Kunming to Vientiane, built at a cost of about US$6 billion — turned Laos, Southeast Asia's only landlocked country, from a "landlocked" state into what its planners call a "land-linked" one. The railway was the headline act of China's economic bet on Laos. The Ban Keun centre is the security coda to that bet.

Laos is, by any measure, China's most dependable partner in the region. A one-party socialist state led by the Lao People's Revolutionary Party, it chaired ASEAN in 2024 and has consistently shielded Chinese interests in the bloc's consensus-bound diplomacy. When Beijing wants a friend that will not ask awkward questions about sovereignty or transparency, Vientiane answers the phone. A training centre that deepens the two militaries' day-to-day habits is, from Beijing's perspective, a low-risk, high-leverage investment.

## The careful grammar of "joint support centre"

Notice the language. China does not call Ban Keun a "base." It calls it a "joint support and training centre," jointly maintained and operated by both sides, "not directed against any third party." The same careful grammar was used for the facility at Cambodia's Ream Naval Base, opened in April 2025, and for the logistics base in Djibouti, established in 2017. The phrasing is deliberate. Calling it a base would trip alarm bells across a region that prizes the fiction of non-alignment and the norm of host-state sovereignty. Calling it a "centre" preserves plausible deniability and keeps ASEAN's diplomatic machinery from seizing up.

Chinese military analysts are blunter about the payoff. As one expert quoted by Global Times put it, Laos has a small air force and has long lacked a systematic pipeline for training pilots, ground crews and maintenance personnel; the centre lets Vientiane cultivate that talent at home rather than shipping cadets abroad. That is both true and beside the point. The centre also binds the Lao military's training rhythm, equipment standards and exercise calendar to Chinese systems — exactly the kind of institutional lock-in that outlasts any single government.

## Ream and Ban Keun: one sea, one air

Read Ban Keun alongside Ream and a pattern emerges. Ream, on Cambodia's coast, is a naval support node guarding the approach to the Gulf of Thailand and the southern South China Sea. Ban Keun is an inland air node in the heart of the Indochina peninsula. One covers the sea; the other covers the sky. Together they bracket Thailand, frame Vietnam's western flank, and give China a continuous security presence from the Gulf of Thailand deep into the Mekong heartland. Djibouti, at the western entrance to the Indian Ocean, is the overseas prototype; the Southeast Asian "centres" are its regional cousins, scaled to local politics.

Critics will note the asymmetry. The United States operates roughly 750 overseas military facilities; China's overseas footprint is a handful of logistics and training sites. Cambodia denies that Chinese troops are stationed at Ream, and Beijing insists the Laos centre hosts no independent Chinese garrison. Those denials should be taken seriously but not naïvely. The question is not whether Chinese soldiers sleep at Ban Keun tonight. It is whether Lao sovereignty over its own air-force training has, in practice, been outsourced to a partner who also built the railway, financed the dams and underwrites the budget.

## What it means for ASEAN's balance

For the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, the centre is another small crack in the edifice of "centrality" and "strategic autonomy" that members invoke so often. Laos and Cambodia, among the bloc's poorest and most aid-dependent states, are the ones most willing to host deeper Chinese security cooperation — and in doing so they pull the regional centre of gravity further toward Beijing. Vietnam, Laos's eastern neighbour and a historical wary observer of Chinese influence in the highlands, will watch Ban Keun closely. The other ASEAN members, keen to avoid choosing sides in the US–China contest, are left to manage a bloc whose internal balance is quietly shifting.

Washington, predictably, has voiced concern about China's expanding military presence, from Ream to Ban Keun. But the American playbook of sanctions and lectures, tried and largely failed on Cambodia, has limited purchase in Vientiane. China's approach — infrastructure first, security second, sovereignty rhetoric always — is slower but stickier. It does not demand alliance; it cultivates dependence.

## Further reading

**Belt and Road Initiative**: China's transnational infrastructure and investment strategy, of which the China–Laos Railway is the flagship mainland-Southeast-Asia project.

**Land-linked strategy**: Laos's national doctrine of converting its landlocked geography into a transit hub connecting China, Thailand and beyond.

**Ream Naval Base**: Cambodia's coastal facility expanded with Chinese funding, now hosting a China–Cambodia joint support and training centre.
