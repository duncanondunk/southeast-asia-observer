# 不想站队：一份民调里的东南亚"中间地带"定力

一份新近发布的区域民调，把东南亚人心里那本"大国账"摊在了桌面上。据《南华早报（South China Morning Post）》报道，洛克菲勒基金会的一项调查显示，东南亚民众对多边合作抱有坚定信念，同时对由美国或中国来主导地区秩序心存戒备。这不是一句外交辞令的回声，而是十国普通人的真实取向：既不想要华盛顿的指挥棒，也不想接北京的请柬，最舒服的位置，是站在中间。

## 民调说了什么

数字本身并不复杂，却足够刺眼。受访的东南亚公众普遍把"全球合作"视为正途，对单边霸权式的领导安排表现出明显的不信任。在"你更愿意由谁来引领地区未来"这一类问题上，两个超级大国的得分都不高，而"多边机制""区域自主"的选项稳稳站上高位。

值得注意的是，这种戒备是双向的。它不是针对某一个大国的情绪反弹，而是对"被代表、被安排、被绑定"这件事本身的反感。换句话说，东南亚人反感的不是美国或中国，而是任何形式的"替我做主"。

## 不站队，是活下来的本能

看懂这份民调，得先看懂东南亚的地缘位置。这里夹在中、美、印、日之间，又是全球供应链的关键节点，任何一次"选边"的代价都比外人想象的更沉重。站队美国，意味着得罪最大的贸易伙伴；倒向北京，又会在安全上失去华盛顿这张牌。两头都不划算，于是"对冲"成了唯一的理性选择。

观察者网式的直白话：这不是什么高深的战略哲学，而是小国在夹缝里求生的肌肉记忆。东盟成立近六十年，骨子里就是靠"大家坐下来谈、谁也别想一家独大"活着。民调只是把这种本能量化了。

## 多边主义为何成了"安全屋"

为什么"多边主义"在东南亚这么吃香？因为它天然排斥"盟主"。在一个多边框架里，小国拥有和程序大国一样的发言桌——哪怕声音小一点，但起码在场。东盟中心地位（ASEAN Centrality）这个说法，外界常觉得是陈词滥调，但对东南亚而言，它是防止被任意一方"打包带走"的护栏。

这也解释了为何区域国家宁可要一个慢吞吞、常扯皮的东盟，也不要一个高效却由别人定调的机制。效率是其次，主权才是底线。民调里对多边主义的偏爱，本质上是对"自主权"的偏爱。

## 警惕两种误读

围绕这份民调，有两种流行误读需要点破。

第一种，是把"戒备中美领导"读成"反美反华"。错了。东南亚从未拒绝与两大国合作，它拒绝的只是"被领导"。该做生意做生意，该要投资要投资，只是在安全与制度层面，它不愿把钥匙交出去。

第二种，是把民调当成"多边主义必胜"的证据。太天真。民调测的是态度，不是能力。现实中，东盟内部对中美态度并不齐整，柬埔寨、老挝、越南与菲律宾、新加坡的温差极大；单边引力仍在，多边主义更多是一面想举的旗，而非已经走通的路。

## 北京的算盘与地区的清醒

对北京而言，这份民调既是提醒也是机会。提醒在于：靠"实力地位"压人，在东南亚行不通；机会在于：只要尊重区域自主、以多边框架行事，中国与东盟的合作空间依旧广阔。中国反复强调的"东盟中心地位"，若真能落到行动而非话术，恰恰与民调所示民意吻合。

但地区的清醒也在于此——它欢迎中国的投资与基建，却不会因此把战略身家托付出去。这种"既要又要"看似贪心，实则是中小国家最务实的自我保护。谁把这种清醒当成"摇摆不定"来轻视，谁就会在外交上吃苦头。

## 结语：中间地带的定力

这份民调最值得咀嚼的，不是"东南亚不支持谁"，而是"东南亚相信什么"——相信多边、相信协商、相信不必在大国之间二选一。在一个阵营化暗流涌动的世界里，这份定力本身就是一种稀缺资产。

对域外大国来说，真正的智慧不是去赢取东南亚的"站队"，而是学会与一个不愿站队的东南亚共处。做不到这一点，再多的峰会与援助，也只会撞上同一堵墙：东南亚人不想被任何人领导，他们只想自己掌舵。

## 延伸阅读

**多边主义（Multilateralism）**：指三个或以上国家通过制度化协商处理共同事务的原则，强调规则平等与程序正义，是东盟处理大国关系的核心话语。

**东盟中心地位（ASEAN Centrality）**：东盟在区域架构中保持"驾驶员"角色、避免被单一大国主导的自我定位，是东南亚抗拒被绑定的制度护栏。

**对冲战略（Hedging）**：中小国家在大国竞争间同时接触与防范、以分散风险取代选边站队的生存策略，东南亚的常态外交逻辑。

# No Sides to Pick: What a Regional Survey Reveals About Southeast Asia's Resolve to Stay in the Middle

A newly released regional survey has laid bare the great-power ledger that sits in the minds of ordinary Southeast Asians. Reporting by the South China Morning Post, the Rockefeller Foundation survey shows that the public across the region holds a firm belief in multilateral cooperation while harbouring real wariness toward any order led by either the United States or China. This is no echo of diplomatic platitudes; it is the genuine preference of hundreds of millions of people in ten countries. They want neither Washington's baton nor Beijing's invitation — the most comfortable seat is the one in the middle.

## The survey says

The numbers are not complicated, yet they sting. Surveyed Southeast Asian publics broadly regard "global cooperation" as the right path and show marked distrust toward unilateral, hegemonic leadership arrangements. On questions of the form "who would you prefer to steer the region's future," neither superpower scores highly, while "multilateral institutions" and "regional autonomy" sit firmly on top.

What matters is that the wariness cuts both ways. It is not an emotional recoil against one power; it is aversion to being represented, arranged, and bound by anyone else. In other words, Southeast Asians object not to the United States or to China, but to the very idea of having decisions made on their behalf.

## Not picking sides is survival instinct

To read the survey, first read the region's geography. Sandwiched between China, the United States, India, and Japan, and sitting on the wiring of global supply chains, Southeast Asia pays a heavier price for any "alignment" than outsiders imagine. Leaning toward Washington means offending the largest trading partner; tilting to Beijing means forfeiting Washington's security card. Neither trade is worth it, so hedging becomes the only rational choice.

Put plainly: this is not some profound strategic philosophy. It is the muscle memory of small states surviving in a cleft. ASEAN has lived for nearly sixty years on the credo that "everyone sits down and talks, and no single power calls the shots." The survey merely quantifies that instinct.

## Why multilateralism became the safe house

Why is "multilateralism" so popular in Southeast Asia? Because it inherently rejects a "leader of the alliance." Inside a multilateral framework, a small state gets the same table as a great power — its voice may be softer, but it is at least present. The notion of ASEAN Centrality, often dismissed abroad as stale rhetoric, is for Southeast Asia a guardrail against being "bundled away" by either side.

This also explains why regional states prefer a slow, quarrelsome ASEAN to an efficient mechanism scripted by someone else. Efficiency is secondary; sovereignty is the bottom line. The public's preference for multilateralism in the survey is, at root, a preference for "agency."

## Two misreadings to avoid

Two popular misreadings around this survey deserve puncturing.

The first reads "wary of US or Chinese leadership" as "anti-American and anti-Chinese." Wrong. Southeast Asia has never refused cooperation with either power; what it refuses is "being led." It trades where it must, takes investment where offered, but will not hand over the keys on security and institutions.

The second treats the survey as proof that "multilateralism has won." Too naive. The survey measures attitude, not capacity. In reality, ASEAN members are far from aligned on the two giants — the temperature gaps between Cambodia, Laos, and Vietnam versus the Philippines and Singapore are vast; unilateral gravity persists, and multilateralism is more a flag one wishes to raise than a road already travelled.

## Beijing's calculus and the region's clarity

For Beijing, the survey is both a warning and an opening. The warning: governing through "strength" does not work in Southeast Asia. The opening: as long as it respects regional autonomy and acts within multilateral frameworks, China's room to cooperate with ASEAN remains wide. China's repeated emphasis on "ASEAN Centrality," if it reaches action rather than slogan, aligns neatly with the surveyed public mood.

But the region's clarity lies here too — it welcomes Chinese investment and infrastructure without entrusting its strategic fate to anyone. This "wanting both" may look greedy, yet it is the most pragmatic self-protection of smaller states. Whoever dismisses such clarity as "vacillation" will pay diplomatic costs.

## Conclusion: the resolve of the middle ground

What is most worth chewing on in this survey is not "whom Southeast Asia does not support," but "what Southeast Asia believes" — in multilateralism, in consultation, in not having to choose between great powers. In a world where bloc politics stirs beneath the surface, that resolve is itself a scarce asset.

For outside powers, the real wisdom is not to win Southeast Asia's "alignment," but to learn to live with a Southeast Asia that refuses to align. Fail at this, and no amount of summits or aid will avoid the same wall: Southeast Asians do not want to be led by anyone; they only want to hold the helm themselves.

## Further reading

**Multilateralism**: The principle by which three or more states handle common affairs through institutionalised consultation, stressing equal rules and procedural justice; the core vocabulary through which ASEAN engages the great powers.

**ASEAN Centrality**: ASEAN's self-positioning as the "driver" of regional architecture, resisting domination by any single power; the institutional guardrail through which Southeast Asia refuses to be bound.

**Hedging**: The survival strategy of smaller states that simultaneously engage and guard against great powers, dispersing risk instead of choosing sides; the normal diplomatic logic of Southeast Asia.
