# 东盟海事中心落地菲律宾：一个没预算、没编制的机构，能管住南海吗？

据《南华早报》报道，东盟成员国11月14日在马尼拉峰会前通过了一项提案，决定在菲律宾设立"东盟海事中心"（ASEAN Maritime Centre），职能从监测船只动向到遏制非法捕捞，包罗万象。菲律宾外长玛丽亚·特蕾莎·拉扎罗在记者会上宣布这一决定，总统通讯办公室称之为马尼拉担任东盟主席国的"重要成果"之一。听上去是一桩区域治理的大事。可只要把报道往后读两段，就会发现这个中心目前更接近一张蓝图：拉扎罗自己承认，中心尚处规划初期，"暂时不会从东盟拿到任何经费，因为作为一个东盟机构它还有待成立，还有一整套法律文书要签"。一句话，牌子先挂，钱和权都没有。这究竟是东盟海事的制度升级，还是一场精心包装的政治秀？不妨拆开看看。

## 从宿务到马尼拉：一张蓝图的两年铺垫

这个中心不是突然冒出来的。今年5月，东盟领导人在宿务举行第48届峰会，通过《东盟领导人海事合作宣言》，其中就写明了拟在菲律宾设立海事中心，并把东盟海岸警卫队论坛升格为东盟部门机构。小马科斯当时说得很漂亮：中心的目的是促进南海航行自由与海事安全，"不是对抗、也不是要推开任何一个国家或某一种力量"。

问题是，宣言和中心之间隔着一条现实的河。小马科斯本人也承认，接下来要做的是搭框架——中心长什么样、谁参与、怎么参与、谁出钱、设在哪里、到底干什么，都还是问号。据菲律宾媒体报道，11月14日外长会议通过的是概念说明与职权范围，但结构、运作和制度安排仍需继续讨论。换句话说，从宿务到马尼拉，半年过去，东盟完成的是"同意设立一个待设立的机构"。这种文字上的推进在东盟并不罕见——它恰好是这个组织最擅长的那种"程序性成果"。

## 职能重叠：一个中心，撞上三套现成机制

真正的问题在于，这个中心要干的事，别人已经在干。分析人士的质疑几乎都指向同一处：职能重叠。

据《南华早报》报道，马来西亚战略与国际问题研究所（ISIS Malaysia）高级研究员托马斯·丹尼尔指出，目前海上执法合作仅限于东盟海事论坛、东盟海岸警卫队论坛等机制；新中心可以增加制度连续性和技术专长，把这些散落的"雄心"变成具体项目。这话听着是补充，其实也点破了尴尬——中心并非补空白，而是给现成机制再加一层协调。

更尖锐的是菲律宾亚洲事实与趋势研究所（FACTS Asia）分析师文森特·凯尔·帕拉达的提醒：东盟早已签署打击海盗与海上安全信息共享协议，相关职能由设在新加坡的信息融合中心（Information Fusion Centre）承担；而拉扎罗为中心列出的职能，与之高度雷同。一个区域中心，如果干的还是别人在干的活，那它存在的理由是什么？这难免让人怀疑：设立中心本身，是不是比中心能干什么更重要？

## 南海这头大象：谁的海事，谁的中心

中心能不能办成，绕不开一个房间里的大象——南海。

菲律宾与中国的南海争端由来已久，中国主张对几乎整片海域拥有主权，并拒绝承认2016年支持菲律宾的仲裁裁决。在这个背景下，由菲律宾这个声索国来主办一个覆盖全区域的海事中心，天然带着一层信任赤字。丹尼尔说得直白：如果成员国之间的信任和认同没有经营好，"这个中心可能被视作菲律宾国家海事议程的延伸，而非真正属于东盟的机构"。帕拉达也强调，正因为它是区域枢纽，菲律宾的战略利益必须服从东盟整体。

这里就出现了一个有意思的悖论：小马科斯反复强调中心"不针对任何国家"，可中心要处理的船舶监测、非法捕捞、航行自由，几乎每一项都落在南海争议的敏感地带。东盟方式的核心是用模糊换取共存，而海事中心的逻辑是用机制把模糊变成清晰——这两者本身就张力十足。新加坡国立大学尤索夫伊萨研究所（ISEAS）高级研究员林（Joanne Lin）的判断因此显得务实：中心的价值在于加强各领域海事合作的协调，而不是只盯着海上安全；它应当做识别缺口、促进信息共享、连接机构与专家的"协调与支持机构"，而非又一个执法中枢。

## "不针对任何国家"：一句外交辞令的两面

小马科斯那句"不针对任何国家"，值得单独拆一拆。

从好的一面看，这是给自己留台阶，也给中国留余地。据菲律宾媒体报道，被问及中国是否可能参与中心活动时，拉扎罗的回答是"取决于这个机构如何演进"——留了口子，没把门关死。中心如果真能做成技术性的信息共享与能力建设平台，而非声索国单边主张的放大器，各方并非没有合作空间。

但从另一面看，"不针对任何国家"也常常是"针对某个国家但不点名"的另一种说法。帕拉达指出，中心可以推动东盟在南海探讨更敏感的问题，聚焦维护航行自由与尊重主权权利，而不必"从战略竞争的棱镜"去看待。这话说得巧妙，却也暴露了中心的真实战场：它要争的，是南海议题的定义权——把它讲成一个区域规则问题，而不是一场双边主权争议。对马尼拉而言，把争端装进东盟的制度框架，比在双边层面硬碰硬更有利；对整个东盟而言，这却是把组织拖进大国博弈的风险。

## 雄心与账本：东盟方式的一次压力测试

把账算清楚，这个中心的成色就出来了。

第一笔是钱账。拉扎罗明说暂时没有东盟经费，法律文书还没签。一个没有预算、没有编制、没有固定场所的机构，短期内只能是"虚拟中心"——靠成员国临时派人、临时开会来运转。这类机构的寿命，往往取决于主办国愿意投入多少政治资本。

第二笔是共识账。帕拉达说得很不客气：成员国虽然同意设立中心的目的，但"分享什么信息、为谁分享"会成为争论焦点；东盟成员立场光谱极其多元，一国眼中的核心战略利益，可能被另一国搁置。在南海这个问题上，柬埔寨、老挝倾向对华友好，菲律宾、越南立场强硬，印尼、马来西亚各有盘算——要在这个圈子里就"监测什么"达成一致，难度不亚于再谈一份南海行为准则。

第三笔是主权账。中心设在菲律宾，地点本身就是信号。东盟一贯强调"东盟中心性"，可当中心性具体化为一个落在某个声索国的实体机构时，中心性到底属于东盟，还是属于主办国？这是比经费更难回答的问题。

## 制度秀之外，东盟还要面对什么

说到底，东盟海事中心的命运，取决于它能否跨过从"宣布设立"到"真正运作"的那道坎。

林優娜给出的条件很清楚：明确的职责范围、以"实际成果"为目标、与现有东盟机构有清晰的工作安排、充足资源、以及成员国持续支持。这五条，条条都不容易。尤其最后一条——"持续支持",恰恰是东盟各类机制的通病：成立时锣鼓喧天，运转时门可罗雀。

但也不必把话说死。东盟在制度建构上有它独特的韧性：它擅长把棘手问题放进谈话框架里慢慢消化，用低门槛的机构先占住位置，再等待时机成熟。海事中心或许正是这样一步棋——现在是个空壳，但空壳也是资产，因为它在南海议题上为东盟、也为菲律宾，占住了一个制度性的位置。真正的考验在于：当南海局势再次紧张时，这个中心是会成为一个区域共同应对的平台，还是会沦为单一边立场的传声筒？答案不在蓝图里，而在未来几年成员国的实际投入中。

对观察者而言，值得记住的不是中心今天有多空，而是它把"谁来定义南海的区域规则"这个问题，从理念之争推向了机构之争。这一步，已经迈出去了。

## 延伸阅读

**东盟中心性（ASEAN Centrality）**：指东盟在区域合作架构中居于主导地位、由东盟设定议程与规则的原则，是东盟对外关系的核心叙事；海事中心的归属之争正是这一原则的现实践证。

**南海行为准则（Code of Conduct in the South China Sea）**：东盟与中国自2000年代起磋商的文件，旨在为南海行为建立约束性规则，至今尚未定案；海事中心被部分分析视为其制度补充。

**信息融合中心（Information Fusion Centre）**：设在新加坡的海上安全信息共享机制，由多国派驻联络官，已承担海盗打击与海事信息交换职能，是新海事中心职能重叠的主要对象。

# ASEAN's maritime centre in the Philippines: can an institution with no budget and no staff police the South China Sea?

According to the South China Morning Post, ASEAN member states adopted a proposal on November 14, ahead of the bloc's summit in Manila, to establish an "ASEAN Maritime Centre" based in the Philippines, with a remit ranging from monitoring vessel movements to curbing illegal fishing. Philippine Foreign Secretary Maria Theresa Lazaro announced the agreement at a press briefing, and the presidential communications office called it one of the "very important accomplishments" of Manila's ASEAN chairmanship. It sounds like a major advance in regional governance. Yet read a little further and the centre looks more like a blueprint: Lazaro herself conceded the project is in its early stages, that "we are not going to get any funding from Asean yet because it is still to be established as an Asean body," and that a whole series of legal instruments remains to be signed. In short, the plaque goes up first; the money and the authority do not exist yet. Is this an institutional upgrade for ASEAN maritime affairs, or a carefully packaged political performance? It is worth taking apart.

## From Cebu to Manila: two years of groundwork for a blueprint

The centre did not appear out of nowhere. In May, ASEAN leaders gathered in Cebu for the 48th summit and adopted the ASEAN Leaders' Declaration on Maritime Cooperation, which wrote in the proposed maritime centre in the Philippines and elevated the ASEAN Coast Guard Forum to a sectoral body. Ferdinand Marcos Jnr put it grandly: the centre would promote freedom of navigation and maritime safety in the South China Sea and was "not to confront or push back on any single force or any single country."

The trouble is that a declaration and a centre are separated by a practical gulf. Marcos himself admitted the next task was to build the framework — what the centre would look like, who would be involved and how, who would fund it, where it would sit, and what it was actually supposed to do. According to Philippine media, what the foreign ministers approved on November 14 was a concept note and terms of reference; structure, operations and institutional arrangements still require discussion. In other words, from Cebu to Manila, half a year later, ASEAN has achieved "agreement to establish an institution yet to be established." Such textual progress is hardly unusual in ASEAN — it is precisely the kind of "procedural achievement" the organisation does best.

## Overlapping mandates: one centre, three existing mechanisms

The real problem is that the centre would do what others already do. Analysts' doubts point to much the same place: duplication of functions.

According to the SCMP, Thomas Daniel, a senior foreign policy analyst at ISIS Malaysia, noted that cooperation on maritime matters such as law enforcement is currently limited to mechanisms like the ASEAN Maritime Forum and the ASEAN Coast Guard Forum; the new centre could add institutional continuity and technical expertise to turn ASEAN's assorted maritime aspirations into concrete initiatives. That sounds like a complement, but it also exposes the awkwardness: the centre is not filling a gap so much as adding another layer of coordination over mechanisms already in place.

Sharper still was the warning from Vincent Kyle Parada, a foreign-policy analyst at FACTS Asia: ASEAN has already signed an agreement on combating piracy and sharing information on regional maritime security, functions carried out by the Singapore-based Information Fusion Centre; and the roles Lazaro outlined for the proposed centre overlap heavily. If a regional centre does what someone else is already doing, what is its reason for being? One cannot help suspecting that establishing the centre matters more than what the centre can do.

## The elephant in the room: whose maritime affairs, whose centre?

Whether the centre can be built cannot be separated from the elephant in the room — the South China Sea.

The Philippines' long-running dispute with China over the waterway is well known: China claims nearly the entire sea and rejects a 2016 international arbitral ruling that favoured the Philippines. Against that backdrop, hosting a region-wide maritime centre in a claimant state carries an inbuilt trust deficit. Daniel put it bluntly: if trust and buy-in among member states are not carefully managed, "it could be perceived as an extension of the Philippines' national maritime agenda rather than a genuinely Asean-owned institution." Parada likewise stressed that, precisely because it is set up as a regional hub, Philippine strategic interests will have to be subordinated to ASEAN as a whole.

Here lies an interesting paradox. Marcos repeatedly stressed the centre is "not aimed at any country," yet the issues it would address — vessel monitoring, illegal fishing, freedom of navigation — almost all land in the sensitive zone of South China Sea disputes. The core of the "ASEAN way" is to trade ambiguity for coexistence; the logic of a maritime centre is to turn ambiguity into clear procedure through institutions. The two are inherently in tension. Hence the pragmatism of Joanne Lin, a senior fellow at ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute: the centre's value lies in strengthening coordination across different areas of maritime cooperation rather than focusing exclusively on maritime security; it should serve as a coordinating and supporting institution that identifies gaps, facilitates information-sharing and connects agencies and experts, rather than becoming yet another law-enforcement hub.

## "Not aimed at any country": two faces of a diplomatic formula

Marcos's line that the centre is "not aimed at any country" deserves a closer look.

On the positive reading, it leaves a step back for Manila and room for Beijing. According to Philippine media, asked whether China might take part in the centre's activities, Lazaro replied that it would depend on how the institution evolves — an opening left, not a door shut. If the centre genuinely becomes a technical platform for information-sharing and capacity-building, rather than an amplifier of a claimant's unilateral positions, cooperation is not out of the question.

On the other reading, "not aimed at any country" is often just another way of saying "aimed at a particular country without naming it." Parada noted the centre could help ASEAN explore more sensitive South China Sea issues, focusing on upholding freedom of navigation and respect for sovereign rights without viewing them "through the lens of strategic competition." The phrasing is clever, yet it reveals the centre's real battlefield: it is fighting over the power to define the South China Sea issue — to recast it as a regional rules question rather than a bilateral sovereignty dispute. For Manila, packaging the dispute inside ASEAN's institutional framework is more advantageous than a hard bilateral confrontation. For ASEAN as a whole, however, it risks dragging the organisation into great-power rivalry.

## Ambition versus the ledger: a stress test of the ASEAN way

Do the arithmetic and the centre's true colour emerges.

First, the money. Lazaro was explicit: no ASEAN funding for now, and the legal instruments have yet to be signed. An institution with no budget, no staff and no fixed premises can only be a "virtual centre" for the time being, running on seconded personnel and ad hoc meetings. The lifespan of such bodies often depends on how much political capital the host is willing to invest.

Second, the consensus. Parada was blunt: members may agree on the centre's purpose, but "what information — on what and for what purpose — may become points of contention"; ASEAN's members span an incredibly diverse spectrum, and an issue seen as a core strategic interest by one country may be sidelined by another. On the South China Sea, Cambodia and Laos lean toward Beijing, the Philippines and Vietnam take a harder line, and Indonesia and Malaysia each have their own calculations. Getting this group to agree on "what to monitor" is no easier than negotiating another code of conduct.

Third, the sovereignty ledger. The centre's location in the Philippines is itself a signal. ASEAN stresses "ASEAN centrality" — yet when centrality takes the form of a concrete institution located in a claimant state, does that centrality belong to ASEAN, or to the host? That is a harder question than funding.

## Beyond institutional theatre: what ASEAN still has to face

Ultimately, the centre's fate depends on whether it can cross the gap from "announced" to "operational."

Lin's conditions are clear: a clearly outlined scope, "practical outcomes" as a key goal, clear working arrangements with existing ASEAN bodies, adequate resources, and sustained support from member states. None is easy. The last — sustained support — is the chronic ailment of ASEAN mechanisms: a rousing launch, followed by an empty room.

Yet one need not write it off. ASEAN has a distinctive institutional resilience: it places thorny issues inside conversational frameworks to be digested slowly, using low-threshold bodies to occupy a position before the time is ripe. The maritime centre may be just such a move — an empty shell for now, but an empty shell is also an asset, because on the South China Sea it secures an institutional foothold for ASEAN and, not incidentally, for the Philippines. The real test is this: when tensions in the South China Sea flare again, will the centre become a platform for collective regional response, or a megaphone for a single side's position? The answer lies not in the blueprint but in what members actually invest over the coming years.

For observers, what deserves remembering is not how empty the centre is today, but that it has pushed the question of "who defines the region's rules for the South China Sea" from a contest of ideas into a contest of institutions. That step has already been taken.

## Further reading

**ASEAN Centrality**: the principle that ASEAN sits at the centre of regional architecture, setting the agenda and rules — a core narrative of the bloc's external relations; the dispute over whose centre this is puts that principle to the test.

**Code of Conduct in the South China Sea**: the document ASEAN and China have negotiated since the 2000s to establish binding rules of behaviour, still unfinished; the maritime centre is seen by some analysts as an institutional complement.

**Information Fusion Centre**: a maritime security information-sharing mechanism based in Singapore with liaison officers from multiple countries, already handling piracy and information exchange — the main source of functional overlap with the new centre.
