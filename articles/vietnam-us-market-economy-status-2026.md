# 越南再争美国「市场经济地位」：贸易红利与产业链依附的两难

据《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》报道，越南贸易官员正游说美国商务部，争取将越南从「非市场经济（Non-Market Economy，简称NME）」重新归类为「市场经济（Market Economy，简称MES）」地位。这并非越南第一次叩门——美国自2002年起将越南列为非市场经济体，2023年的最新复审仍维持原判。在特朗普第二任期关税工具被法院削弱、美越贸易协议进入收官阶段的当下，河内为何再次把这张旧牌摆上桌面？答案藏在越南对美出口的黄金时代与其难以摆脱的产业链依附之间。

## 再叩「市场经济地位」之门

越南此番游说，时机耐人寻味。一方面，美越围绕互惠贸易协议的谈判已进入「决定性阶段」，越南国家主席苏林二月在华盛顿签下总计372亿美元（约2678亿元人民币）的采购大单，从波音客机到美国农产品，几乎是把「买美国货」写在了脸上；另一方面，美国对越南商品的关税仍处高位，真正在本地合规生产的商品适用20%税率，被认定借道转运的则面临40%惩罚性关税。经济形势逼人：2026年上半年，越南对美货物顺差达到1140亿美元（约8208亿元人民币），创下全球最高，比中国还高。顺差越大，谈判筹码越重，被盯得也越紧。

## 什么是「市场经济地位」

要理解河内的执念，先得弄清这张「身份牌」值多少钱。在反倾销调查中，美国对「非市场经济体」不适用其本国价格，而是找「替代国（surrogate country）」的成本来推算倾销幅度，结果往往大幅高估，加征的反倾销税（anti-dumping duty）动辄高出一截。一旦获认市场经济地位，越南企业就能用自身的生产成本与财务数据自证清白，同样的出口商品，被课以的惩罚性关税可能显著回落。对一家纺织或水产企业而言，这不止是几个百分点的利润，而是订单能否续签的生死线。

## 数字：关税与顺差的双重压力

数字最能说明压力。自2026年七月起，美国依据301条款（Section 301）对六十个经济体加征强迫劳动相关附加税，越南被归入12.5%的最高档，而印尼、马来西亚、孟加拉国、柬埔寨、印度等竞争对手仅适用10%。以2026年全年对美出口约1730亿美元（约1.25万亿元人民币）估算，这2.5个百分点的差距，相当于每年约40亿美元（约288亿元人民币）的税负劣势。更棘手的是，301条款没有到期日，企业必须把它当作长期变量，重排供应链与客户结构。

## 症结：为什么华盛顿迟迟不点头

既然好处如此具体，华盛顿为何屡屡摇头？核心顾虑不在河内，而在北京。美国商务部历年维持越南非市场经济地位的理由，集中在政府在经济中的深度介入、汇率管制与国企比重；但今天真正的拦路虎，是越南与中国之间难以分割的产业链。越南从中国大量进口电子零件、机械设备、面料与化工原料，本地组装后再出口美国——这正是全球供应链的常态，却也模糊了「本地生产」与「借道转运（transshipment）」的边界。华盛顿担心的，正是中国商品经越南简单加工后洗产地说走美国关税。

## 中国因素：繁荣背后的供应链依附

数据戳破了「越南制造已经独立造血」的想象。2026年一至八月，越南自中国进口约1619亿美元（约1.17万亿元人民币），同比增长37.3%，中国稳居第一大进口来源国；而越南对华贸易逆差规模，几乎与对美贸易顺差等量齐观。换言之，越南对美出口的繁荣，相当程度上建立在中国中间品之上。美国要求越南在出口管制、供应链审查上与己「保持一致」，本质是要越南在两条产业链之间二选一——这正是河内最不愿面对、也最无力破解的困局。

## 越南的两难：既要美国市场，也离不开中国

越南的尴尬在于，它既不敢失去美国市场，也无法一夜切断中国供给。对美出口占越南总出口近三分之一，是增长的基本盘；而中国提供的核心零部件，短期根本造不出来替代。为换取协议落地，河内已主动让步：承诺加大采购美国高科技与农产品，并出台禁止强迫劳动产品的二九二号议定，以完善法律框架、回应美方关切。但法案易立，产业难改。越南虽已出台二〇二六至2035年工业发展规划，力求培育本土配套、减少外购依赖，但本土企业的产能与技术，仍跟不上外资工厂的扩产速度。

## 远水难救近火：改革与产业的深层短板

比起身份牌，更深的短板在产业地基。越南电网年负荷增速约一成，电力供给跟不上工业扩张，官方已预警2027年起或频繁停电；2023年的大范围断电曾拖累增长。与此同时，劳动力红利消退，出生率走低、老龄化加速，年轻人不愿进流水线而偏好服务业，工厂招工普遍困难。新能源项目也留下烂尾：全国约85座风电光伏项目搁浅，合计装机4.7吉瓦，仅1.6吉瓦并网。贸易条件再优惠，若供电、配套、人力这三条地基不稳，优质制造企业也留不住。

## 结语：一场注定长期的博弈

说到底，越南此次再争市场经济地位，是一场「以身份换空间」的长期博弈。它能在谈判桌上签下天价采购单、出台合规法令，却难以在短期内重塑依附于中国的供应链、补齐产业与基建短板。华盛顿是否会点头，取决于它是否相信河内真能厘清原产地、堵住转运漏洞——而这恰恰需要时间。对越南而言，真正的护城河不是一纸身份，而是扎实的配套工厂、稳定的电力与够用的工人。短期区位红利换得的增速终会见顶，唯有一步步补齐短板，小国经济才能走得更稳。

## 延伸阅读

**非市场经济地位（Non-Market Economy, NME）**：美国反倾销调查中，对被认定政府深度介入经济的贸易伙伴，改用「替代国」成本推算倾销幅度，往往导致更高关税。

**301条款（Section 301）**：美国贸易法授权美方对「不公平贸易行为」单边加征报复性关税的条款，2026年七月起对越南等六十个经济体加征强迫劳动相关附加税。

**原产地规则（Rules of Origin）**：判定商品「真正产自何处」的海关标准，是美越谈判中区分「本地合规生产」与「借道转运」的核心抓手。

# Vietnam's Second Push for US 'Market Economy' Status: Trade Gains vs Industrial Dependence

Nikkei Asia reports that Vietnamese trade officials are lobbying the U.S. Commerce Department to reclassify the country from "non-market economy" (NME) to "market economy" (MES) status. This is hardly Hanoi's first knock on the door: the United States has listed Vietnam as a non-market economy since 2002, and its most recent review in 2023 kept the label intact. Why is Hanoi playing this old card again, just as the Trump administration's tariff toolkit has been curtailed by the courts and a bilateral trade deal enters its final stage? The answer lies between Vietnam's golden age of exports to America and an industrial chain it cannot easily untangle.

## Knocking on the door again

Vietnam's timing is telling. On one front, negotiations over a reciprocal trade agreement have reached a "decisive phase"; during General Secretary Tô Lâm's February visit to Washington the two sides signed procurement packages worth a combined US$37.2 billion — from Boeing jets to American farm goods, almost a public display of "buy American." On the other, U.S. tariffs on Vietnamese goods remain elevated: goods genuinely made in Vietnam face a 20% rate, while those judged to be transshipped carry a punitive 40%. The numbers are sobering. In the first half of 2026 Vietnam ran a US$114 billion goods surplus with the United States — the largest of any country, ahead of China. The bigger the surplus, the heavier the bargaining chip, and the tighter the scrutiny.

## What "market economy status" actually means

To grasp Hanoi's obsession, one must see what the label is worth. In anti-dumping investigations, Washington does not use a non-market economy's own prices; instead it picks a "surrogate country" to estimate dumping margins, routinely overstating them and piling on heavier penalties. Once granted market-economy status, Vietnamese firms could defend themselves with their own production costs and financial data, and the punitive duties on the same exports could fall sharply. For a textile or seafood exporter, this is not a few points of margin — it is the difference between renewing a contract and losing it.

## The numbers: a tariff sword over exporters' heads

The figures expose the pressure. Since July 2026 the United States has used Section 301 to add forced-labor-related tariffs across 60 economies; Vietnam sits in the highest 12.5% bracket, while competitors such as Indonesia, Malaysia, Bangladesh, Cambodia and India face only 10%. On projected 2026 exports to the U.S. of roughly US$173 billion, that 2.5-point gap translates into about US$4 billion of annual tax disadvantage. Worse, Section 301 tariffs have no expiry date. Firms must treat them as a permanent variable and rework supply chains and customer mixes accordingly.

## The crux: why Washington hesitates

If the upside is so concrete, why does Washington keep shaking its head? The real obstacle is not Hanoi but Beijing. The Commerce Department's past rationale for keeping Vietnam on the NME list — state involvement in the economy, currency controls, the weight of state firms — matters, but today's decisive concern is the supply chain Vietnam cannot sever from China. Vietnam imports vast quantities of Chinese electronics components, machinery, fabrics and chemicals, assembles them locally, and re-exports to the United States. That is normal global supply-chain behaviour, yet it blurs the line between "made in Vietnam" and "transshipped." Washington's fear is precisely that Chinese goods undergo superficial processing in Vietnam to dodge American tariffs.

## The China factor: supply-chain dependence behind the boom

The data puncture the illusion that "Made in Vietnam" has learned to stand on its own. In the first eight months of 2026 Vietnam imported about US$161.9 billion from China, up 37.3% year on year, with China firmly its largest supplier; its trade deficit with China is almost as large as its surplus with the United States. In other words, much of Vietnam's export boom to America rests on Chinese intermediate goods. When Washington asks Hanoi to align on export controls and supply-chain scrutiny, it is in effect asking Vietnam to choose between two industrial chains — exactly the bind Hanoi least wants, and least can solve.

## Vietnam's dilemma: the US market it cannot lose, the Chinese inputs it cannot replace

Hanoi's awkwardness is that it dares not lose the American market yet cannot unplug Chinese supply overnight. Exports to the U.S. are nearly a third of its total and the bedrock of growth, while the core Chinese components cannot be domestically substituted in the short run. To secure a deal, Hanoi has already conceded: it pledged to buy more American high-tech and farm products and enacted Decree 292 to ban forced-labor goods, tightening its legal framework to answer U.S. concerns. Laws are easy to pass; industries are hard to reshape. Vietnam's 2026–2035 industrial plan aims to nurture local suppliers and cut import reliance, yet domestic capacity and technology still lag the expansion pace of foreign factories.

## No quick fix: structural and industrial weaknesses

Beneath the status question lie deeper faults in the industrial foundation. Vietnam's power grid load grows about 10% a year, outpacing supply; officials have warned of frequent blackouts from 2027, and the 2023 widespread outages already dented growth. Meanwhile the labor dividend is fading: falling birth rates, accelerating ageing, and young people shunning factory lines for services make hiring difficult across the board. Renewable projects have also stalled — some 85 wind and solar plants, totaling 4.7 gigawatts, are stranded, with only 1.6 gigawatts connected. No matter how favorable the trade terms, without stable power, local suppliers and enough workers, the best manufacturers will not stay.

## Conclusion: a long game

In the end, Vietnam's renewed bid for market-economy status is a long game of trading identity for room. Hanoi can sign multibillion-dollar purchase orders and enact compliance laws at the table, but it cannot, in the short run, rewire a supply chain bound to China or close its industrial and infrastructure gaps. Whether Washington nods depends on believing Hanoi can truly clarify origin and seal transshipment loopholes — and that takes time. For Vietnam, the real moat is not a piece of paper but solid supplier networks, reliable power and enough workers. The growth bought by short-term geographic dividends will eventually peak; only by closing the gaps step by step can a small economy walk steadier.

## Further reading

**Non-Market Economy (NME):** In U.S. anti-dumping cases, trading partners judged to have heavy state involvement are assessed using "surrogate country" costs, typically producing higher duties.

**Section 301:** A U.S. trade-law provision letting Washington impose retaliatory tariffs unilaterally over "unfair trade practices"; from July 2026 it added forced-labor tariffs on Vietnam and 59 other economies.

**Rules of origin:** Customs standards that determine where a good is "really made" — the central lever in U.S.–Vietnam talks for separating genuine local production from transshipment.
