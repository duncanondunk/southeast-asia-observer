# 曼谷降门槛、新加坡搭桥：东南亚两家交易所的"上市争夺战"

## 一个1家，一个5家

先看一组让人不太舒服的数字。2026年上半年，泰国证券交易所（SET）只有1家企业首次公开募股（IPO），募资1040万美元（约7000万元人民币）；新加坡交易所（SGX）有5家，募资10.5亿美元（约70.6亿元人民币）。同期，整个亚太地区有247起首次公开募股，募资470亿美元（约3158亿元人民币）。

247对1，247对5。这不是"节奏慢一点"的问题，而是东南亚最大的两个资本市场，在几乎缺席一场正在改写全球资本流向的融资潮。据日经亚洲报道，两家交易所的管理层都在本月给出了他们的答案：曼谷打算把门槛往下调，新加坡打算往外接一座桥。

这两个动作方向相反，指向的却是同一个焦虑。

## 曼谷做减法：把门槛降下来

泰国证券交易所总裁阿萨德（Asadej Kongsiri）透露，交易所正准备放宽上市标准，以吸引从电子、电动车到机器人等"新经济"公司。具体方案包括：主板上市的最低市值要求，从现行的75亿泰铢（约15.4亿元人民币）下调到30亿至50亿泰铢（约6.2亿至10.3亿元人民币）。

更值得注意的，是另一条拟议中的修改——取消外国公司上市时必须证明对本地经济有贡献的要求，比如在泰国设立生产基地。

这条要求的初衷并不难理解：泰国不希望自己的资本市场变成外国公司的提款机，而希望上市行为能带来工厂、就业和技术转让。但在全球供应链重新布局的当口，它同时也是一道把跨国科技公司挡在门外的墙。取消它，等于承认一个不太舒服的现实——对那些真正高增长的科技公司而言，先在泰国建厂再上市，这个先后顺序已经不成立了。

阿萨德的说法很坦率："我们当然希望拿到新经济——更高增长的公司、新的板块，说得直白些，就是更性感，或者至少更有趣、更多元化的公司。"他还补了一句更见功力的话：交易所能控制的是让自己的规则有竞争力；如果企业的业务和收入都在泰国，长远来看，在泰国上市才更合理。

前半句是愿望，后半句是自知之明。

## 新加坡做加法：搭一座通往纳斯达克的桥

新加坡交易所选择的是另一条路。它与纳斯达克合作设立了一个新的"全球上市板"（Global Listing Board），为寻求在新加坡与美国双重上市的公司提供通道。该板块瞄准的是"与亚洲有关联、有全球抱负的成长型企业"，上市时市值需达到20亿新元（约106亿元人民币）以上。

新加坡交易所总裁冼显明（Michael Syn）的说法是，这座桥更多是"把流程统一起来"。为了配合双重上市，新加坡已经修改了相关法律，他希望在"未来几个月或几个季度"看到头几家落地。

这个设计的聪明之处在于，它不要求企业在"新加坡还是美国"之间二选一。过去十几年，东南亚最好的科技公司几乎都做了同一个选择：去美国。冼显明没有去拦这个选择，而是试图让新加坡成为这条路上的一站，而不是终点站的对面。

但他也划了一条线。新加坡交易所并不打算不计代价地追逐人工智能热潮。冼显明说，交易所要做的，是与新加坡的外部经济相关、新加坡能发挥一些作用的事。"人工智能这个东西跑得非常非常快，说我们在'攻'某个板块，思路可能就是错的。应该问的是：如果这确实是真实存在的实体经济，当它们需要大笔资金去扩张时，你准备好没有？"

## 存托凭证里藏着的那份不甘

另一个细节更能说明问题。今年7月，新加坡交易所推出了新加坡存托凭证（SDR），首批标的包括马斯克旗下的SpaceX——它6月刚在纳斯达克上市——以及Grab和Sea这两家选择在美国而非本土上市的新加坡数字巨头。

存托凭证的作用是让本地投资者能通过新加坡交易所的工具，投资在海外上市的公司。功能上讲，这是产品创新；情绪上讲，这是一份不甘。Grab和Sea是新加坡最成功的两家科技公司，它们的收入、员工和用户都在东南亚，资本却在美国募。把它们"请回"本地市场的方式，不是说服它们回新加坡上市，而是先让本地散户能买到它们的凭证。

这是一种退而求其次的做法，但它至少说明管理层的头脑是清醒的：把已经跑掉的公司追回来，不如先把管道铺好，让下一批公司不必跑。

## 估值与流动性：真正的瓶颈

安永（EY）东盟首次公开募股业务主管Chan Yew Kiang的判断，给这场争夺战泼了一盆必要的冷水。他说，估值和流动性，仍是这两个市场吸引新经济科技与人工智能企业面临的主要挑战；除了数据中心，市场对这类公司的风险和回报还不熟悉。

这话点到了要害。降门槛能解决"能不能来"，解决不了"来了值不值"。一家科技公司选择在哪个市场上市，核心考虑不是最低市值要求是30亿还是75亿泰铢，而是它能不能拿到合理的定价，以及它的股票在上市之后能不能真正被交易。东南亚市场长期以金融、地产等传统板块为主，投资者对半导体、机器人这类资产的定价经验不足，流动性也薄。门槛降下来，如果定价机制和流动性跟不上，结果只会是来一批估值被压低的公司。

反观香港，安永7月的报告指出，上半年上市募资额比2025年同期增长了60%以上，硬科技——人工智能基础设施、半导体、机器人、先进制造——正在重新定义上市资源。差距不在规则，在市场深度。

当然，亮点也是有的。今年截至8月中，新加坡芯片测试设备制造商AEM控股股价上涨近500%，泰国电源与电子设备制造出口商台达电子泰国公司上涨超过50%；马来西亚半导体设计公司SkyeChip今年5月在本地交易所上市。这些例子证明，东南亚不是没有好公司，而是好公司还没有形成板块效应。

## 交易所之争，争的究竟是什么

两家交易所都给出了自己的管道数据。新加坡交易所本月早些时候披露，约有50家公司处于接触或筹备上市的不同阶段，其中三分之一属于科技、先进制造与数字基础设施；上个月，医疗服务提供商Foundation Healthcare Holdings上市募资2.42亿新元（约12.8亿元人民币）。泰国交易所则披露了10家近期储备项目，5家已获批、5家在审，并预计今年主板与次板的新上市总数至少能与去年的18家持平。

数字在好转，但问题的性质没变。东南亚交易所真正在争的，不是几单承销费，而是"定价权"——一个经济体能否为自己的科技公司定价，决定了这些公司的融资成本、并购能力和最终留在谁手里。当东南亚最好的公司长期在纽约或香港完成定价，本地市场拿到的就只是二手流动性和一点点残渣。

所以，曼谷降门槛和新加坡搭桥，本质上都是同一个动作：让资本留在本地的时间长一点。前者赌的是供应链重构会带来一批真正在泰国设厂的公司，后者赌的是亚洲公司终将需要一个亚洲的资本市场入口。两笔赌注都不小，也都还没到揭晓的时候。

但方向是对的。一个只有工厂而没有资本市场的东南亚，永远只能拿到制造业那一半的利润。

## 延伸阅读

**首次公开募股（IPO）**：一家公司首次向公众出售股份并在证券交易所挂牌的过程，是企业融资、早期投资者退出的主要通道，也是衡量一个资本市场活跃度的核心指标。

**存托凭证（Depository Receipt）**：由托管银行发行的、代表境外上市公司股份的可交易凭证，让本地投资者无需跨境开户即可投资海外公司；新加坡交易所2026年7月推出的新加坡存托凭证（SDR）首批标的包括SpaceX、Grab与Sea。

**市场流动性（Market liquidity）**：资产能够以接近市价的价格快速买卖的能力。流动性不足会压低估值、放大波动，是新兴市场交易所吸引科技公司时最难克服的结构性短板。

# Bangkok lowers the bar, Singapore builds a bridge: Southeast Asia's exchanges chase the listing boom

## One and five

Start with an uncomfortable set of numbers. In the first half of 2026, the Stock Exchange of Thailand managed a single initial public offering, raising US$10.4 million. The Singapore Exchange managed five, raising a combined US$1.05 billion. Across the Asia-Pacific region there were 247 IPOs raising US$47 billion over the same period, according to consultancy EY.

Two hundred and forty-seven to one. Two hundred and forty-seven to five. This is not a matter of a slightly slower tempo. Two of Southeast Asia's largest capital markets have been almost entirely absent from a fundraising wave that is rewriting where global capital flows. As Nikkei Asia reported, the management of both exchanges set out their answers this month: Bangkok intends to lower the bar, Singapore intends to build a bridge outward.

The two moves point in opposite directions. They answer the same anxiety.

## Bangkok subtracts: lowering the bar

Asadej Kongsiri, president of the Stock Exchange of Thailand, says the bourse is preparing to relax its listing criteria to attract "new-economy" companies ranging from electronics and electric vehicles to robotics. The proposed changes would cut the minimum market capitalisation for a main-board listing from the current THB 7.5 billion (US$229.4 million) to THB 3–5 billion (US$91.7–152.9 million).

More telling is another proposed amendment: scrapping the requirement that foreign companies seeking a listing demonstrate a contribution to the local economy, such as establishing a manufacturing base in Thailand.

The original intent is easy to understand. Thailand does not want its capital market to become a cash machine for foreign firms; it wants listings to bring factories, jobs and technology transfer. At a moment when global supply chains are being redrawn, however, the rule doubles as a wall keeping multinational technology companies out. Dropping it amounts to conceding an uncomfortable fact: for genuinely high-growth tech firms, the sequence of "build the Thai factory first, list later" no longer holds.

Asadej is candid about it. "Obviously, ideally, we'd like to get new economies — higher-growth companies, new sectors that are, so to speak, sexy, or at least more interesting and more diversified." Then a line that shows more self-knowledge: from the exchange's perspective, what it can control is making sure its regulations are competitive; and if a business is based in Thailand and earns its revenue in Thailand, listing in Thailand makes more sense in the long run.

The first half is a wish. The second half is an admission.

## Singapore adds: a bridge to Nasdaq

The Singapore Exchange has chosen a different road. Working with Nasdaq, it has set up a new Global Listing Board for companies seeking dual listings in Singapore and the United States. The board targets "growth-oriented companies with an Asian nexus and global ambitions" with a market capitalisation of at least SGD 2 billion (US$1.6 billion) at listing.

Michael Syn, president of SGX, describes the arrangement as more about "unifying that process" for dual IPOs. Singapore has changed its laws to accommodate it, and he expects the first few listings in "the coming months or quarters."

The elegance of the design is that it does not ask companies to choose between Singapore and America. For more than a decade, nearly every good Southeast Asian technology company has made the same choice: go to the United States. Syn is not trying to block that choice. He is trying to make Singapore a stop on the route rather than the opposite of the destination.

He also draws a line. SGX does not intend to chase the AI boom at any cost. The exchange wants to do things relevant to Singapore's external economy, where Singapore has some role to play. "This AI thing is moving very, very fast, and to say that we're attacking a sector is probably the wrong approach," he said. "It's to say if this is actually the genuine real economy that exists, can you facilitate the fact that if they need more capital to invest big, are you ready for them?"

## What the depository receipts betray

Another detail says more. In July, SGX launched Singapore Depository Receipts for Elon Musk's SpaceX, which listed on Nasdaq in June, and for Grab and Sea — the two Singaporean digital giants that chose to list in the United States rather than at home.

SDRs let local buyers invest in overseas-listed companies through SGX instruments. Functionally, that is product innovation. Emotionally, it reads as regret. Grab and Sea are Singapore's two most successful technology companies. Their revenue, staff and users are in Southeast Asia; their capital was raised in America. The way to bring them "back" is not to talk them into relisting in Singapore but to let local retail investors buy receipts in them first.

It is a second-best solution. But it shows a clear-headed management: chasing back the companies that have already left is harder than laying the pipe so the next batch never has to go.

## Valuations and liquidity: the real bottleneck

Chan Yew Kiang, EY's ASEAN IPO leader, pours the necessary cold water. Valuations and liquidity, he says, remain the key challenges the two markets face in attracting new-economy technology and AI businesses. Except for data centres, the markets remain unfamiliar with the risks and rewards of such companies.

That gets to the point. Lowering the bar solves whether companies can come; it does not solve whether coming is worth it. A technology firm deciding where to list cares less about whether the minimum market cap is THB 3 billion or THB 7.5 billion than about whether it gets a fair price and whether its shares actually trade afterwards. Southeast Asian markets have long been weighted towards traditional sectors such as finance and property. Investors have limited experience pricing semiconductors or robotics, and the order books are thin. Drop the threshold without fixing pricing and liquidity, and the result is a batch of companies listed at depressed valuations.

Hong Kong offers the contrast. EY's July report noted that IPO proceeds there were already up more than 60 per cent in the first half against the same period of 2025, with hard technology — AI infrastructure, semiconductors, robotics, advanced manufacturing — increasingly defining what comes to market. The gap is not in the rules. It is in market depth.

There are bright spots. As of mid-August, Singaporean chip testing equipment maker AEM Holdings had seen its share price jump nearly 500 per cent this year, while Delta Electronics Thailand, a maker and exporter of power supplies and electronic equipment, was up more than 50 per cent. Malaysian semiconductor design company SkyeChip went public on the local bourse in May. These examples prove Southeast Asia is not short of good companies. It is short of a sector effect.

## What the exchanges are really competing for

Both exchanges produced pipeline numbers. Earlier this month SGX disclosed a slate of around 50 companies at various stages of engagement and preparation, a third of them in technology, advanced manufacturing and digital infrastructure. Last month, medical services provider Foundation Healthcare Holdings raised SGD 242 million (US$190.5 million) through its IPO. The Thai bourse disclosed a near-term pipeline of ten companies, five approved and five under review, and expects total new listings across its main and secondary boards this year at least to match last year's 18.

The numbers are improving. The nature of the problem has not changed. What Southeast Asian exchanges are really competing for is not underwriting fees but pricing power — the ability of an economy to price its own technology companies, which determines their cost of capital, their capacity to acquire, and ultimately who owns them. As long as the region's best companies are priced in New York or Hong Kong, local markets receive only second-hand liquidity and a little residue.

So lowering the bar in Bangkok and building a bridge in Singapore are, in essence, the same gesture: make capital stay at home a little longer. The first bets that supply-chain repositioning will produce a wave of companies genuinely manufacturing in Thailand. The second bets that Asian companies will eventually need an Asian capital market on-ramp. Neither bet is small, and neither has come due yet.

The direction is right, though. A Southeast Asia that has factories but no capital market will only ever capture the manufacturing half of the profit.

## Further reading

**Initial public offering (IPO)**: The first sale of a company's shares to the public and its listing on an exchange. It is the main channel for corporate fundraising and early-investor exit, and a core measure of how active a capital market is.

**Depository receipt**: A tradable certificate issued by a custodian bank representing shares in a foreign listed company, letting local investors buy overseas equities without cross-border accounts. The Singapore Depository Receipts launched by SGX in July 2026 covered SpaceX, Grab and Sea.

**Market liquidity**: The ability to buy or sell an asset quickly at a price close to the prevailing market price. Thin liquidity depresses valuations and amplifies volatility, and is the hardest structural weakness for emerging-market exchanges trying to attract technology companies.
