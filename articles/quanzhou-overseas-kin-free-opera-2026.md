# 免费看戏唤醒乡愁：泉州借闽南戏曲留住海外侨亲

以“古戏新声·海丝薪传”为主题的2026年闽南戏剧周，近日在福建泉州持续上演。两百多场演出散布在古城的街巷、剧场与广场，梨园戏、高甲戏、木偶戏、南音等闽南语系剧种轮番登台。与往届不同，这一届多了一层明确的“任务”：泉州同步启动海外侨亲“欢迎回家”计划，广邀海外侨胞返乡免费看戏、共叙乡情。计划将一直延续到2027年2月，侨亲凭有效证明即可享受多项观演优惠与免费预约。

表面看，这是一场再寻常不过的家乡待客。但把它放进海外华侨华人的代际图谱里，味道就不一样了。

## 乡音：离散身份最割不断的锚

日本泉州商会副会长常楹在海外生活了十九年。她说，外婆是歌仔戏（Gezaixi）演员，自己从小浸在戏味里，童年最深的记忆就是节日里的戏台。可这些年节奏太快，“几乎没有渠道接触南音、歌仔戏等闽南本土戏曲”。一句熟悉的泉腔，就能让世界福建青年菲律宾联会创会会长王荣忠“勾起万千思乡情愫”；东盟福建社团联合总会轮值主席蔡永宁更是直白：身在海外，每逢南音响起，脑海里就浮起家乡戏台的热闹。

这揭示了一个常被忽略的事实：对远渡重洋的闽南人而言，方言与戏曲不是点缀，而是身份本身。当第二、第三代华侨在居住国说母语、读当地书，闽南语（泉腔）往往只在戏文里存活。换句话说，戏台成了方言的“方舟”——保住戏，就保住了那一口乡音，也保住了“我是谁”的答案。

## 从侨汇到乡情：侨务逻辑的转向

过去谈侨务，绕不开侨汇、投资与捐赠。那是实打实的经济账。可今天的中国早已不是靠侨汇过日子的年代，海外侨胞的财富也在当地扎根成熟。于是，“情感”开始取代“汇款”，成为新的经营对象。

泉州这套“欢迎回家”计划，本质上是把乡愁制度化：不是节庆那天热热闹闹演一场就散，而是用长达数月的窗口、用免费门票这种低门槛的诚意，把侨亲与故土的关系，从“偶尔回来”变成“常想回来”。这对侨乡而言是笔划算的账——用一场戏的成本，换回的是跨越山海的情感契约。

## 戏窝子的底气：文化资本变软实力

泉州敢这么玩，靠的是家底。作为南戏重要发祥地与活态传承地，这座城孕育了梨园戏、提线木偶戏、高甲戏等一批国家级乃至世界级非遗。它自称“戏窝子”，底气就在这里。

更妙的是“海丝”叙事的叠加。本届戏剧周设了“丝海牵缘·和谐共生”海丝国际戏剧交流专场、“同乡同音·南地留珍”多剧种折子戏专场，把闽南戏曲摆进“海上丝绸之路”的框架里。于是地方戏不只是地方戏，成了联通东盟闽籍社团的文化货币——新加坡、菲律宾等地的团体同台，演的既是戏，也是一圈圈以乡音为纽带的华人网络。

## 制度化的“欢迎回家”：柔性治理的一招

把一次节庆拉长成跨年的常态计划，值得玩味。它传递的信号很清晰：回家不是特权，而是被持续邀请的权利。当“欢迎回家”变成可预期、可预约、可重复的安排，侨亲对故土的归属感就被悄悄“养”成了习惯。

这比单纯喊口号管用。情感这种东西，最怕一阵风；一旦做成机制，就会自己生长。泉州显然想明白了这一点。

## 真正的考题在年轻人

新加坡传统艺术中心艺术总监蔡碧霞这次来泉演了歌仔戏《芙蓉仙子》。她的话点出了隐忧：“希望吸引更多年轻人观看、爱上中华传统戏曲。”这句话的反面，正是海外戏曲最大的危机——观众在老去。

没有年轻一代接续，乡音终会褪色，“文化根脉”四个字就悬了空。所以这场“免费看戏”的真正对手，不是别的剧团，而是短视频、流行乐与异国日常对年轻华侨的争夺。泉州把戏台搭给长辈，也得想清楚怎么把年轻人请进门。

## 延伸阅读

**乡音（Dialect）**：闽南语系方言（含泉腔、厦语）是东南亚闽籍华侨的身份标记；当日常用语被居住国语言替代，戏曲成为少数仍完整保存方言韵律的载体。

**侨乡（Qiaoxiang）**：以福建、广东、海南等地为代表，历史上大量居民下南洋谋生，形成“本土—海外”双向依存的社会结构，侨务政策长期以之为基础。

**南音（Nanyin）**：发源于闽南的世界级非物质文化遗产，以泉州方言演唱，被誉为“中国音乐活化石”，是海外闽籍社群重要的乡愁符号。

# Free Shows, Awakened Homesickness: How Quanzhou's Minnan Opera Keeps Its Overseas Kin Close

The 2026 Minnan Drama Week, billed under the slogan "Ancient Opera, New Voice; Maritime Silk Road Heritage," has been unfolding across the Fujian city of Quanzhou. More than 200 performances spill out of the old town's alleyways, theatres and squares, with Liyuan opera, Gaoja opera, puppetry and Nanyin — all Minnan-language forms — taking the stage in rotation. What sets this edition apart is a deliberate second purpose: alongside the festival, Quanzhou launched an "overseas compatriots, welcome home" programme, inviting emigrants back to watch opera for free and reconnect with their hometown. The scheme runs all the way to February 2027; with proof of status, returning kin can claim discounted or free tickets.

On the surface this looks like ordinary hometown hospitality. Placed against the generational map of the overseas Chinese, it reads rather differently.

## The dialect: the anchor that will not break

Chang Ying, vice-chair of the Quanzhou chamber of commerce in Japan, has lived abroad for nineteen years. Her grandmother was a Gezaixi (Taiwanese opera) actress; she grew up steeped in the sounds of the stage, and her sharpest childhood memory is the festival theatre. Yet in recent years, she told China News Service, the pace of life left "almost no channel to access Nanyin, Gezaixi and other indigenous Minnan performing arts." A single familiar Quanzhou cadence is enough to stir "countless longings for home" in Wang Rongzhong, founding chair of the World Federation of Fujianese Youth in the Philippines. Cai Yongning, rotating chair of the ASEAN Federation of Fujian Clans, is blunter still: no matter how far he travels, the gentle tune of Nanyin conjures the bustle of his hometown stage.

Here is the fact easily missed: for Minnan people who crossed the seas, dialect and opera are not decoration — they are identity itself. As the second and third generations grow up speaking the language of their country of residence, the Minnan tongue (the Quanzhou cadence) often survives only inside the libretto. In other words, the stage has become the ark of the dialect: keep the opera, and you keep the accent — and the answer to "who am I."

## From remittances to sentiment: the turn in diaspora affairs

Talk about "qiaowu" (diaspora affairs) used to mean remittances, investment and donations — a hard economic ledger. But China today is no longer the country that lived on overseas remittances, and diaspora wealth has taken root and matured abroad. So "emotion" is quietly replacing "remittance" as the new object of management.

Quanzhou's "welcome home" plan is, in essence, the institutionalisation of nostalgia. It is not a single festive show that scatters once the lanterns go dark; it is a months-long window and a low-threshold gift of free tickets, meant to turn the bond between emigrants and their native soil from "occasional return" into "frequent longing to return." For a qiaoxiang (emigrant homeland) this is a cheap bargain — one opera's cost, in exchange for an emotional contract that crosses mountains and seas.

## The "theatre cradle's" confidence: cultural capital as soft power

Quanzhou can play this game because it has the stock. As a cradle of Southern drama and a living heritage site, the city gave birth to Liyuan opera, string-puppet theatre and Gaoja opera — national, even world-class, intangible treasures. When it calls itself a "theatre nest," the confidence is real.

Neatly layered on top is the "Maritime Silk Road" narrative. This year's week staged a "Silk-Sea Bond, Harmonious Coexistence" international exchange showcase and a "Same Hometown, Same Sound" highlights gala, placing Minnan opera inside the maritime-silk-road frame. Local opera thus becomes more than local opera: it turns into the cultural currency that links Fujian-native associations across ASEAN. Troupes from Singapore, the Philippines and elsewhere share the stage, performing not only theatre but the web of Chinese networks knotted by a shared accent.

## Institutionalised "welcome home": a move in soft governance

Stretching a festival into a year-spanning routine is worth noting. The signal is clear: coming home is not a privilege but a right that is continuously extended. When "welcome home" becomes predictable, bookable and repeatable, the sense of belonging to the native place is quietly "cultivated" into a habit.

This works better than slogans. Emotion fears nothing so much as a passing wind; once made into a mechanism, it grows on its own. Quanzhou, plainly, has figured this out.

## The real exam is the young

Cai Bixia, artistic director of Singapore's Traditional Arts Centre, performed the Gezaixi piece "The Hibiscus Fairy" in Quanzhou this time. Her remark names the unease: she hopes "to attract more young people to watch and fall in love with Chinese traditional opera." The flip side of that hope is the biggest crisis facing overseas opera — its audience is ageing.

Without a younger generation to carry it forward, the accent will fade, and the phrase "cultural root" will hang empty. So the true opponent of this "free opera" drive is not another troupe but the short-video feed, pop music and the everyday pull of a foreign land competing for young overseas Chinese. Quanzhou has built the stage for the elders; it must also work out how to usher the young through the door.

## Further reading

**Dialect**: The Minnan-language family (including the Quanzhou cadence and Xiamen speech) marks the identity of Southeast Asian Fujianese. As daily speech yields to host-country languages, opera remains one of the few carriers that preserve the dialect's full musical system.

**Qiaoxiang**: Epitomised by Fujian, Guangdong and Hainan, these are homelands from which waves of people sailed to Nanyang (Southeast Asia) to make a living, producing a "home–abroad" dual-dependent social structure that has long anchored diaspora policy.

**Nanyin**: A UNESCO-listed intangible heritage born in Minnan, sung in the Quanzhou dialect and hailed as a "living fossil of Chinese music" — a central nostalgia symbol for overseas Fujianese communities.
