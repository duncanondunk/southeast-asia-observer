# 一部"外国代理人法"，为何急着为2028年大选上锁

10月中旬，菲律宾（Philippines）移民局与军方突击队在马尼拉（Manila）奎松市索科罗村一栋设施内逮捕了76名外国人——74名中国籍、2名缅甸籍。这栋建筑离菲律宾武装部队总部和国防部所在地阿吉纳尔多营仅一步之遥。当局怀疑此处与菲律宾离岸博彩（POGO）有关，更令人不安的是，怀疑它牵涉间谍活动。这场突袭成了菲律宾安全叙事最新的一处爆点：官方与在野的一种论调是，外部势力——尤其是中国——正悄悄为2028年总统大选布局。国会正仓促推动一部《外国影响与干涉法》。但在一个本就充斥着恩庇政治与虚假信息的民主体里，这部法很可能既对准间谍，也瞄准异见。

## 突袭撕开的安全焦虑

突袭地点本身就充满象征意味。索科罗村那栋设施，与阿吉纳尔多营隔着一条街。那里驻扎着菲律宾武装部队总部和国防部，是菲律宾军事与国家安全机器的心脏。负责西菲律宾海（南海）事务的军方发言人、退役海军少将罗伊·文森特·特立尼达在记者会上说，相关机构"必须彻查"，并称此事"将带来非常严重的国家安全影响"。一句话，就把一栋涉嫌博彩的民宅，抬高成了国家安全事件。这种叙事的升级并不偶然：近年菲律宾军方把重心从内部治安转向外部防御，新的安全挑战之一，就是所谓"通过信息行动影响民意"。当军事语言开始主导政治讨论，法律的边界往往随之模糊。

## 三亿比索的疑云

真正把这部法案推上快车道的是一笔钱。菲律宾国家安全委员会正在调查一笔据称来自中国大陆与香港企业的3.19亿比索汇款，收款方是卡莱88食品公司（Cale88 Foods）——其总裁兼董事长正是副总统莎拉·杜特尔特的丈夫曼纳塞斯·卡皮奥。反洗钱委员会（AMLC）主任罗内尔·布埃纳文图拉在副总统弹劾审理中作证说，这些汇款之所以被标记，是因为"看上去没有正当用途"。国防部长吉尔伯托·特奥多罗要求"彻底调查"，称其"事关国家安全的严重后果"；总统小马科斯则在10月7日表示，此事"最令人不安"，若属实，将是"对共和国最高层面的威胁"。莎拉·杜特尔特的回应干脆利落："我们被禁止和中国做生意了吗？因为这成了国家安全风险？"一句话，把安保逻辑的反噬摆上了台面。

## 一部酝酿中的"外国代理人法"

菲律宾并非没有相关立法。现行的《外国代理人法》已要求外国代理人登记并披露部分信息。但朝野现在要的是一部升级版——《外国影响与干涉法》，把现行法的尺度大幅拉长。据披露，一个工作组正把至少8项相关提案合并为单一法案，提交众议院。新法拟把"线上影响行动"也纳入管制，包括所谓"水军农场"，以及"代表外国个人或实体操纵民意以谋政治目的"的本地或外国代理人。违法者可能面临监禁，以及最高2000万比索（约36万美元，约合230万元人民币）的罚款。前最高法院大法官安东尼奥·卡皮奥公开敦促国会加速通过。从"登记披露"到"刑事重罚"，这一步跨得不可谓不大。

## 谁在害怕"亲中候选人"

法案背后的选举焦虑一览无余。多名官员的担忧很直白：外国代理人可能在2028年大选中扶持"亲中候选人"。这种担忧并非空穴来风——此前国防部长特奥多罗就断言，在"中国日报"把菲律宾人画成猴子的视频风波之后，打"亲中"牌去竞选"已经不可行"。亚洲脉搏（Pulse Asia）民调显示，副总统莎拉·杜特尔特、参议员蒙·吴（Bong Go）与罗宾·帕迪利亚分别在2028年正副总统与参议员选情中领先，而他们都与前总统罗德里戈·杜特尔特的政治谱系相连，后者被广为报道在对华政策上更趋友好。于是，"中国"在选战话语里被悄悄塑造成一种"负资产"：与其说是防范干涉，不如说是在选前先把对手贴上"亲中"标签。

## 舆论战：从标语到"水军农场"

特立尼达还透露，外国影响的手法已经转向国内议题——比如副总统弹劾程序、防洪腐败丑闻，用菲律宾语内容"更难被察觉"地渗透。他的判断颇具玩味："他们的目的不是让我们相信它们，而是让我们谁都不信。"换句话说，真正的威胁被描述成"对政府与政府机构信任的侵蚀"。更值得警惕的是地方内政部长乔恩维克·雷穆拉在一次访谈中援引情报报告称，某个未点名的"超级大国"在其薪水上养着"超过4万名博主、网络红人与评论员"散布假新闻——他既未指认国家，也未提供任何佐证。当"4万水军"这样的数字可以不负责任地抛向舆论场，反干涉的立法边界，就已经踩在了寒蝉效应的边缘。

## 北京（Beijing）的回应与马尼拉的困境

北京方面早已否认干涉。2025年4月，中国驻马尼拉大使馆称，中国"无意干涉菲律宾选举"，并反指菲方一些政客利用此类指控谋取政治利益。这一来一回，恰好点出马尼拉的困境：安全关切或许是真实的，但当反干涉话语被选举周期劫持，它很容易从"防御外患"滑向"整肃异见"。一部把"外国来源""外国影响"定义得过于宽泛的法律，可能让正常的涉华学术讨论、经贸往来乃至华人社区的日常联系，都被套上"国安"的审视。更要紧的是，对"外国影响"最响亮的声讨，恰恰发生在马尼拉不断深化与华盛顿安保绑定的时刻——安全化的矛头，似乎总是选择性地朝向某一方。

## 观察者说：安全叙事下的民主代价

这部法案生于真实的焦虑，却可能被框定成把"外国关联言论"一并入罪。一个被离岸博彩、间谍疑云与选举算计搅在一起的安全叙事，正在把每一条对华联系都变成潜在的"国安负债"。美国当年推出《外国代理人登记法》（FARA），初衷也是透明，结果常被批评沦为政治工具；菲律宾若把门槛设得更低、罚则更重，恐怕只会给滥用留下更大空间。最讽刺的莫过于：当马尼拉把"外部影响"视作心腹大患时，它正在更紧地把自己绑进另一套外部同盟的安全叙事里。一个把"外国影响"禁得太宽的社会，到头来可能连自己的公民都不敢信任。民主的韧性，终究不该靠把邻居当成间谍来维系。

## 延伸阅读

**外国代理人登记法（FARA）**：美国1938年立的法律，要求代表外国政府或利益方从事政治活动的代理人登记并披露，近年常被批评沦为政治工具。

**菲律宾离岸博彩（POGO）**：曾在前总统杜特尔特任内迅速扩张的线上博彩产业，长期被怀疑为洗钱、诈骗乃至间谍活动的掩护。

**亚洲脉搏（Pulse Asia）**：菲律宾本地民调机构，其2028年选情调查常被政界与媒体引为风向标。

# Manila's foreign agents law: securitising the 2028 vote

In mid-October, Philippine immigration and military operatives raided a facility in Barangay Socorro, Quezon City, and arrested 76 foreigners — 74 Chinese nationals and two from Myanmar. The building sits a stone's throw from Camp Aguinaldo, the headquarters of the Armed Forces of the Philippines and the Department of National Defence. Authorities suspected the site was linked to Philippine offshore gaming operators (Pogos) and, more ominously, to espionage. The raid has become the latest flashpoint in a hardening Philippine security narrative: that outside powers, above all China, are quietly working to shape the country's politics ahead of the 2028 presidential election. Legislators are now rushing a Foreign Influence and Interference Act. But in a democracy already saturated with patronage politics and disinformation, the law risks becoming a weapon aimed as much at dissent as at spies.

## The raid that exposed the anxiety

The location alone was symbolic. The Socorro facility stood across the street from Camp Aguinaldo, the nerve centre of the Philippine military and national-security establishment. Roy Vincent Trinidad, a retired rear-admiral and the military spokesman for West Philippine Sea (South China Sea) matters, told reporters that the appropriate agencies "must look into this" and warned it "will have very serious national security implications". In a single sentence, a suspected gaming house was elevated into a national-security event. The escalation is no accident: as the armed forces pivot from internal policing to external defence, one of the new security challenges, in official parlance, is "influence operations" that seek to sway public opinion. When military language starts to dominate political debate, the line of the law tends to blur.

## The 319-million-peso cloud

What truly accelerated the bill was money. The National Security Council is investigating some 319 million pesos in transfers from corporations in mainland China and Hong Kong to Cale88 Foods, whose president and board chairman was Manases Carpio — the husband of Vice-President Sara Duterte-Carpio. Ronel Buenaventura, director of the Anti-Money-Laundering Council, testified during the vice-president's impeachment proceedings that the transfers were flagged because they appeared to have "no justifiable purpose". Defence Secretary Gilberto Teodoro Jnr called for a "thorough investigation" with "serious implications to our national security"; President Ferdinand Marcos Jnr said on 7 October that he found the matter "most disturbing" and, if proven true, a "threat at the highest level to the Republic". Duterte-Carpio's retort was blunt: "Are we forbidden from doing business with China? Because it is a national security risk?" In one line, she laid bare the boomerang of the security logic.

## A foreign agents law in the making

The Philippines is no stranger to such legislation. The current Foreign Agents Act already requires registration and some disclosure. But what the legislature now wants is an upgraded Foreign Influence and Interference Act that stretches the threshold dramatically. A working group is consolidating at least eight related measures into a single bill for the House of Representatives. The new law would extend to online influence operations, including so-called troll farms, and to local or foreign agents acting "on behalf of foreign persons or entities to manipulate public opinion for political ends". Offenders could face prison terms and fines of up to 20 million pesos (about US$318,000). Retired Supreme Court associate justice Antonio Carpio has publicly urged Congress to speed passage. The step from "register and disclose" to "imprison and fine heavily" is, to put it mildly, a large one.

## Who fears the "pro-China" candidate

The electoral anxiety behind the bill is plain. Several officials worry, quite openly, that foreign agents could bankroll "pro-China" candidates in the 2028 race. The fear is not entirely abstract — Defence Secretary Teodoro has argued that, after a state-linked Chinese outlet depicted Filipinos as monkeys in a video tied to the South China Sea arbitral award, running on a "pro-China" platform had become "not viable". A Pulse Asia survey shows Vice-President Sara Duterte, Senator Christopher "Bong" Go and Senator Robin Padilla leading the 2028 presidential, vice-presidential and senatorial contests respectively, all tied to the political lineage of former president Rodrigo Duterte, widely reported to have tilted foreign policy toward Beijing. Thus "China" is quietly recast in campaign discourse as a liability: less a safeguard against interference than a label to pin on opponents before the vote.

## The information war: from slogans to troll farms

Trinidad also revealed that foreign-influence tactics had shifted toward domestic issues — the vice-president's impeachment proceedings and flood-control scandals — using Filipino-language content that is, in his words, "more difficult to detect". His framing is telling: "The objective is not even for us to believe them. The objective is for us not to believe anybody." In other words, the real threat is described as the erosion of trust in government and its institutions. More worrying still, local government secretary Jonvic Remulla cited intelligence reports on a talk show claiming an unnamed "superpower" had "more than 40,000 bloggers and influencers and commentators" on its payroll to spread fake news — without naming the country or offering any evidence. When a figure like "40,000 trolls" can be lobbed into the public square without accountability, the boundary of anti-interference law is already teetering on the edge of a chilling effect.

## Beijing's denial and Manila's dilemma

Beijing has long denied interference. In April 2025, the Chinese embassy in Manila said China had "no interest in interfering in elections in the Philippines", accusing some Philippine politicians of using such allegations for political gain. The exchange lays bare Manila's dilemma: the security concern may be genuine, but once the anti-interference narrative is hijacked by the electoral cycle, it slides easily from "defending against outside meddling" to "policing dissent". A law that defines "foreign source" and "foreign influence" too broadly could subject ordinary scholarly debate on China, legitimate business ties and even the daily connections of the ethnic Chinese community to national-security scrutiny. The ironies pile up: the loudest securitisation of "foreign influence" arrives precisely as Manila deepens its security alignment with Washington — the spear of suspicion always seems to point selectively in one direction.

## Commentary: the democratic cost of the security narrative

The bill is born of real anxiety, yet it risks being framed to criminalise "foreign-linked speech" wholesale. A security narrative tangled up with offshore gaming, espionage suspicion and electoral calculation is turning every China connection into a potential national-security liability. The United States enacted its Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA) in 1938 for transparency, only to be repeatedly criticised for weaponising it; if the Philippines sets the bar lower and the penalties heavier, it will simply leave more room for abuse. The deepest irony is this: as Manila treats "external influence" as an existential threat, it is binding itself more tightly into the security narrative of another external alliance. A society that bans "foreign influence" too broadly may end up distrusting its own citizens. The resilience of a democracy, in the end, should not depend on treating one's neighbour as a spy.

## Further reading

**Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA):** A 1938 US law requiring agents acting for foreign governments or interests in political activities to register and disclose; in recent years it has been criticised as a political tool.

**Philippine offshore gaming operators (Pogos):** An online-gambling industry that expanded rapidly under former president Rodrigo Duterte, long suspected of laundering, fraud and even espionage cover.

**Pulse Asia:** A Philippine polling outfit whose 2028 electoral surveys are treated by politicians and media as a bellwether.
