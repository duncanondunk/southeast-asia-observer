# 十年磨一剑，布鲁塞尔签字：印尼（Indonesia）—欧盟自贸协定真正的价码

据联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）报道，11月21日，欧盟与印度尼西亚（Indonesia）在布鲁塞尔正式签署《印尼—欧盟全面经济伙伴关系协定》与附属的《投资保护协定》。欧盟委员会主席冯德莱恩与印尼经济统筹部长艾尔朗加出席签约仪式。这不是一次例行的贸易签字：谈判2016年7月启动、2025年9月收官、拖到2026年11月才落笔，前后近10年。签得慢，恰恰说明双方争的不是税率表上那几个百分点，而是谁来定义游戏规则。

## 签字在布鲁塞尔，生效要等2027

协定签了，并不等于马上能用。欧洲议会要投票，印尼也要走完自己的内部核准程序，最快预计要到2027年初才能生效。冯德莱恩把它称作「欧盟在亚洲达成的最雄心勃勃的协议之一」，并强调双方合起来是一个拥有7亿3000万人口的市场。她感谢印尼总统普拉博沃（Prabowo Subianto）的政治决断，并透露普拉博沃将于12月11日访问布鲁塞尔，随后两人同赴迈阿密参加二十国集团峰会。

把签字与核准分开看，才不会被仪式感带跑。签字是政治承诺，核准才是法律义务。对布鲁塞尔而言，这份协定要过欧洲议会那一关；对雅加达（Jakarta）而言，国会层面同样要摆平。近10年的谈判桌上攒下来的文本，任何一处被重新打开，成本都不低。这才是「最快2027年初生效」这句官方措辞里真正的信息量。

## 98.5%与90%：两组数字不是同一笔账

欧盟方面的口径很漂亮：协定将取消印尼对欧盟商品98.5%的关税。印尼进口商品平均税率约10%，个别税目高达150%，降下来之后，欧盟出口商每年可省下逾6亿欧元（约48亿元人民币）关税。欧盟对印尼出口2025年为102亿欧元（约82亿元人民币），布鲁塞尔预计，15年内这一数字将提高近40%。

印尼方面的口径则是另一套：约90%的印尼输欧商品享受免税待遇，涵盖棕榈油、鞋类、纺织品和渔业产品。艾尔朗加说，出口商得到的好处「具体且可衡量」。欧盟在印尼的投资存量247亿欧元（约198亿元人民币），超过1.5万家欧盟中小企业向印尼出口，相关出口支撑近20万个欧盟就业岗位。欧盟委员会还预测，到2042年，欧盟国内生产总值将比无协定情形高出160亿欧元（约128亿元人民币），印尼则高出270亿欧元（约216亿元人民币）。

两组数字看着对称，实则各算各的账。欧盟拿到的，是进入东南亚（Southeast Asia）最大经济体的准入与可预期性；印尼拿到的，是免税进入欧盟市场的名额，以及更关键的——投资保护与产业链位置。关税归零只是入场券，不是终点。真正值得追问的是：购物车里的货物能换回多少工厂、多少技术、多少本地附加值？

## 棕榈油与镍：卡住十年的是标准，不是税率

谈判拖了近10年，卡点从来不在关税表，而在标准。棕榈油与毁林争议是最大的绊脚石，最终文本附了专门的棕榈油议定书，把可持续贸易与监管合作写进机制；欧盟保护的221个地理标志也进了协定，罗克福干酪（Roquefort）这类名字不能再被随意套用。农产品一侧，欧盟没有为稻米、糖、鸡蛋、鲜香蕉和乙醇开放新准入，大蒜、蘑菇、甜玉米、木薯淀粉和高糖产品只给有限配额。所谓自由贸易，从来都是挑着自由。

另一条主线是关键矿产。印尼是全球镍、钴的重要产地，欧盟把「供应链多元化」「能源与原料来源分散」直接写进协定目标，还要求矿业项目做环境影响评估。协定同时开放电动车、可再生能源和电子行业的投资环境，并在数字贸易上承诺不对电子传输征收关税——这是印尼首次作出此类承诺。换句话说，欧盟想要的不只是便宜的咖啡和奶酪，而是稳定的电池原料与绿色产业链入口；印尼想换的也不是几个税目，而是把「挖矿」升级为「制造」的资格。

## 规则出口：钱袋收紧，布鲁塞尔改用标准开路

把这份协定放回欧盟当前的处境里看，逻辑就更清楚了。布鲁塞尔这几年的东南亚攻势一直卡在钱上：多年期财政框架谈不拢，对外经费被净出资国压减，基建与清洁能源项目排队等钱。支票开不出来，规则就成了最趁手的替代品。

协定的可持续章节把《巴黎协定》与国际劳工组织核心公约设为约束性承诺，允许公民社会监督执行，还搭起监管对话平台。这套东西对欧洲企业是「可预期性」，对印尼小农和矿企则是实实在在的合规成本：棕榈油要证明不毁林，矿山要交环境影响评估，劳工标准要能被外部审视。据联合早报报道，欧盟强调这家协定有助于「可持续增长」；但谁来为转型买单，报道没有回答，欧盟的文件也没有回答。规则不是免费的，账单往往落在出口的一方。

这也是欧盟与中国的差别所在。北京（Beijing）在东南亚谈的是港口、铁路、电站和融资，布鲁塞尔如今更擅长输出标准、认证与法律框架。两种路径各有受众，但对当地企业而言，标准往往比贷款更难消化——贷款可以还，标准改不了。

## 雅加达的算法：让供应链多一个选项

印尼近年的经济外交有一条清晰的线：用市场换位置。镍矿出口禁令、下游化要求、本土加工比例，本质都是要把资源租金留在国内。与加拿大签协定，解决的是「第一个北美伙伴」的象征问题；与欧盟签协定，拿到的却是投资保护、技术准入和标准认证，重量级完全不同。

普拉博沃政府的同时操作也值得玩味：一边与中国保持全面战略对话与经贸合作，一边与欧盟签下近10年磨成的协定，还在推进与加拿大的安排。这不是选边站，是把筹码摊在桌面上，让各方竞争自己的注意力。对小国而言，被需要比被偏爱更安全。

但风险同样明确。投资保护条款可能挤压印尼调整产业政策的余地：今天鼓励本地化，明天被投资者以「不公平歧视」告上仲裁庭，并非没有先例。欧盟在电动车与可再生能源领域的技术优势，也可能让「下游化」变成另一轮依赖——从出口镍矿，变成出口贴着欧洲标准的电池部件。门打开了，站在门口的是谁，才是问题。

## 中国的影子：不点名的第三方

欧盟的官方声明没有指名道姓，但冯德莱恩承认，这份协定是欧盟面对美国与中国挑战、推动贸易多元化的一部分。第三方就在字缝里。

对欧盟来说，印尼的镍与钴是电池产业链的刚需，而这条链目前高度集中于中国加工环节。把印尼纳入自己的协定网络，等于给关键原料多开一个来源。对印尼来说，中印尼（China–Indonesia）合作已经很深，雅万高铁（Jakarta-Bandung High-Speed Railway）、镍冶炼园区与全面战略对话机制都是既成事实；欧盟协定提供的，是一个不必把鸡蛋放在同一个篮子里的机会。

不过，把这份协定读成「印尼倒向欧盟」就过于戏剧化了。标准由谁定、技术从哪来、融资谁给，决定了产业链的实际归属。协定能改变的是法律文本，改不了地理与经济重力。雅加达真正要的，是在两种模式之间保留议价权，而不是换一个依附对象。

## 批准才是硬仗：议会、国会与12月的布鲁塞尔

接下来的看点是批准。欧洲议会要不要为棕榈油与劳工条款争执一轮，印尼国会会不会在本地产业压力下重开文本，都是未知数。官方给出的最快时间表是2027年初临时适用，这个「最快」两个字，本身就说明弹性。

12月11日普拉博沃访问布鲁塞尔，随后与冯德莱恩同赴迈阿密参加二十国集团峰会，是这份协定的政治背书。欧盟在东盟（ASEAN）的朋友名单也在变长：新加坡、越南（Vietnam）已签，泰国（Thailand）在谈，菲律宾（Philippines）去年10月宣布达成实质性协议。印尼是这块版图上最大的经济体，补上它，欧盟的印太贸易网才算成形。

但对东南亚来说，协定签下来只是开始。98.5%的关税取消能写进新闻标题，小农能不能卖出更多棕榈油、矿企能不能达到环境标准、本地工人能不能分到下游化的收益，才是十年谈判最终要回答的问题。签字仪式上的掌声，从来不等于工厂里的订单。

## 延伸阅读

- **棕榈油议定书（Palm Oil Protocol）**：欧盟—印尼协定中处理棕榈油可持续性与监管合作的专门文件，是谈判多年最棘手的议题之一。
- **地理标志（Geographical Indications）**：受法律保护的产品名称，只有来自特定产地的商品才能使用；本次协定保护欧盟221个此类名称。
- **投资保护协定（Investment Protection Agreement）**：与自贸协定配套的独立条约，约束签约国对投资者的待遇，并常设投资者—国家争端解决机制。

# A Decade in the Making, Signed in Brussels: The Real Price of the Indonesia–EU FTA

According to Lianhe Zaobao, the European Union and Indonesia signed the Indonesia–EU Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA) and an accompanying Investment Protection Agreement (IPA) in Brussels on 21 November. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and Indonesian Coordinating Minister for Economic Affairs Airlangga Hartarto attended the ceremony. This was no routine trade signing. Negotiations opened in July 2016, were concluded in September 2025, and were only put to paper in November 2026 — nearly a decade. The slowness is the point: what the two sides were bargaining over was not a few percentage points on a tariff schedule, but who gets to define the rules.

## Signed in Brussels, in force only in 2027

A signature is not the same as an operative agreement. The European Parliament must approve the deal, and Indonesia must complete its own internal ratification; the earliest realistic entry into force is early 2027. Von der Leyen called it "one of the most ambitious deals the EU has concluded in Asia" and noted that together the two sides represent a market of 730 million people. She thanked President Prabowo Subianto for his political commitment and said he would visit Brussels on 11 December before both leaders travelled to Miami for the G20 summit.

It is worth keeping the signing and the ratification separate, or the ceremony does the thinking for us. A signature is a political promise; ratification is a legal obligation. For Brussels, the text must survive a vote in the European Parliament; for Jakarta, it must clear the legislature at home. Any provision reopened after a decade of negotiations carries a price. That is the real information packed into the official phrase "as early as early 2027".

## 98.5% and 90%: two different ledgers

The EU's numbers are handsome: 98.5% of Indonesian tariffs on EU goods will be removed. Indonesia's average tariff is around 10%, with individual lines as high as 150%; eliminating them will save EU exporters more than €600 million (roughly US$700 million) a year in customs duties. EU exports to Indonesia stood at €10.2 billion (about US$12 billion) in 2025, and Brussels projects they will rise by nearly 40% within 15 years.

Jakarta tells a different story. Around 90% of Indonesian exports to the EU will become tariff-free, covering palm oil, footwear, textiles and fisheries products. Airlangga said the benefits for exporters were "concrete and measurable". EU investment stock in Indonesia is €24.7 billion (about US$29 billion); more than 15,000 EU small and medium-sized enterprises export to Indonesia, supporting nearly 200,000 EU jobs. The Commission also projects that by 2042 EU GDP will be €16 billion (roughly US$19 billion) higher and Indonesian GDP €27 billion (about US$32 billion) higher than without the deal.

The two sets of figures look symmetrical but are not. Brussels gets access to Southeast Asia's largest economy and the predictability its exporters crave. Jakarta gets preferential entry to the EU market and, more importantly, investment protection and a place in industrial supply chains. Zero tariffs are an entrance ticket, not the prize. The question worth asking is how many factories, how much technology and how much local value added can be bought with a shopping cart full of cheese and machinery.

## Palm oil and nickel: what stalled the talks was standards, not tariffs

The decade-long delay had almost nothing to do with tariff rates and everything to do with standards. Palm oil and deforestation were the biggest stumbling blocks. The final text includes a dedicated Protocol on Palm Oil, embedding sustainable trade practices and regulatory cooperation, and protects 221 EU geographical indications — names like Roquefort can no longer be freely appropriated. On sensitive farm goods, the EU won no new access for rice, sugar, eggs, fresh bananas or ethanol, with only limited quotas for garlic, mushrooms, sweetcorn, manioc starch and high-sugar products. "Free trade" has always been selective about what it frees.

The second axis is critical minerals. Indonesia is a leading producer of nickel and cobalt, and the EU wrote supply-chain diversification and diversified sources of energy and raw materials directly into the agreement's objectives, requiring environmental impact assessments for mining projects. The deal also improves the regulatory environment for investment in electric vehicles, renewable energy and electronics, and bans customs duties on electronic transmissions — a first for Indonesia. In other words, Brussels wants more than cheaper coffee and cheese: it wants stable access to battery inputs and a green industrial chain. Jakarta wants more than tariff lines: it wants the credentials to move from extraction to manufacturing.

## Exporting rules: with its purse tightening, Brussels leads with standards

Seen against the EU's current predicament, the logic is clearer. Brussels' recent Southeast Asian offensive has been constrained by money: the multiannual financial framework is deadlocked, net-contributor governments are squeezing external funding, and Global Gateway-style infrastructure and energy projects queue for cash. When the chequebook is thin, rules become the most handy substitute.

The sustainability chapter makes the Paris Agreement and core International Labour Organization conventions binding commitments, gives civil society a role in monitoring implementation, and sets up a platform for regulatory dialogue. To European firms this is "predictability". To Indonesian smallholders and miners it is a concrete compliance bill: palm oil must be shown to be deforestation-free, mines must file environmental impact assessments, labour standards must survive external scrutiny. Lianhe Zaobao reported the EU's emphasis on "sustainable growth"; neither that report nor the Commission's documents say who pays for the transition. Rules are not free, and the invoice usually lands on the exporting side.

That is also where the EU differs from China. Beijing tends to arrive in Southeast Asia with ports, railways, power plants and financing. Brussels now specialises in standards, certification and legal frameworks. Both have an audience, but to local firms standards can be harder to digest than loans: debt can be repaid, specifications cannot be voted away.

## Jakarta's calculus: giving supply chains another option

Indonesia's economic diplomacy has followed a clear line in recent years: trade market access for industrial position. The nickel export ban, downstreaming requirements and local processing ratios all aim to keep resource rents at home. The Canada CEPA solved a symbolic problem — a first North American partner. The EU agreement delivers far more weight: investment protection, technology access and standards recognition.

The Prabowo government's simultaneous moves are telling. It maintains a Comprehensive Strategic Dialogue and deep trade ties with China, signs a decade-in-the-making deal with the EU, and has arranged a similar pact with Canada. This is not bandwagon diplomacy; it is putting chips on the table so that each partner competes for Jakarta's attention. For a mid-sized power, being needed is safer than being favoured.

The risks are equally clear. Investment protection provisions can narrow the room to change industrial policy: a government that mandates localisation today can find itself before an investor-state tribunal tomorrow, and such cases are not hypothetical. Europe's technological lead in electric vehicles and renewables may also turn "downstreaming" into a new dependency — from exporting nickel ore to exporting battery components built to European specifications. Opening the door is one thing; deciding who stands in it is another.

## The shadow of China: the unnamed third party

EU statements did not name names, but von der Leyen acknowledged that the deal forms part of Brussels' drive to diversify trade amid challenges from the United States and China. The third party is there in the gap between the lines.

For the EU, Indonesian nickel and cobalt are indispensable to battery supply chains that remain heavily concentrated in Chinese processing. Bringing Indonesia into its agreement network opens a second source of critical inputs. For Indonesia, cooperation with China is already deep — the Jakarta–Bandung high-speed railway, nickel smelting parks and the strategic dialogue are established facts. What the EU deal offers is the chance not to keep every egg in one basket.

But reading this as Jakarta "tilting towards Europe" is overdramatic. Who sets the standards, where the technology comes from and who provides the finance determine where the industrial chain actually sits. An agreement can rewrite legal text; it cannot redraw economic gravity. What Jakarta wants is bargaining power between two models, not a new patron.

## Ratification is the real fight: parliaments, Congress and Brussels in December

The next test is approval. Whether the European Parliament picks a fight over palm oil and labour clauses, and whether Indonesia's legislature reopens the text under pressure from domestic industries, remain open questions. The official timeline says provisional application as early as early 2027 — the word "early" carries its own elasticity.

Prabowo's 11 December visit to Brussels, followed by the G20 in Miami with von der Leyen, is the deal's political endorsement. The EU's list of partners in ASEAN is lengthening: Singapore and Vietnam have signed, Thailand is negotiating, and the Philippines announced a substantial agreement in October 2025. Indonesia is the largest economy in that map; without it, Brussels' Indo-Pacific trade network is incomplete.

For Southeast Asia, though, signing is only the beginning. The removal of 98.5% of tariffs fits in a headline. Whether smallholders can sell more palm oil, whether mines can meet environmental standards, and whether local workers share in the gains of downstreaming are the questions that a decade of negotiations was supposed to answer. Applause at a signing ceremony has never been the same thing as orders on a factory floor.

## Further reading

- **Palm Oil Protocol**: the dedicated instrument in the EU–Indonesia deal covering sustainable palm-oil trade and regulatory cooperation; one of the most contentious issues in the negotiations.
- **Geographical Indications**: legally protected product names reserved for goods from a specific origin; the agreement protects 221 such EU names.
- **Investment Protection Agreement**: a separate treaty accompanying the FTA, governing the treatment of investors and typically providing for investor–state dispute settlement.
