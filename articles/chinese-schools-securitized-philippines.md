# 每天两小时：当菲律宾华校的课程表变成一份安全档案

2026年7月29日，在马尼拉一场名为"马尼拉湾咖啡座谈"的论坛上，菲律宾国防部长特奥多罗（Gilberto Teodoro Jr.）呼吁加强审查外国学术与文化交流项目。他的直接由头，是美国智库"海光计划"（SeaLight）发布的一份调查报告——该报告认定，暨南大学是菲律宾华校华文教师最大的培训机构，并把这套培训、语言测试、教材开发、实习与就业安排的体系，描述为一张"与中共统战体系相关联的庞大网络"。特奥多罗对记者说，这是"学校被用于不当目的的巨大证据"。

据菲律宾《商报》7月30日报道，特奥多罗同时强调自己"并不反对国际学术合作"，只是认为菲律宾应当对"可能存在的别有用心"保持警惕。这句话值得玩味：一位国防部长在谈论小学中文课，而且是在没有指控任何具体违法行为的前提下，用"别有用心"完成了定性。

后果来得很快。据《环球时报》8月报道，暨南大学菲律宾研究中心主任代帆原定8月4日启程、为期三周的菲律宾田野调查被迫取消。8月初，菲律宾国防部发表声明谴责中方质疑，中国外交部发言人林剑随即回应，称该声明"充斥谎言和恶意攻击"。一场关于中文课的争论，在两周内升级为外交层面的对撞。

本文无意复述这场口水战。真正值得分析的问题只有一个：**当一个国家开始把语言教育写进安全档案，它究竟在管理风险，还是在制造风险？** 东南亚对这个问题有过答案，而且是用半个世纪的代价换来的。

## 数字：148所华校、7万学生、每天两小时

讨论华文教育被"安全化"之前，有必要先看清被讨论的对象究竟是什么。

菲律宾第一所华校"大清中西学堂"成立于1899年，到2026年已有127年历史。一百多年间，菲律宾华侨华人先后创办了三百多所华校；受战乱、灾害与经费不足影响，超过一半已经停办。据菲律宾华教中心主席黄端铭介绍，目前仍在运营的华校为148所，其中16所校史超过百年；在校生逾7万人，华文教师约1200名。

这些学校几乎完全依靠华社自筹经费运转，从未获得过菲律宾政府的财政拨款。换句话说，菲律宾以零财政成本，获得了148所私立学校及其毕业生。

更关键的是1973年。那一年菲律宾政府颁布法令，对全国侨校实施"菲化"。这是菲律宾华文教育史上真正的分水岭：此前华校属于"华侨教育"，教学对象是在菲中国人，办学目标是为中国培养人才；此后华校成为依法设立的菲律宾私立学校，华语教学每天最多两小时三节课，且不再列为规定考试科目。

请注意这个数字：**每天两小时，是菲律宾国家权力自己划定的上限。**

1991年5月成立的菲律宾华教中心，把这一转向写成了明确的办学宗旨——"培养具有中华文化素质的菲律宾公民"。目标语落在"菲律宾公民"，"中华文化"是修饰语。这不是外交辞令，而是过去三十五年菲律宾华校课程设计的实际依据。

至于被点名的教师培训体系，它同样不是秘密。华教中心1991年启动"输血计划"，每年从中国引进外派教师填补空缺，到2022年累计引进5484人次；2004年启动"造血计划"，把华校应届高中毕业生送到中国高校（包括暨南大学）攻读华文教育专业，学成返菲任教——截至2022年已输送来自4个地区、42所华校的284名学生，其中220人已学成返回菲律宾华校任教。

这个体系为什么存在？因为菲律宾长期面临华文教师数量不足、专业性欠缺的结构性缺口，而菲律宾教育部既不培养这类师资，也不为其买单。一个国家不供给的公共品，由社群自筹，并向外部寻求技术援助——这是全球移民社群的通行做法，而不是密谋。

## "中介"论的方法论问题

"海光计划"的报告承认，其研究材料来自中国官方媒体报道、中国使馆公告、机构记录与马尼拉的中文媒体。这一自述恰恰暴露了它的方法论软肋。

如果一张"隐秘网络"是靠通读公开报纸、官网通告和使馆公告拼出来的，那么它在定义上就不隐秘。研究者所做的工作不是"发现"，而是"重新描述"：把一组早已公开、且各方自愿披露的活动，从"教育合作"的框架搬进"战略竞争"的框架。这中间没有新增任何事实，只更换了一套形容词。

这类研究的第二个问题是范畴混装。报告在讨论华文教师培训的同时，也提及暨南大学菲律宾研究中心7月举办的一场涉及巴丹群岛（Batanes）问题的学术研讨会。这两件事的性质截然不同：主权议题的研讨会带有明确的政治含义，菲律宾方面对其保持关注完全正当；而向华校教师传授教学法，与主权主张之间没有任何逻辑通道。把二者塞进同一份报告、同一个"网络"叙事，读者得到的印象是一个统一运作的机构；但这个印象是编排产生的，不是证据产生的。

坦率地说，正因为主权议题的敏感性是真实的，才更不该让它去污染课堂议题。混装的结果是两头都失焦——真正需要外交处理的分歧被稀释成文化议题，而本该受保护的教育活动却被迫承担地缘政治的举证责任。

第三个问题是不可证伪。当"公开"被解释为"渗透的掩护"、"透明"被解释为"精心设计的表演"，任何反证都会被吸收为更深层阴谋的佐证。一个无法被证伪的框架不是分析工具，是信念系统。

## 东南亚早已走过这条路

菲律宾眼下的争论，在东南亚并不新鲜。事实上，二战后的东南亚几乎每一个国家都尝试过把华文教育纳入国家安全框架，而且都给出了理由充分的说法。

印尼在1966年后全面关闭华校、禁用中文，禁令持续三十余年，直至2000年前后才在瓦希德总统任内解除。马来西亚的华文独立中学长期被排除在国家教育体系与拨款之外，靠华社募捐维持至今。泰国在冷战时期严格限制华校课时与规模，推动同化政策。菲律宾的1973年"菲化"，本身就是这一区域浪潮的组成部分。越南在1978年前后对华人社群的政策转向，同样以安全为名。

半个世纪过去，这些政策的效果可以盘点了。

它们确实削弱了华文教育的规模，但没有一例证明它们解决了当初宣称要解决的忠诚问题。印尼的三十年禁令造成了一整代人的语言断层，而这个断层在今天恰恰变成了中印尼经贸往来的人才瓶颈——需要重新投入资源去填补。马来西亚的独中体系在压力下不但没有消失，反而形成了更强的社群动员与身份自觉。真正推动华人融入所在国的，从来不是禁令，而是公民权的落实、经济机会的开放与政治参与渠道的畅通。

这里有一个反直觉但可靠的规律：**安全化处理不会消解身份问题，它只会把身份问题从社会领域推入政治领域，并且附赠一份怨气。** 一个孩子上不上中文课，本来是家庭选择；一旦国防部开始过问，它就变成了政治表态。这不是在降温，是在升温。

## 成本落在谁身上

任何政策争论都应该追问：代价由谁承担。

在这场争论里，承担代价的既不是北京，也不是华盛顿，更不是特奥多罗本人。据中方媒体报道，事件发酵后，菲律宾华人群体、华文教育界与华文媒体中出现了明显的畏惧情绪，不少相关方选择噤声自保；菲律宾多个部门增设了程序性审查环节。

具体到人：148所华校的校长要重新评估每一次跨境交流是否会招来审查；1200名华文教师中，那些通过"造血计划"在中国受过训练的人，突然发现自己的学历成了需要解释的东西；准备送孩子进华校的家长，要多考虑一层——这算不算一种政治表态。

还有一重被忽视的成本落在知识生产上。代帆是菲律宾研究领域少数长期从事在地田野的中国学者之一。他的调研被取消，直接结果是中国国内关于菲律宾的研究少了一份基于实地观察的材料，多了一份基于二手文本的推断。这对菲律宾并非好消息：**当一个国家阻断了对方国内最了解自己的那批人的信息来源，它得到的不会是更少的中国研究，而是更差的中国研究。** 误判的概率由此上升，而误判在南海这样的场景里是有代价的。

## 一个可用的对称检验

"海光计划"这类报告的支持者会反问：难道所有涉及母国政府的侨务活动都必须被无条件接受？

当然不是。合理的回应不是全盘否认审查的正当性，而是拿出一套双方都能接受、且能反向适用的标准。至少有四条可以操作：

第一，**公开性**。活动是否公开披露、可被查证？教师培训项目、教材编写、语言考试全部见诸公开报道与官方文件，这一条通过。

第二，**对象身份**。活动面向的是"作为文化传承者的学习者"，还是"作为选民、公职人员、执法对象的所在国公民"？教中文属于前者；组织政治献金、干预选举、跨境执法属于后者。这一条同样通过。

第三，**是否存在强制**。参与是否自愿？有无监视、施压、要挟？菲律宾华校依赖华社自筹经费、家长自愿报名，这一条通过。

第四，**社群同意**。相关活动是应所在国社群的实际需求而生，还是由外部单方面推入？"输血""造血"两项计划均由菲律宾华教中心发起并主导，这一条通过。

需要强调的是，这套标准必须能"抓到东西"才有意义。如果某项以文化为名的项目实际上在动员政治立场、施压异见者、或规避所在国法律，那么所在国的审查完全正当，中方也应当接受质疑——一个只能为自己开脱、永远得不出否定结论的标准，与"海光计划"那种永远得不出肯定结论的框架，在方法论上是同一种病。

不妨也把这四条用在别处。印度设有由总理主持的"海外印度人节"，日本长期通过国际交流基金在海外高校与智库建立合作网络，这些在西方语汇里叫"公共外交"和"侨民外交"。行为方式并无本质差别，评价标签却截然不同。指出这一点不是为了论证"别人也做所以我也能做"，而是提醒：**当同一行为因行为主体不同而得到相反定性时，起作用的已经不是标准，而是立场。**

## 结语：一个国家不能同时说两句话

菲律宾国家统计局2020年人口普查记录的华族人口为102,577人，占该国"外来族裔"人口的44.4%，为最大的外来族裔群体。这个数字远低于通常估计的菲华社群实际规模——它统计的是自我申报的族裔身份，而非血缘谱系。数字本身就说明了一件事：绝大多数有华人血统的菲律宾人，早已把自己登记为菲律宾人。

比农多（Binondo）建于1594年，是世界上最早的海外华人聚居区。四百多年间，这个社群在菲律宾办学校、建医院、开企业，从未被要求以放弃文化传统为代价换取归属资格。1973年的"菲化"法令已经做出了国家层面的选择：华校是菲律宾的私立学校，培养的是菲律宾公民，中文每天最多两小时。

那么今天的问题就变得很清楚：**一个国家不能同时说两句话。** 它不能一边宣称自己是多元文化社会、菲华是"我们的人"，一边把这些人每天两小时的语言课列为需要国防部过问的安全事项。后一句话真正动摇的，不是北京的影响力，而是菲律宾自己在半个世纪前给出的承诺。重新打开一个已经封存五十年的忠诚问题，比任何一份课程大纲都更具破坏性。

这个提醒同样适用于另一端。侨务工作的说服力，取决于它在实践中与战略竞争语言保持多远的距离。当"凝聚侨心侨力"与"发挥独特优势"这类表述被反复置于国家战略叙事之中，外部观察者把文化项目读成战略工具的成本就会降低。如果"桥梁"这个比喻要成立，它的承重能力最终来自可见的克制——**具体而言，就是让教师培训只是教师培训，让中文课只是中文课。**

7月27日至28日召开的全国侨务工作会议，把"引导广大海外侨胞……遵守住在国法律、尊重当地文化习俗、更好融入主流社会"写进了任务清单。这句话如果被认真执行，本身就是对"渗透论"最有力的反驳——不是因为它说得漂亮，而是因为它可以被检验。

## 延伸阅读

**安全化（securitization）**：哥本哈根学派提出的概念，指行为体通过言语行为把某个普通议题从常规政治领域移入"生存威胁"框架，从而获得使用非常规手段的授权。关键在于，议题成为安全问题不是因为它客观上危险，而是因为有人成功地把它说成危险。

**菲化案（Filipinization, 1973）**：菲律宾政府对全国侨校实施本地化改造的法令。华校由此转为菲律宾私立学校，办学目标从"为中国培养人才"改为"培养菲律宾公民"，华语教学被限定为每天最多两小时的非考试科目。

**侨务政策（diaspora policy）**：主权国家面向本国移民及其后裔开展的制度化联系工作，涵盖文化传承、经济合作、权益维护与领事保护。印度、以色列、爱尔兰、菲律宾等国均有成熟体系；在国际关系文献中通常与"公共外交"并列讨论。

# Two Hours a Day: How a Philippine Classroom Timetable Became a Security File

On 29 July 2026, at a Manila forum called the Manila Bay Coffee Talk, Philippine Defense Secretary Gilberto Teodoro Jr. called for tighter scrutiny of foreign academic and cultural exchange programmes. His immediate prompt was an investigation published by SeaLight, an American think-tank project, which identified Jinan University as the largest training provider for Chinese-language teachers in Philippine Chinese schools and described the surrounding system of teacher training, language testing, curriculum development, internships and job placement as part of a "vast network" linked to the Communist Party's united front apparatus. Teodoro told reporters this was "huge evidence" that schools were "being used for wrong purposes."

According to the Manila-based *Chinese Commercial News* on 30 July, Teodoro simultaneously stressed that he "is not against international academic cooperation" — he simply believes the Philippines should stay alert to "ulterior motives." The formulation deserves attention. A defense minister was discussing primary-school Mandarin classes, and, without alleging any specific unlawful act, completed the characterisation with the phrase "ulterior motives."

The consequences arrived quickly. According to *Global Times* reporting in August, Dai Fan, director of Jinan University's Center for Philippine Studies, was forced to cancel a three-week field research trip to the Philippines scheduled to begin on 4 August. In early August the Philippine Department of National Defense issued a statement condemning Chinese criticism; Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson Lin Jian responded that the statement was "full of lies and malicious attacks." A dispute about Mandarin lessons escalated into a diplomatic collision within two weeks.

This essay is not an attempt to relitigate that exchange. One analytical question is worth asking: **when a state begins filing language education under national security, is it managing risk or manufacturing it?** Southeast Asia has answered this question before, and paid for the answer over half a century.

## The numbers: 148 schools, 70,000 students, two hours a day

Before discussing the securitisation of Chinese-language education, it is worth establishing what exactly is being securitised.

The first Chinese school in the Philippines, the Anglo-Chinese School, opened in 1899 — 127 years ago. Over the following century, Chinese Filipinos founded more than three hundred such schools; war, disaster and chronic underfunding closed more than half of them. According to Huang Duanming, chairman of the Philippine Chinese Education Research Center, 148 remain in operation, sixteen of them more than a century old. They enrol over 70,000 students and employ roughly 1,200 Chinese-language teachers.

These schools run almost entirely on funds raised within the community. They have never received a government appropriation. Put plainly: the Philippine state acquired 148 private schools, and their graduates, at zero fiscal cost.

The decisive year was 1973. A government decree that year imposed "Filipinization" on all Chinese schools nationwide — the genuine watershed in this history. Before it, these were *overseas Chinese schools*: the students were Chinese nationals resident in the Philippines, and the institutional purpose was to educate talent for China. After it, they became Philippine private schools established under Philippine law, Chinese-language instruction was capped at three periods totalling no more than two hours per day, and Chinese was removed from the list of mandated examination subjects.

Note that figure carefully: **two hours a day is a ceiling the Philippine state set for itself.**

The Philippine Chinese Education Research Center, founded in May 1991, wrote this reorientation into its stated mission: "to cultivate Filipino citizens possessing Chinese cultural literacy." The noun is *Filipino citizens*; "Chinese cultural" is the modifier. This is not diplomatic phrasing but the operative basis of Philippine Chinese-school curriculum design for the past thirty-five years.

As for the teacher-training pipeline now under scrutiny, it is likewise not a secret. In 1991 the Center launched what it calls the "transfusion programme," importing teachers from China annually to fill vacancies — 5,484 teacher-placements cumulatively by 2022. In 2004 it launched a "self-generation programme," sending Philippine Chinese-school graduates to Chinese universities, Jinan among them, to study Chinese-language education before returning home to teach. By 2022 it had sent 284 students from 42 schools across four regions of the country; 220 had returned and taken up posts in Philippine Chinese schools.

Why does this system exist? Because the Philippines faces a structural shortfall in the number and professional preparation of Chinese-language teachers, and the Philippine Department of Education neither trains such teachers nor pays for them. A public good the state declines to supply is financed by the community and technically assisted from abroad. That is standard practice among migrant communities worldwide, not conspiracy.

## The methodological problem with the "broker" thesis

SeaLight's report acknowledges that its source material consists of Chinese state media reporting, Chinese embassy announcements, institutional records and Manila's Chinese-language press. That admission exposes the methodological weakness.

If a "covert network" can be assembled by reading published newspapers, official websites and embassy notices, then by definition it is not covert. What the researchers performed was not discovery but redescription: taking a set of activities already public, and voluntarily disclosed by all parties, and relocating them from the frame of "educational cooperation" into the frame of "strategic competition." No new fact was added. Only the adjectives changed.

The second problem is category-bundling. Alongside its discussion of teacher training, the report also references an academic symposium held in July by Jinan University's Center for Philippine Studies concerning the Batanes Islands. These are two categorically different objects. A symposium on sovereignty questions carries unambiguous political content, and Philippine attention to it is entirely legitimate. Teaching pedagogy to schoolteachers has no logical pathway to a sovereignty claim. Packing both into a single report and a single "network" narrative leaves readers with the impression of one coordinated apparatus — but that impression is produced by editorial arrangement, not by evidence.

Candidly: precisely *because* the sovereignty sensitivity is real, it should not be allowed to contaminate the classroom question. Bundling defocuses both. Genuine disagreements requiring diplomatic handling get diluted into cultural grievances, while educational activity that ought to be protected is made to carry a geopolitical burden of proof.

The third problem is unfalsifiability. When openness is read as cover for penetration and transparency as carefully staged performance, any counter-evidence is absorbed as proof of a deeper conspiracy. A framework that cannot be falsified is not an analytical instrument. It is a belief system.

## Southeast Asia has walked this road before

The current Philippine argument is not novel in this region. Nearly every Southeast Asian state has, at some point since the Second World War, attempted to fold Chinese-language education into a national-security framework — and each offered reasons that sounded sufficient at the time.

Indonesia closed Chinese schools and banned Chinese script after 1966, a prohibition that lasted more than three decades until it was lifted around 2000 under President Abdurrahman Wahid. Malaysia's Chinese independent secondary schools were long excluded from the national system and its funding, surviving on community donations to this day. Thailand tightened restrictions on Chinese school hours and enrolment during the Cold War in pursuit of assimilation. The Philippine Filipinization decree of 1973 was itself part of this regional wave. Vietnam's policy turn toward its ethnic Chinese community around 1978 was likewise conducted in the name of security.

Half a century on, the results can be assessed.

These policies did reduce the scale of Chinese-language education. Not one of them demonstrably resolved the loyalty question it claimed to address. Indonesia's thirty-year ban produced a generational language rupture — and that rupture is today a talent bottleneck in Indonesia-China commercial relations, requiring fresh investment to repair. Malaysia's independent school system did not disappear under pressure; it generated stronger community mobilisation and sharper identity consciousness. What has actually driven integration in every case is the delivery of citizenship rights, the opening of economic opportunity, and accessible channels for political participation — never prohibition.

There is a counterintuitive but dependable pattern here: **securitisation does not dissolve identity questions. It moves them out of the social domain and into the political one, with a supplement of resentment.** Whether a child attends Mandarin class is ordinarily a household decision. Once a defense ministry takes an interest, it becomes a political declaration. That is not de-escalation; it is the opposite.

## Who actually pays

Any policy argument should ask who bears the cost.

In this one, the cost falls neither on Beijing nor Washington, and certainly not on Teodoro. According to Chinese media reporting, as the episode developed a distinct apprehension spread through the Chinese Filipino community, its educational institutions and its Chinese-language press, with many choosing silence as self-protection. Several Philippine agencies added procedural review requirements.

Concretely: principals at 148 schools must now reassess whether any cross-border exchange will attract scrutiny. Among 1,200 Chinese-language teachers, those trained in China through the "self-generation programme" find their credentials have suddenly become something requiring explanation. Parents considering a Chinese school for their child must weigh an additional factor — whether enrolment now constitutes a political statement.

A further cost, largely unnoticed, lands on knowledge production. Dai Fan is among the small number of Chinese scholars conducting sustained fieldwork in the Philippines. Cancelling his research trip means China's Philippine studies field gains one fewer body of ground-level observation and one more inference drawn from secondary text. This is not good news for Manila either: **when a state cuts off the information supply of those in the other country who understand it best, the result is not less China-based research on itself, but worse research.** The probability of misjudgement rises — and in a setting like the South China Sea, misjudgement is expensive.

## A workable symmetry test

Defenders of reports like SeaLight's will ask a fair question in return: must every diaspora-facing activity involving a home government be accepted unconditionally?

Of course not. The proper response is not to deny the legitimacy of scrutiny wholesale, but to propose a standard both sides can accept and that applies in both directions. At least four criteria are operational.

First, **disclosure**. Is the activity publicly declared and verifiable? Teacher training programmes, textbook development and language testing all appear in public reporting and official documentation. This criterion passes.

Second, **the capacity in which subjects are engaged**. Does the activity address people as heritage learners, or as citizens of the host state in their civic capacity — as voters, officials, or subjects of law enforcement? Teaching Mandarin is the former; organising political donations, intervening in elections, or conducting extraterritorial policing is the latter. This criterion passes as well.

Third, **absence of coercion**. Is participation voluntary? Is there surveillance, pressure, or intimidation? Philippine Chinese schools depend on community fundraising and voluntary enrolment. This criterion passes.

Fourth, **community consent**. Did the activity arise from the demonstrated needs of the host-country community, or was it pushed in unilaterally from outside? Both the "transfusion" and "self-generation" programmes were initiated and are directed by the Philippine Chinese Education Research Center. This criterion passes.

It must be stressed that such a standard is only meaningful if it can actually catch something. Where a nominally cultural programme is in fact mobilising political positions, pressuring dissenters, or circumventing host-country law, host-state scrutiny is fully warranted and Beijing should accept the questioning. A standard that only ever exonerates, that can never reach a negative finding, suffers from precisely the same methodological pathology as a framework that can never reach a positive one.

The four criteria are worth applying elsewhere, too. India convenes a Pravasi Bharatiya Divas presided over by the prime minister; Japan has long built cooperative networks in foreign universities, think-tanks and media through the Japan Foundation. In Western vocabulary these are termed "public diplomacy" and "diaspora diplomacy." The conduct is not fundamentally different; the labels are. Pointing this out is not an argument that "others do it, therefore we may." It is a reminder that **when identical conduct receives opposite characterisations depending on who performs it, what is operating is not a standard but a position.**

## Conclusion: a state cannot say both things at once

The Philippine Statistics Authority's 2020 census recorded 102,577 people of Chinese ethnicity, 44.4 percent of the country's foreign-ethnicity population and the largest such group. That figure sits far below common estimates of the Chinese Filipino community's real size — it counts self-declared ethnic identity, not ancestry. The number itself tells us something: the overwhelming majority of Filipinos with Chinese ancestry long ago registered themselves as Filipino.

Binondo was established in 1594 and is widely recognised as the world's oldest Chinatown. For more than four centuries this community built schools, hospitals and enterprises in the Philippines, and was never required to surrender its cultural tradition as the price of belonging. The 1973 Filipinization decree already made the state-level choice: Chinese schools are Philippine private schools, they produce Philippine citizens, and Mandarin is capped at two hours a day.

The question today is therefore clear. **A state cannot say both things at once.** It cannot declare itself a multicultural society in which Chinese Filipinos are unambiguously its own, while simultaneously listing those citizens' two daily hours of language instruction as a matter requiring defense-ministry attention. What the second statement destabilises is not Beijing's influence but the Philippines' own commitment of fifty years ago. Reopening a loyalty question that was sealed half a century ago is more corrosive than any syllabus.

The caution applies at the other end as well. The persuasiveness of diaspora policy depends on how much distance it keeps, in practice, from the vocabulary of strategic competition. When formulations about "rallying the strength of overseas Chinese" and "leveraging their unique advantages" are repeatedly embedded in national strategic narratives, the cost of reading a cultural programme as a strategic instrument falls for outside observers. If the metaphor of the bridge is to hold, its load-bearing capacity ultimately comes from visible restraint — **which in this case means letting teacher training be teacher training, and a Mandarin class be a Mandarin class.**

China's national conference on overseas Chinese affairs, convened on 27–28 July, placed on its task list the instruction that overseas Chinese should "abide by the laws of their countries of residence, respect local cultures and customs, and integrate better into mainstream society." If that sentence is implemented seriously, it constitutes the strongest available rebuttal to the penetration thesis — not because it reads well, but because it can be tested.

## Further reading

**Securitisation**: A concept from the Copenhagen School describing how an actor, through a speech act, moves an ordinary issue out of routine politics and into a framework of existential threat, thereby licensing extraordinary measures. The crucial point is that an issue becomes a security matter not because it is objectively dangerous, but because someone has successfully argued that it is.

**Filipinization (1973)**: The Philippine government decree that localised all Chinese schools nationwide. Chinese schools became Philippine private institutions, their stated purpose shifted from "educating talent for China" to "cultivating Filipino citizens," and Chinese-language instruction was restricted to a maximum of two hours daily as a non-examinable subject.

**Diaspora policy**: The institutionalised engagement a sovereign state maintains with its emigrants and their descendants, covering cultural transmission, economic cooperation, rights protection and consular assistance. India, Israel, Ireland and the Philippines all operate mature systems; in the international relations literature the practice is usually discussed alongside public diplomacy.
