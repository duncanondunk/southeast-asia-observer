# 海面下的三千亿美元：泰柬如何把海上争端从"对峙"拉回"谈判"

据《南华早报（South China Morning Post）》报道，泰国（Thailand）与柬埔寨近日在荷兰海牙和平宫结束第二轮联合国海洋法公约（公约）框架下的海洋划界调解会谈，双方同意设立海军指挥官热线、克制公开表态，为这片能源富集、估值约3000亿美元（约2.16万亿元人民币）的争议海床降温。表面看，这只是一次外交程序性进展；但把它放进泰柬关系的脉络里，却是一场被复制、且可能被放大的对抗的"急刹车"。

## 海面下埋着三千亿美元

真正让这片海"值钱"的，是海底。泰国湾那块面积约2.7万平方公里的楔形争议海域，蕴藏的石油与天然气估值高达约3000亿美元（约2.16万亿元人民币）。对一个刚经历陆地边境流血、跨境贸易一度冻结数十亿美元（约数百亿元人民币）的国家组合而言，这笔"海上浮财"既是诱惑，也是隐患。

有意思的是，陆地边境的硝烟尚未散尽，海上的算计已经开场。据《南华早报》梳理，两国陆地边界长达约800公里，去年爆发的冲突造成数十人丧生，价值数十亿美元（约数百亿元人民币）的跨境贸易一度停摆；而十二月达成的停火虽勉强维持，双方仍互相指责对方在冲突中袭击平民、违反停火。

## 从陆地到海面：被复制的对抗

陆地上的对峙逻辑，正在海面上被"复制"。泰国今年五月单方面退出二〇〇一年与柬埔寨签署的联合开发海上油气资源的协议，触发柬方转而请求公约下设的调解委员会介入。据《南华早报》报道，首轮调解去年九月在新加坡举行，次轮本月十四日至十八日在海牙和平宫闭门进行。

更棘手的是，物理冲突降温了，舆论战却升温。两国网民在社交媒体上围绕"谁侵占了谁的土地""谁抄袭了谁的传统服饰"吵成一团；调解委员会因此特意把"防范虚假信息与误导宣传"写进建立信任措施。换句话说，信息战已经和领土争端绑在了一起——这正是一九九〇年代以来多场周边冲突的熟悉剧本。

## 海牙第二轮：不是裁决，是"劝和"

第二轮会谈最值得玩味的，是它"非裁决"的性质。据《南华早报》引述常设仲裁法院（仲裁法院）声明，双方展现"以和平方式解决争端的诚意"，并就建立信任措施达成一致；这些措施包括承诺在谈及海洋边界与双边关系时"为建设性接触留出空间，而非向对方施压或堵死选项"。

这不是有约束力的判决。该调解程序史上仅第二次启用——上一次是二〇一六年东帝汶诉澳大利亚，耗时近两年才了结。五人调解委员会由澳大利亚律师兼外交官卡特里娜·库珀主持，结论同样不具法律约束力。说白了，这是一场"劝和"，不是"判案"。

## 一条热线与一顿"舆论停火"

本轮最受瞩目的具体成果，是一条海军指挥官热线。据《南华早报》报道，仲裁法院称双方海军指挥官将使用既有热线，"及时通报并处置海上突发事件，避免误解与紧张升级"。

这条热线看似微小，意义却不小：它把"擦枪走火"的紧急制动从纸面拉进实操。相比之下，南海（South China Sea）各方虽有类似热线，却在压力下屡屡失灵。与此同时，双方还约定克制可能煽动公众情绪的公开表态、共同防范虚假信息——相当于一顿"舆论停火"。泰国与柬埔寨外交部均表态支持，柬方更重申"国际法是国家间和平解决争端的路径"。

## 为何选"调解"而非"仲裁"

一个关键问题：为什么是"调解"，而不是更"硬"的仲裁？答案藏在两国的地缘站位里。泰国与柬埔寨都与中国关系友好，也都无意让一个第三方做出有约束力的裁决、从而立下可能反噬自身的先例。二〇一六年菲律宾（Philippines）诉中国南海仲裁案就是前车之鉴——那是一份有约束力、却被北京（Beijing）拒绝承认的裁决。

对曼谷和金边而言，调解的妙处在于"软"：过程不具约束力，结论靠双方自愿落地，既保全颜面，又把争端的所有权留在自己手里。与其让海牙的仲裁庭"判"，不如让海牙的调解委员会"劝"。这是小国在大国阴影下自保的一种精明。

## 资源民族主义的暗流

把镜头拉远，这场争端处在东南亚（Southeast Asia）"资源民族主义"回潮的暗流中。从印度尼西亚（Indonesia）收紧镍矿、到各国争相锁定关键矿产，能源与矿藏正被重新政治化。泰国湾那三千亿美元（约2.16万亿元人民币）的海底资产，只会让双方更难轻易让步。

但历史也提供了出口。泰国与马来西亚（Malaysia）在泰国湾的联合开发机构，正是"共同开发、收益共享"的成熟范本；柬泰若想绕开划界僵局，最现实的出路同样是先把资源"搁置争议、共同开发"，再把政治信任慢慢补回来。问题是：当民族主义情绪已被点燃，政客还敢不敢先伸出那只手？

## 东盟（ASEAN）方式的又一次压力测试

这场争端也是对"东盟方式"的一次压力测试。东盟惯以共识与双边主义化解矛盾，忌讳外部强制仲裁。泰柬把海上争端主动拉上规则轨道、用调解替代对抗，恰恰是对"东盟方式"的某种回归与修补。

但也要看到，东盟方式的长处是"不激化"，短处是"不解决"。建立信任措施能防止最坏情况，却未必能带来划界终点。第三轮会谈已定于十一月二十四日至二十七日在马来西亚吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）举行——真正的考验，是双方能否在"劝和"之外，拿出可执行的共同开发方案。

## 先把火药味从海面抽走

说到底，泰柬海上争端最吊诡之处在于：陆地上的火药味最难散，海面上的算计却有更大的"共同开发"空间。把这场争端从"对峙"拉回"谈判"，海牙第二轮迈出的虽是小小一步，却可能是关键的一步。

对东南亚（Southeast Asia）而言，这提供了一个不同于南海的模板：当大国不愿、或小国不敢诉诸强制仲裁时，"软性调解+热线+共同开发"未必不是一条更稳的出路。先把火药味从海面抽走，剩下的，留给时间与耐心。

## 延伸阅读

**联合国海洋法公约（UNCLOS）**：一九八二年签署、一九九四年生效的海洋法框架，规定了领海、专属经济区与大陆架的划界原则；其下设的"调解委员会"是争端解决机制中最软的一档。

**共同开发（Joint Development）**：相邻国家在划界未决前，就争议海域资源达成"搁置争议、共同开发、收益共享"的安排；泰国—马来西亚联合开发机构是泰国湾内的成熟范例。

**常设仲裁法院（PCA）**：位于荷兰海牙和平宫的政府间仲裁机构，曾受理二〇一六年菲律宾诉中国南海仲裁案；本次泰柬调解的秘书处即设于此。

# The US$300 Billion Beneath the Waves: How Thailand and Cambodia Pulled Their Seabed Dispute Back From Confrontation

According to the South China Morning Post, Thailand and Cambodia have closed a second round of conciliation talks under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) at the Peace Palace in The Hague, agreeing to a naval commanders' hotline and restraint in public statements to cool a dispute over an energy-rich seabed estimated at US$300 billion. On the surface this is a procedural diplomatic step; placed in the context of Thailand–Cambodia relations, it is an emergency brake on a standoff that was being replicated — and risked being magnified — at sea.

## The US$300 billion beneath the waves

What makes this stretch of water "valuable" is what lies beneath. The roughly 27,000-square-kilometre wedge of contested Gulf of Thailand holds oil and gas reserves valued at an estimated US$300 billion. For a pair of countries that just endured deadly land-border fighting and a freeze on cross-border trade worth billions of dollars, this "offshore windfall" is both a temptation and a hazard.

Tellingly, before the smoke on land had cleared, the calculus at sea had already begun. As the SCMP recounts, the two states share an 800-kilometre land border; last year's clashes killed scores and froze billions of dollars in cross-border commerce, and although a December ceasefire roughly holds, each side still accuses the other of targeting civilians and breaching the truce.

## From land to sea: a standoff replicated

The logic of confrontation on land is being copied at sea. In May, Thailand unilaterally withdrew from a 2001 agreement with Cambodia to jointly exploit offshore oil and gas, triggering Phnom Penh's request for the UNCLOS conciliation commission to step in. The first round was held in Singapore last September, the second behind closed doors at the Peace Palace from 14 to 18 October, the SCMP reports.

The harder problem is that, as the physical conflict cooled, the information war heated up. Netizens from both countries bicker online over who encroached on whose land and who appropriated whose traditional dress; the commission therefore explicitly wrote "guard against misinformation and disinformation" into its confidence-building measures. In other words, the info-war is now fused with the territorial dispute — a familiar script from many peripheral conflicts since the 1990s.

## The Hague round two: conciliation, not adjudication

The most instructive feature of the second round is its non-adjudicative character. Citing the Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA), the SCMP reports both sides showed "good faith towards the peaceful resolution of the dispute" and agreed on confidence-building measures, including a commitment that public statements on the maritime boundary and bilateral relations should "create space for constructive engagement, rather than generate pressure on the other party or foreclose options."

This is not a binding verdict. The conciliation process has been used only twice in UNCLOS history — the first being East Timor v Australia in 2016, which took nearly two years to conclude. The five-member commission is chaired by Australian lawyer-diplomat Katrina Cooper, and its conclusions are likewise not legally binding. Put plainly: this is a mediation, not a ruling.

## A hotline and a 'communications ceasefire'

The most concrete deliverable is a naval commanders' hotline. Per the SCMP, the PCA said the two navies' commanders will use an existing hotline "for the prompt notification and management of maritime incidents, so as to avoid misunderstandings and tensions."

Small as it looks, the hotline matters: it moves the emergency brake against accidental escalation from paper into practice. Compare this with the South China Sea, where analogous hotlines have repeatedly failed under pressure. At the same time, the two sides pledged restraint in public statements that could inflame sentiment and joint vigilance against disinformation — a de facto "communications ceasefire." Both foreign ministries voiced support; Phnom Penh reiterated that "international law provides the pathway for peaceful resolution of disputes between States."

## Why conciliation, not arbitration

A key question: why conciliation rather than the "harder" arbitration? The answer lies in the two states' geopolitical positioning. Both Thailand and Cambodia enjoy friendly ties with China, and neither wants a third party to issue a binding ruling that could set a precedent rebounding on itself. The 2016 Philippines v China South China Sea arbitration is the cautionary tale — a binding award that Beijing simply refused to recognise.

For Bangkok and Phnom Penh, the virtue of conciliation is its softness: the process is non-binding, the outcome lands only by mutual consent, and ownership of the dispute stays in their own hands. Rather than have a Hague tribunal "rule," let a Hague commission "persuade." It is small-state savvy for self-preservation in the shadow of great powers.

## The undercurrent of resource nationalism

Zooming out, the dispute sits in the undercurrent of resurgent resource nationalism across Southeast Asia. From Indonesia tightening its nickel grip to states racing to lock in critical minerals, energy and ores are being re-politicised. The US$300 billion on the Gulf of Thailand seabed only makes compromise harder for either side.

History, however, also offers an off-ramp. The Thailand–Malaysia Joint Development Area in the Gulf of Thailand is a mature template for "joint development, shared benefit"; if Phnom Penh and Bangkok wish to bypass the delimitation deadlock, the most realistic path is likewise to set the resources aside for co-development first and rebuild political trust slowly. The question is whether, once nationalist sentiment is lit, politicians still dare to extend that hand.

## Another stress test for the 'ASEAN Way'

The dispute is also a stress test for the "ASEAN Way." ASEAN habitually manages friction through consensus and bilateralism, wary of externally imposed arbitration. By pulling the maritime dispute onto a rules-based track and substituting conciliation for confrontation, Thailand and Cambodia are in a sense returning to — and repairing — that ASEAN Way.

Yet one must note the Way's strength is "not escalating" and its weakness is "not resolving." Confidence-building measures prevent the worst, but need not deliver a delimitation endpoint. A third round is scheduled for Kuala Lumpur on 24–27 November — the real test is whether, beyond "persuading," the two sides can produce an executable joint-development plan.

## First, drain the gunpowder from the sea

Ultimately the strangest thing about the Thailand–Cambodia maritime dispute is this: the gunpowder on land is hardest to clear, yet the calculus at sea has far more room for "joint development." Pulling the contest back from confrontation to negotiation, the Hague's second round is a small step that may prove decisive.

For Southeast Asia, it offers a template distinct from the South China Sea: when great powers are unwilling, or small states dare not, resort to binding arbitration, "soft conciliation plus hotline plus joint development" may well be the steadier exit. First, drain the gunpowder from the sea; leave the rest to time and patience.

## Further reading

**UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS):** The 1982 framework, in force since 1994, that sets principles for delimiting territorial seas, exclusive economic zones and continental shelves; its Conciliation Commission is the softest tier of the dispute-settlement mechanism.

**Joint Development:** An arrangement whereby neighbouring states, before delimitation is settled, govern disputed-area resources through "setting aside the dispute, joint development, shared benefits"; the Thailand–Malaysia Joint Development Area is a mature Gulf of Thailand example.

**Permanent Court of Arbitration (PCA):** The intergovernmental arbitration body at the Peace Palace in The Hague that handled the 2016 Philippines v China South China Sea case; the secretariat for the current Thailand–Cambodia conciliation sits here.
