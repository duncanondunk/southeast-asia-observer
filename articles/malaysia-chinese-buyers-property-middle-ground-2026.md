# 马来西亚的“中间牌”：用泰国的价格，买新加坡的安稳

多年来，中国资金海外置业的故事只有两种色调：新加坡的安全与昂贵，泰国的便宜与松弛。如今一组数据显示，第三种色调正在补进来。据覆盖111个国家、约700万套房源的房产平台居外（Juwai IQI）统计，2026年上半年，马来西亚在中国买家咨询目的地中位列第四，高于2024年的第七名和2025年的第六名。名次只前移几位，方向却很说明问题：这个长期被当配角的国家，正悄悄成为香港与内地买家“没想到自己会找”的中间选项。

《南华早报》（South China Morning Post）10月4日报道了这组数据。其意义不在排名本身，而在中国出海置业需求正重新排队：从区域市场的两极，流向那个“两头都占一点”的国家。咨询量榜单并非完美标尺——咨询不等于成交——但它先行于资金，且今年的指向与各地区中介的一线观察一致。

## 数字：马来西亚跃居中国买家第四大目的地

居外数据显示，2026年上半年马来西亚在中国买家咨询榜位列第四，仅次于传统热门。该平台联合创始人兼首席执行官安萨里（Kashif Ansari）称，马来西亚“对香港与内地买家非常受欢迎，尤其作为生活方式、教育、退休或居留挂钩的投资”。香港亚洲银行家俱乐部创始人金斯顿·莱（Kingston Lai）则把需求归因于生活方式偏好与经济走强——马来西亚上半年增长5.7%。排名是标题，两年从第七爬到第四才是趋势。咨询榜是先行指标，在资金流动之前捕捉意图，比单季成交更有意义；它也说明谁在看——不只是逐利的投资者，还有为学校和居留探路的家庭。

## 卡在新马泰之间的那张“中间牌”

分析人士把马来西亚描述成一道取舍题：买家想要新加坡的法治与银行稳定，却不愿付新加坡的溢价；想要泰国的可负担，又不愿接受其偏慢的长期升值。用他们的话说，这是“以泰国的价格，买新加坡的安稳”，还附赠比香港更便宜的国际学校。对决定在哪里安家的家庭而言，生活方式契合加上财务稳定，正是被兜售的产品。

## 价格落差：新加坡是天花板，泰国是地板

这套比较只有在价格鸿沟摊开时才成立。市场数据显示，新加坡住房均价约120万美元（约864万元人民币），泰国仅约18.5万美元（约133万元人民币）——这道鸿沟足以把被门槛挡住的买家推向别处，又不让他们放弃对稳定的追求。新加坡租金回报率仅2.8%，泰国达5.2%，泰国房价年涨幅仅1.5%。两个均值都有遮蔽，但量级并无争议。马来西亚恰卡在中间：制度可信度更近新加坡，成本结构更近泰国。对逐利投资者，算盘又不同：同样的本金在泰国能买到新加坡给不了的现金流——两个市场几乎服务着互不重叠的买家。这种重新定位是相对的：既因买家在别处拒绝了什么，也因马来西亚本身给出了什么。

## 为什么是马来西亚：制度、语言与华人脉络

答案一半平常，一半悠久。马来西亚的法律与制度框架对香港和内地投资者并不陌生，银行体系被认为稳健；与泰国不同，外国人在这里可以购买永久产权房产，包括有地住宅。马来西亚还握着邻国难以复制的牌：庞大而根基深厚的华人社群，以及对中文友好的环境，让移居不像移民，更像在同一文化圈里横向平移。对华南买家尤其如此——华人脉络把一次置业决策，变成语言、学制与社群的延续。医疗体系长期吸引区域退休客聚集槟城，又添一层；在一个英语和中文都能应付日常、合同与课堂的国家，现实便利本身就是卖点。

## “第二家园”的吸力与代价

“马来西亚第二家园”（MM2H）计划是这股需求的隐形引擎。2025年它收到3172份申请，带来约10亿美元（约72亿元人民币）外资。2024年改革后，每档都强制购房：银档60万林吉特（约90万元人民币）、金档100万林吉特（约150万元人民币）、白金档200万林吉特（约300万元人民币）。但欢迎也有价签：自2026年1月1日起，外资购房印花税从4%升至8%，五年内转售还要缴30%房产利得税。门是敞开的，退路是要缴税的。中国申请者高度集中，也在马来西亚国内引来政治关注：旅游部长张庆信呼吁勿将其政治化，强调计划面向全球；业界人士则指出，外籍居民每年为经济贡献超过842亿林吉特（约1263亿元人民币）。

## 香港“亏本卖房”的推力

时机并非偶然，香港自身正在向外输出资本。2026年9月，香港至少100套二手住宅亏本成交，高于8月的81套——尽管代理行估计全年楼价可能上涨15%。头条式复苏与增多的亏本成交并存，恰是这种矛盾信号把资金推向海外。对在本地割肉的卖家来说，把套现所得投向回报更清晰的市场，吸引力不言而喻。

## 柔佛与数据中心：新叙事的副线

生活叙事之外，多了一条产业副线。与新加坡一桥之隔的柔佛新山，自2024年以来数据中心容量增长132%，带动周边住房需求；柔佛—新加坡经济特区把两城越系越紧，定于2026年底通车的新柔捷运，有望把长堤上一小时的蠕行压缩到几分钟——中介早已围绕它卖房。一桩曾靠退休与学区叫卖的生意，正悄悄长出“基建加就业”的逻辑——尽管它仍抵押在新加坡的引力之下。

## 冷思考：热门不等于稳赚

有两点提醒值得摆上台面。其一，部分区域供应过剩风险真实存在，森林城市（Forest City）——那座因中国收紧资本管制而远未达预期的柔佛巨型项目——仍是前车之鉴，而中国的资本管制至今仍是最大变数。其二，中国买家规模常被夸大。拉欣与科行（Rahim & Co）的希瓦·香卡（Siva Shanker）指出，马来西亚缺乏外资持房的全面数据，中国买家的兴趣虽显眼，体量大体与英国买家相当。没有哪个可信的声音认为，中国第二家园申请者正在重塑马来西亚楼市。马来西亚赢下的是细分市场，不是接管。

## 延伸阅读

- **马来西亚第二家园（MM2H）**：马来西亚长期居留计划，2024年改革为银、金、白金三档，每档均须购房。
- **居外（Juwai IQI）**：覆盖约111国、700万套房源的房产平台，其咨询榜常被引作中国出海置业需求的晴雨表。
- **森林城市（Forest City）**：柔佛面向中国买家的巨型第二家园项目，因中国资本管制停滞，警示需求跑在执行前面。

# Malaysia's Quiet Pitch: Singapore's Comfort at Thailand's Prices

For years, the story of Chinese money buying homes abroad was told in two colours: the safe, expensive gleam of Singapore, and the cheap, relaxed sprawl of Thailand. A new data point suggests a third shade is filling in. According to Juwai IQI, a property portal that tracks roughly 7 million listings across 111 countries, Malaysia ranked fourth among destinations for inquiries from Chinese buyers in the first half of 2026 — up from seventh in 2024 and sixth in 2025. The jump is modest in rank but telling in direction: a country long treated as a bit player is quietly becoming the middle ground that Hong Kong and mainland buyers did not know they were looking for.

The South China Morning Post reported the figures on 4 October. Their significance lies less in a single ranking than in how Chinese outbound property demand is re-sorting itself: away from the two poles of the regional market, toward the country promising a bit of both. Rankings built on inquiries are an imperfect gauge — an enquiry is not a transaction — but they move early, and this year they point the same way as the on-the-ground evidence from agents across the region.

## The numbers: Malaysia climbs to fourth for Chinese buyers

Juwai IQI's first-half 2026 data places Malaysia fourth for inquiries from Chinese buyers, behind the usual leaders. Kashif Ansari, co-founder and group CEO of the portal, called Malaysia "a very popular destination for Hong Kong and mainland Chinese buyers, especially as a lifestyle, education, retirement or residency-linked investment." Kingston Lai, founder and CEO of Hong Kong-headquartered Asia Bankers Club, attributed the demand to lifestyle preferences and a firmer economy — Malaysia grew 5.7 per cent in the first half of 2026. The ranking is the headline; the two-year climb from seventh to fourth is the trend. Inquiry rankings are a leading indicator: they capture intent before money moves, which makes the trajectory more meaningful than any single quarter's transactions. They also say something about who is looking — not just investors chasing yield, but families scouting schools and residency. Whether intent converts into titles remains to be seen, but intent is where every cycle starts.

## The card that sits between Singapore and Thailand

Analysts frame Malaysia as a deliberate trade-off. Buyers want the rule of law and banking stability that Singapore offers, without paying Singapore's premium; they want Thailand's accessibility without accepting its slower long-term appreciation. The pitch, in the analysts' own words, is Singapore's stability at Thailand's prices — with international schools cheaper than Hong Kong's thrown in. For a family deciding where to settle, that combination of lifestyle fit and financial stability is the product being sold.

## The price gap: Singapore's ceiling, Thailand's floor

The comparison only works once the price chasm is laid bare. Market data puts Singapore's average home price at about US$1.2 million, against roughly US$185,000 in Thailand — a gap wide enough to push priced-out buyers elsewhere without making them abandon the search for stability. Singapore's rental yield sits at a modest 2.8 per cent, Thailand offers 5.2 per cent, and Thai home prices are growing just 1.5 per cent a year. Both averages hide as much as they reveal, but the order of magnitude is not in dispute. Malaysia threads between the two: institutional credibility closer to Singapore's, cost structures closer to Thailand's. For the yield-seeking investor the arithmetic differs again: the same capital buys a stream of income in Thailand that Singapore simply cannot match — which is why the two markets serve largely non-overlapping buyers. The repositioning is relative, driven as much by what buyers are rejecting elsewhere as by what Malaysia itself delivers.

## Why Malaysia: institutions, language and the Chinese diaspora

Part of the answer is mundane, part of it is historical. Malaysia's legal and institutional framework feels familiar to Hong Kong and mainland investors, and its banking system is regarded as stable; unlike Thailand, it lets foreigners buy freehold property, including landed homes. But Malaysia also holds a card its neighbours cannot easily replicate: a large, deep-rooted ethnic Chinese community and a Mandarin-friendly environment, which make relocation feel less like emigration than a lateral move within a shared cultural orbit. For buyers from southern China especially, the diaspora turns a property decision into a continuation of language, schooling and community. The healthcare system, long a draw for regional retirees in Penang, adds another layer; so does the sheer practicality of a country where English and Chinese both work in daily life, contracts and classrooms.

## The pull and the price of "My Second Home"

The Malaysia My Second Home (MM2H) programme has been the quiet engine behind much of this demand. In 2025 it drew 3,172 applications and roughly US$1 billion in foreign investment. Under the 2024 revamp, every tier now requires a property purchase: Silver at RM600,000, Gold at RM1 million and Platinum at RM2 million — about US$138,000, US$230,000 and US$460,000 respectively. But the welcome has a price tag of its own. From 1 January 2026, foreign buyers pay a flat 8 per cent stamp duty on residential transfers, double the previous 4 per cent, and face a 30 per cent gains tax if they sell within five years. The door is open; the exit is taxed. The programme's concentration of Chinese applicants has also drawn political attention in Malaysia: Tourism Minister Tiong King Sing has urged that it not be politicised, noting it accepts applicants worldwide, while industry figures point out that foreign residents contribute more than RM84.2 billion (about US$19 billion) to the economy each year.

## Hong Kong's loss-making sales as a push factor

Timing is no accident, because Hong Kong itself is exporting capital. At least 100 secondary homes in the city sold at a loss in September 2026, up from 81 in August — even as agents estimate citywide prices could finish the year up 15 per cent. A headline recovery sitting beside rising loss-making sales is precisely the mixed signal that sends money abroad. For sellers absorbing a loss at home, redeploying the proceeds into a market with clearer yields has obvious appeal. The arithmetic is legible enough: a Hong Kong flat sold at a discount can fund an outright purchase in Kuala Lumpur or Penang with change to spare — and schooling costs a fraction of what it would at home.

## Johor and the data-centre subplot

The lifestyle story now has an industrial sequel. Johor Bahru, the city across the causeway from Singapore, has seen data-centre capacity grow 132 per cent since 2024, feeding demand for nearby housing, while the Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone tightens the two cities together — and the Rapid Transit System rail link, scheduled to open by the end of 2026, is expected to compress the hour-long causeway crawl into minutes. Agents are already selling around it. A play once sold on retirement and schooling is quietly acquiring an infrastructure-and-jobs thesis — though one still mortgaged to Singapore's gravity.

## A cooler read: hot demand is not a safe return

Two cautions are worth stating. First, oversupply is a real risk in parts of the market, and Forest City — the Johor mega-project left well short of its projections when China tightened capital controls — remains the cautionary tale; Chinese capital controls are still the biggest wild card: the lesson of the past decade is that controls, not taste, set the ceiling on Chinese outbound buying. Second, the scale of Chinese buying is routinely exaggerated. Siva Shanker of Rahim & Co points out that Malaysia lacks comprehensive data on foreign ownership, and that Chinese interest, while visible, is broadly comparable to that from British buyers. Nobody credible claims Chinese MM2H applicants are reshaping the Malaysian housing market. Malaysia is winning a niche, not presiding over a takeover.

- **Juwai IQI**: A property portal tracking roughly 7 million listings across 111 countries; its inquiry rankings are a widely cited proxy for Chinese outbound property demand.
- **Forest City**: A Johor mega-development pitched as a second-home hub for Chinese buyers, later stalled by China's capital controls — a warning about demand outrunning execution.
