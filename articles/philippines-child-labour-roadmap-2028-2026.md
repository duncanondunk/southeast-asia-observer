# 一张通往2028的路线图：菲律宾能否兑现"消灭童工"的承诺？

马尼拉（Manila）正在起草一份国家路线图，要在2028年前消除童工；但分析人士直言，真正的对手是贫困，而不是文件。

## 现场：棉兰老岛以南，120名童工的"解救"

在菲律宾（Philippines）南部的邦萨摩洛（Bangsamoro）地区，劳动监察人员今年从市场和街头摊贩中解救出至少120名童工——有的孩子不得不靠打零工贴补家用或满足日常所需。该地区的劳工部长穆萨利明·塞马（Muslimin Sema）表示，这些孩子的父母和监护人在康复计划中每人领到了1.5万比索（约239美元，约合1720元人民币）。过去三年，这一计划已覆盖邦萨摩洛地区近4000名童工。这只是马尼拉眼下声称要在2028年前彻底解决的问题的一帧缩影，却也最直观地暴露了问题的底色：童工往往不是被"外人"拐走，而是被自家生存账本逼上了街头。

## 数字：三年救出23万余人，仍在册50.9万

菲律宾劳工部（DOLE）称，作为"2028年前消除童工"行动的一部分，过去三年它已从童工处境中解救出232966名儿童。菲律宾统计局（Philippine Statistics Authority）的数据则显示，从事童工的儿童数量在2024年降至50.9万，低于2022年的82.8万。菲律宾大学迪利曼分校的社会工作学者弗洛伦斯·弗洛雷斯—帕索斯（Florence Flores-Pasos）指出："下降是有意义的，但剩余数字仍然庞大，因为童工的根源在于家庭贫困、脆弱的乡村生计、上学障碍以及非正式工作中的薄弱保护，而不单是父母的选择或法律的缺失。"换句话说，数字在好转，但好转的速度赶不上是被什么拖住的。

## 根源：贫困、失学与"非正式就业"的三重夹击

南华早报（South China Morning Post）的报道揭示了一个政策文件难以扭转的结构性事实：菲律宾的童工问题由相互勾连的因素驱动。贫困迫使家庭把孩子送上工；优质教育的门槛把孩子拽出课堂；而庞大的非正式经济——家政、农业、渔业、街头贩卖——既无保护，也无执法的触角。国际劳工组织补充说，气候冲击和社会保护的缺口让这种挤压雪上加霜。把邦萨摩洛的那些孩子重新送回学校，需要的不是一次"解救行动"，而是一张能托住整个家庭的保障网。

## 政策：一张通往2028的路线图

9月23日至24日，菲律宾政府官员、民间团体与福利机构在马尼拉聚首，起草这份路线图，并重申终结童工与人口贩运的承诺。这一动作紧随劳工部2025年表达加入"第八点七同盟"（Alliance 8.7）意向之后——该全球伙伴关系建立在联合国可持续发展目标第八项目标（SDG Target 8.7）之上，呼吁消除强迫劳动、现代奴役、人口贩运与最恶劣形式的童工。成为"探路者国家"意味着要磨快立法、国家行动计划与执法。国际劳工组织强调，这一努力远早于任何关税政治：马尼拉已为此耕耘多年，并由这一联合国机构提供技术支援。

## 区域镜鉴：东南亚560万童工之困

菲律宾并非孤例。国际劳工组织估算，整个东南亚（Southeast Asia）约有560万儿童身处劳动之中，其中370万从事危险工作。该机构9月发布的一份缅甸报告发现，经济脆弱与受教育机会有限，正把年仅12岁的儿童推进建筑、农业等危险工种。印尼（Indonesia）反复上演的烟霾与洪灾、以及该地区庞大的非正式部门，让数百万家庭随时可能因一次冲击而把孩子拉出校门。菲律宾的路线图，本质上是一场区域性的压力测试：一个中低收入国家，能否让承诺转化为保护的速度，快过贫困将其消解的速度？

## 隐忧：承诺易写，贫困难消

这里要抛出一个观察评论式的尖锐问题：2028年究竟是里程碑，还是海市蜃楼？数字确实朝对的方向移动，但驱动它的引擎——贫困、脆弱的生计、非正式工作中疲软的执法——恰恰是没有哪份路线图能单独关掉的。弗洛雷斯—帕索斯直白地说，法律与计划必要却不充分。邦萨摩洛的解救，无论那笔1.5万比索的补贴听起来多么慷慨，治的只是症状；真正的药方，在于对弱势家庭的现金支持、对上学的普惠式保障，以及去监管非正式经济（多数童工藏身其中）的政治意愿。马尼拉当然可以领衔东南亚的这场战役——但前提是，它打击贫困的决心，要像起草计划一样认真。

## 延伸阅读

**第八点七同盟（Alliance 8.7）**：建立在联合国可持续发展目标第八项目标之上的全球伙伴关系，敦促各国消除强迫劳动、现代奴役、人口贩运与最恶劣形式的童工；菲律宾正争取成为"探路者国家"。

**童工（child labour）**：剥夺儿童童年、潜能与尊严，并损害其身心发展的劳动；国际劳工组织按年龄与条件，将其与可接受的轻量工作区分开来。

**危险童工（hazardous child labour）**：最恶劣的形式之一，包括可能危害儿童健康、安全或道德的工作；国际劳工组织统计东南亚此类童工达370万。

# The Philippines' Quiet Gamble: Can a Roadmap End Child Labour by 2028?

Manila is drafting a national roadmap to wipe out child labour by 2028 — but analysts say the real opponent is poverty, not paperwork.

## The scene: 120 children rescued in the south

In the Bangsamoro region of southern Philippines, labour inspectors pulled at least 120 children out of markets and street vending this year — some pushed into work to meet daily needs or top up their parents' income. The regional labour minister, Muslimin Sema, said the children's parents and guardians each received 15,000 pesos (about US$239) through a rehabilitation programme. Over the past three years, the effort has reached nearly 4,000 child labourers across Bangsamoro. It is a grim snapshot of a problem Manila now says it wants to close out by 2028 — and it exposes the real colour of that problem: child labourers are rarely "taken" by strangers; far more often they are driven onto the street by their own household's survival arithmetic.

## The numbers: 233,000 rescued, yet 509,000 remain

The Philippine Department of Labour and Employment (DOLE) says it rescued 232,966 children from child labour over the past three years as part of a campaign to eliminate the practice by 2028. According to the Philippine Statistics Authority, the count of children in labour fell to 509,000 in 2024, down from 828,000 in 2022. "The decline is meaningful, but the remaining number is large," notes Florence Flores-Pasos, assistant professor at the University of the Philippines Diliman, "because child labour is rooted in household poverty, insecure rural livelihoods, barriers to schooling and weak protection in informal work — not simply in parents' choices or a lack of laws." The figures are moving in the right direction; the question is what is holding them back.

## The roots: poverty, school dropouts and informal work

The SCMP reporting underscores a structural truth that policy papers struggle to bend: child labour in the Philippines is driven by interconnected factors. Poverty pushes families to send children to work; barriers to quality education pull them out of classrooms; and the sprawling informal economy — domestic work, agriculture, fishing, street vending — offers neither protection nor enforcement. The International Labour Organization adds that climate shocks and gaps in social protection compound the squeeze. Getting those Bangsamoro children back to school requires not a one-off "rescue operation" but a safety net that can hold up the entire family.

## The policy: a roadmap to 2028

On September 23 and 24, government officials, civil society groups and welfare organisations convened in Manila to draft the roadmap and reaffirm a commitment to ending child labour and human trafficking. The push follows the Department of Labour's 2025 expression of interest in becoming an Alliance 8.7 Pathfinder Country — a global partnership built on UN Sustainable Development Goal Target 8.7, which calls for eradicating forced labour, modern slavery, human trafficking and the worst forms of child labour. Pathfinder status means sharpening legislation, national action plans and enforcement. The ILO stresses the effort predates any tariff politics: Manila has worked on this for years, with the UN agency providing technical support.

## Regional mirror: 5.6 million child labourers across Southeast Asia

The Philippines is not alone. The ILO estimates about 5.6 million children are engaged in labour across Southeast Asia, with 3.7 million in hazardous conditions. A September ILO report on Myanmar found economic vulnerability and limited schooling driving children as young as 12 into hazardous work in construction, agriculture and other trades. Indonesia's recurrent haze and flood disasters, and the region's vast informal sector, keep millions of households one shock away from pulling a child out of school. The Philippine roadmap is, in effect, a regional stress test: can a lower-middle-income country turn a pledge into protection faster than poverty can undo it?

## The catch: pledges are easy, poverty is stubborn

Here is the uncomfortable question a commentary demands: will 2028 be a milestone or a mirage? The numbers have moved in the right direction, but the engine behind them — poverty, insecure livelihoods, weak enforcement in informal work — is exactly what no roadmap alone can switch off. Flores-Pasos is blunt that laws and plans are necessary but not sufficient. The Bangsamoro rescue, generous as the 15,000-peso stipend sounds, treats the symptom; the cure lies in cash support for vulnerable families, universal access to schooling, and the political will to police the informal economy where most child labour hides. Manila can lead Southeast Asia's fight — but only if it fights poverty as seriously as it drafts plans.

## Further reading

**Alliance 8.7**: A global partnership under UN SDG Target 8.7 that urges states to eradicate forced labour, modern slavery, human trafficking and the worst forms of child labour; the Philippines is seeking Pathfinder status.

**Child labour**: Work that deprives children of childhood, potential and dignity, and is harmful to physical and mental development; the ILO distinguishes it from acceptable light work by age and conditions.

**Hazardous child labour**: The worst forms — including work likely to harm the health, safety or morals of children — which the ILO counts at 3.7 million across Southeast Asia.
