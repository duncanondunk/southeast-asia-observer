# 绕开南中国海：一条美资海底光缆如何重绘东南亚数字版图

海底光缆承载着全球九成五到九成九的国际互联网流量，是数字经济的“血管”。9月14日，美国贸易发展署宣布资助一项名为“泰国—美国海底光缆系统”（THUS）的可行性研究——一条全长约一万八千五百公里、刻意绕开南中国海、连接泰国并延伸至新加坡、印度尼西亚、马来西亚、菲律宾与越南五个东盟国家的跨太平洋光缆。表面看，这是一条替代泰国老旧对美线路的通信基础设施；往深里看，这是华盛顿在印太海底悄然落子的最新一步，把中美科技竞争从芯片与5G，一路压到了海床。

## 一条被悄然资助的光缆

美国贸易发展署在9月14日发布声明，为泰美光缆系统项目提供可行性研究资金。这条设计全长一万一千五百英里（约一万八千五百公里）的光缆，将完全采用美国工程与安全标准建造，旨在替代泰国现役、已显老化的对美海底光缆。泰国国有企业国家电信公共有限公司已选定总部位于佛罗里达的美国佛罗里达的电信服务商承担前期研究。美国贸易发展署副署长托马斯·哈迪在声明中称，此举将确保连接泰国与美国的“关键基础设施”采用“可信技术（trusted technology）”标准建造，防止敏感数据被利用。据规划，泰美光缆系统除连接泰国外，还将串联新加坡、印度尼西亚、马来西亚、菲律宾与越南。

值得玩味的是，这仍只是一笔“可行性研究”的资金，而非建缆承诺。也就是说，华盛顿先以小额前期投入锁定路线与标准的话语权，把“可信技术”写进项目基因；至于真正动辄上百亿美元的铺设成本，则留给后续融资与东盟伙伴去扛。这是一种低投入、高杠杆的地缘布局——花小钱，定规矩。

## 为什么要绕开南中国海

泰美光缆系统最引人注目的设计，是路线刻意避开南中国海及其他“存在安全风险”的水域。据国际战略研究所（IISS）评估，2022年至2030年间，预计将有近二十条国际海底光缆横跨太平洋铺设，而其中相当一部分原本会经过这片争议海域。

绕行并非技术必需，而是安全算计。中国在南中国海等战略水道提出广泛主权主张，使新光缆的路由、登陆点与供应商选择都复杂化。对华盛顿而言，把数据动脉铺在竞争对手主张主权的海底，等于把国家安全的命脉交到别人手里。于是，“绕开”成了政治正确：多绕几百公里、多花数亿美元，换来的是路由“干净”、归因清晰、不被轻易切断。须知，海底光缆虽是民用基础设施，却早已被大国视为可武器化的战略资产——近年来波罗的海与台湾周边屡屡出现缆线被切断或受损的事件，虽多为灰色地带、难以归因，却足以让规划者寝食难安。

## 两条平行架构，同一片海底

泰美光缆系统不是孤立项目，而是美国“清洁网络（Clean Network）”倡议在海底的延伸。自2020年美方推出该倡议以来，华盛顿通过立法与外交，系统性地将中国企业排除在关键光缆项目之外：太平洋光缆网络（PLCN）因顾虑香港节点被取消；东南亚—中东—西欧6号（SeaMeWe-6）项目原本选中中国供应商，在美国施压下最终改由美国一家海底光缆工程企业承建；东密克罗尼西亚光缆也被重组为美日澳主导。2026年，美国更推出《战略海底光缆法案》，把对俄中破坏缆线的防范写入法律。

与此相对，中国以华为海洋（HMN科技）与“数字丝绸之路”为支点，在亚洲、非洲、中东与拉美铺设低成本、端到端的并行架构，主动绕开西方司法管辖。结果是：海底正缓慢形成两套平行系统，一套锚定华盛顿与布鲁塞尔，一套锚定北京。每条新光缆的落地，都在逼沿线国家做一次“二选一”。这哪还是单纯的通信工程？分明是数字版的阵营化。

## “可信技术”的排他逻辑

美国联邦通信委员会近年持续收紧对海底光缆的审查，对来自“外国对手”实体的登陆许可采取“推定拒绝”，并禁止被列入清单的供应商设备接入美国。美方逻辑自洽：把治理机制用作排除工具，确保数据不被潜在对手截取。

但问题也由此而生：当全球最大的光缆强国把治理机制武器化，这些机制还能否被称为“中立”？排他本身也是一种姿态——它迫使盟友与伙伴在路由、供应商与标准上向美方靠拢，客观上压缩了第三国（包括东盟国家）的技术与供应商选择空间。所谓“可信”，最终往往等同于“可信赖美国”。对中小国家而言，这既是安全保障，也是被绑上战车的代价。

## 东南亚的“中间道路”被改写

对东盟而言，泰美光缆系统的诱惑与压力并存。六个东盟国家被纳入同一条“美国标准”光缆，意味着区域数字联通有望获得更稳定的对美直连，支撑数据中心、云计算与人工智能产业——泰国国家电信总裁讪巴猜便直言，泰美光缆系统将增强国际网络的“韧性与多样性”，并服务区域六亿人口市场。

但“中间道路”的叙事正被悄悄改写。长期以来，东南亚以“对冲”立足：在中美之间左右逢源，既接中国的“数字丝绸之路”，又享美国的资本与市场。如今，当一条光缆以“避开中国主张水域”为设计前提、以“可信技术”为排他门槛，东盟的“不选边”空间被实质压缩。你可以用中国的设备，也可以接美国的光缆，但当两套系统彼此不兼容、且都要求排他性信任时，“两边都不得罪”正在变成“两边都得表态”。这恰是中小国家最不愿面对的处境。

## 安全的代价：更长的线路与集中风险

绕开南中国海并非免费。行业估算显示，2025至2027年间，全球新建海底光缆总投资将超过一百四十亿美元（约一千亿元人民币）；而现代带中继系统的单位铺设成本约为每公里四万五千至六万五千美元（约合每公里三十二万至四十六万元人民币）。为规避主权声索而多绕的一百公里，在系统层面就是一笔可观的溢价。

更隐蔽的风险是“集中”。为绕开争议水域，流量被迫向少数“安全”登陆点聚集。以菲律宾为例，其境内已有十余条国际光缆、另有数条在建，地理上本不必如此集中；恰恰是“避华”逻辑，把过多流量压到同一群岛，反而制造了单点故障——十七八条缆线汇聚于一地，一旦遭遇事故或蓄意破坏，区域性断网的风险被放大而非降低。一项旨在“降低风险”的政策，竟制造出新的、更集中的风险。此之谓“以安全之名，行脆弱之实”。

## 谁出钱，谁定标准

美国贸易发展署并未公布泰美光缆系统的具体造价，但声明直言，对齐美国工程标准，将为美国企业创造出口“可信硬件、软件与安装服务”的机会。换言之，前期可行性研究不仅定路线，更为后续美国供应商锁定入口。

这是一笔典型的“标准即权力”的生意。谁出资、谁定标准、谁供设备，谁就掌握了未来数十年区域数据流向的阀门。对东南亚而言，接受美国标准意味着更顺畅的西方资本与技术接入，却也可能在关键基础设施上形成对美依赖，削弱自身的议价能力与战略自主。历史地看，通信基础设施的标准之争，从来不只是技术问题，而是秩序之争。

## 一条光缆背后的区域秩序投射

据国际战略研究所数据，2022至2030年横跨太平洋的新建光缆近二十条；海底光缆承载的每日金融交易额以万亿美元计。泰美光缆系统只是其中一条，却极具象征意义。

一条光缆，照见的是整个印太的秩序投射。华盛顿以“可行性研究”的小投入，换取在东南亚安全数字网络中的深度绑定；北京以“数字丝绸之路”的低成本方案，拓展全球连接版图；而夹在中间的东盟，正被迫在“可信”与“可负担”、“安全”与“自主”之间做艰难取舍。海底没有硝烟，却已是前沿。东南亚想要的，或许从来不是选边，而是一条既连得上世界、又不被任何人掐住喉咙的“血管”。这条泰美光缆系统光缆，能否承载这样的期待，仍要打个大问号。

## 延伸阅读

**海底光缆（Submarine cable）**：铺设在海底、承载国际互联网与金融数据流量的光纤通信线路，被称为“全球互联网的骨干”，约九成五以上国际流量经此传输。

**清洁网络计划（Clean Network）**：美国国务院2020年推出的倡议，意在通信基础设施中排除所谓“不可信”供应商，涵盖海底光缆、应用商店、云等多个层面。

**数字丝绸之路（Digital Silk Road）**：中国“一带一路”在数字经济领域的延伸，包括海底光缆、数据中心与通信网络建设，重点投向亚洲、非洲、中东与拉美。

# Bypassing the South China Sea: How a US-Backed Submarine Cable Redraws Southeast Asia's Digital Map

Submarine cables carry 95 to 99 per cent of the world's international internet traffic — the arteries of the digital economy. On 14 September, the US Trade and Development Agency (USTDA) announced funding for a feasibility study of the Thailand–US Submarine Cable System (THUS): a trans-Pacific cable of roughly 18,500 km designed to deliberately avoid the South China Sea and link Thailand with five other ASEAN states — Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Vietnam. On the surface, it is a piece of communications infrastructure replacing Thailand's ageing US link. Look closer, and it is Washington's latest move in a quiet land grab on the Indo-Pacific seabed, pushing the US–China tech rivalry from chips and 5G down to the ocean floor.

## The cable Washington is quietly underwriting

The USTDA said on 14 September it would fund the THUS feasibility study. The proposed cable, at 11,500 miles (about 18,507 km), would be built entirely to US engineering and security standards, replacing Thailand's ageing subsea connection to the United States. Thailand's state-owned National Telecom Public Company has tapped Florida-based APTelecom to conduct the study. USTDA Deputy Director Thomas Hardy said the project would ensure the "critical infrastructure" linking the two countries is built to "trusted technology" standards, protecting sensitive data from exploitation. Beyond Thailand, THUS is planned to connect Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and Vietnam.

What is telling is that this is funding for a study, not a commitment to build. Washington is spending a small upfront sum to lock in control over the route and the standards — baking "trusted technology" into the project's DNA — while leaving the multi-billion-dollar laying cost to future financing and ASEAN partners. It is a low-cost, high-leverage move: spend little, set the rules.

## Why bypass the South China Sea

The most striking design choice is the route's deliberate avoidance of the South China Sea and other "security risk" waters. According to the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS), nearly 20 international submarine cables are expected to be laid across the Pacific between 2022 and 2030, a large share of which would otherwise have passed through the disputed sea.

Detouring is not a technical necessity; it is a security calculation. China's sweeping sovereignty claims across the South China Sea and other strategic waterways have complicated the routing, landing points and supplier choices of new cables. For Washington, laying the data artery on the seabed a rival claims is to hand a rival the chokehold on national security. Hence "avoidance" becomes politically correct: route a few hundred kilometres longer, spend a few hundred million dollars more, and gain a "clean" path with clear attribution and low cut-off risk. Submarine cables, though civilian infrastructure, are now treated by great powers as weaponisable strategic assets — repeated cable cuts near the Baltic and around Taiwan, mostly grey-zone and hard to attribute, are enough to keep planners awake at night.

## Two parallel architectures on one seabed

THUS is not isolated; it is the Clean Network initiative extended to the seabed. Since Washington launched that initiative in 2020, it has systematically excluded Chinese firms from critical cable projects through legislation and diplomacy: the Pacific Light Cable Network (PLCN) lost its Hong Kong segment over security concerns; the Southeast Asia–Middle East–Western Europe 6 (SeaMeWe-6) project, initially awarded to a Chinese supplier, was shifted to the US firm SubCom under American pressure; the East Micronesia Cable was restructured under a US–Japan–Australia build. In 2026, the US went further with a Strategic Subsea Cables Act, codifying defences against suspected Russian and Chinese sabotage.

China, for its part, uses HMN Technologies (formerly Huawei Marine) and the Digital Silk Road to lay low-cost, end-to-end parallel architecture across Asia, Africa, the Middle East and Latin America, deliberately routing around Western jurisdiction. The result: two parallel systems are slowly taking shape on the seabed — one anchored in Washington and Brussels, the other in Beijing. Every new cable forces littoral states to make a choice. This is no longer mere communications engineering; it is digital bloc-building.

## The exclusionary logic of "trusted technology"

The US Federal Communications Commission has tightened submarine-cable review, adopting a "presumption of denial" for landing licences linked to "foreign adversary" entities and banning listed vendors' equipment from US-connected systems. The logic is self-consistent: weaponise governance mechanisms to keep data from potential adversaries.

But a problem follows. When the world's leading cable power turns governance into an exclusion tool, can those mechanisms still be called "neutral"? Exclusion is itself a posture — it pushes allies and partners to align with Washington on routing, suppliers and standards, objectively shrinking the technological and supplier choices of third countries, including ASEAN states. "Trusted" in the end often means "trustworthy to the United States." For smaller states, this is both a security guarantee and the price of being harnessed to a great-power chariot.

## Southeast Asia's "middle path" rewritten

For ASEAN, THUS brings both temptation and pressure. Six member states are drawn into one "US-standard" cable, promising more stable direct US connectivity and support for data centres, cloud computing and AI — Colonel Sanpachai Huvanandana, president of Thailand's National Telecom, noted plainly that THUS would strengthen the "resilience and diversity" of international networks and serve a regional market of over 600 million people.

But the "middle path" narrative is being quietly rewritten. For years, Southeast Asia hedged — courting both China's Digital Silk Road and American capital and markets. Now, when a cable is designed around avoiding waters China claims and gated by "trusted technology," ASEAN's "non-alignment" space is materially compressed. You may use Chinese equipment and ride American cables, but when the two systems are mutually incompatible and each demands exclusive trust, "offending no one" becomes "declaring for someone." That is precisely the predicament smaller states least want to face.

## The price of security: longer routes and concentration risk

Avoiding the South China Sea is not free. Industry estimates put global new-cable investment at over US$14 billion (around S$17.8 billion, ~US$14 billion) between 2025 and 2027; a modern repeatered system costs roughly US$45,000–65,000 per km. An extra 100 km routed to evade sovereignty claims is a material premium at the system level.

The more insidious risk is concentration. To dodge contested waters, traffic is forced toward a few "safe" landing points. Take the Philippines: it already hosts over a dozen international cables with several more under construction — geographically unnecessary concentration. It is precisely the "avoid-China" logic that piles too much traffic onto one archipelago, creating a single point of failure: when seventeen or eighteen cables converge on one place, an accident or deliberate cut magnifies, rather than reduces, the risk of regional outage. A policy meant to lower risk manufactures a new, more concentrated one. Security purchased, fragility delivered.

## Who pays sets the standards

USTDA has not disclosed THUS's cost, but its statement is explicit: aligning the project with US engineering standards creates opportunities for American firms to export "trusted hardware, software and installation services." In other words, the upfront feasibility study locks not just the route but the entry point for subsequent US suppliers.

This is a classic "standards are power" play. Whoever funds, sets the standards, and supplies the equipment holds the valve over the region's data flows for decades. For Southeast Asia, accepting US standards means smoother access to Western capital and technology, but also risks dependency on the US for critical infrastructure, eroding bargaining power and strategic autonomy. Historically, the contest over communications-infrastructure standards was never merely technical — it is a contest over order.

## What one cable reveals about regional order

IISS data show nearly 20 new Pacific cables between 2022 and 2030, carrying daily financial flows counted in trillions of US dollars. THUS is just one, yet it is emblematic.

One cable mirrors the entire Indo-Pacific order. Washington spends a small "feasibility" sum to deepen its binding with Southeast Asia's secure digital network; Beijing extends its global connectivity through the Digital Silk Road's low-cost model; and ASEAN, caught in between, is forced into hard trade-offs between "trusted" and "affordable," "secure" and "autonomous." There is no smoke on the seabed, yet it is already a frontier. What Southeast Asia wants may never be choosing sides, but an artery that connects to the world without being throttled by anyone. Whether THUS can carry that hope remains a very open question.

## Further reading

**Submarine cable**: Fibre-optic lines laid on the seabed carrying international internet and financial data traffic — the "backbone of the global internet," through which over 95 per cent of international traffic passes.

**Clean Network**: A US State Department initiative launched in 2020 to exclude so-called "untrusted" suppliers from communications infrastructure, spanning submarine cables, app stores and cloud, among others.

**Digital Silk Road**: China's Belt and Road extension into the digital economy, comprising submarine cables, data centres and communications networks, focused on Asia, Africa, the Middle East and Latin America.
