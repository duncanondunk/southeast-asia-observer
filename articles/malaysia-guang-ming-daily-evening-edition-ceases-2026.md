# 一份晚报的落幕：马来西亚《光明日报》晚报停刊与华文媒体的转身

10月31日，马来西亚《光明日报》晚报将出版最后一期，并从11月1日起停止在北马地区发行。这意味着马来西亚最后一份晚报彻底退场。表面看，这是一份报纸发行安排的调整；往深里看，它折射的是华文纸媒在数字洪流中的集体转身，以及马来西亚华社“文化骨骼”的一次细微松动。

## 一份晚报的退场：北马读者的“傍晚仪式”将成记忆

《光明日报》晚报主要服务北马（槟城一带）读者，对其中一部分人而言，傍晚翻开报纸、看看一天发生了什么，早已成为生活的一部分。如今这个习惯正被手机取代。报方给出的理由很直白——读者从"等报纸送到手上"变成"拿起手机就能掌握最新消息"。这话讲得克制，却点破了一种普遍焦虑：当纸本不再是获取资讯的第一入口，依附于纸本的那套生活仪式感，也一并被冲刷掉。

## 从星槟到光明：一份报纸九十余年的华人社区坐标

《光明日报》的前身是槟城《星槟日报》，1986年因财务危机停刊，原班人马于1987年在民政党（Gerakan）人士支持下创办《光明日报》；1992年并入张晓卿旗下的常青集团，2004年加入星洲媒体集团，与《星洲日报》等同属世界华文媒体有限公司（Media Chinese International，港交所代码00685）。按2004年尼尔森（Nielsen）数据，《光明日报》日均读者42.4万人，为马来西亚第三大华文报。换句话说，它从来不只是一份晚报，更是北马华人社区九十余年的信息坐标与情感纽带。

## 三支柱之一：华文报为何是马来西亚华社的“文化骨骼”

在马来西亚，华文报、华团与华文教育被并称为华社“三大支柱”。华文报不只是新闻载体，更是华人语言、身份与社区记忆的“保存器”。当一份报纸收缩，缩掉的不仅是版面，还有社区自我叙述的一条通道。对一个以移民后裔为主体的族群而言，母语文媒的存续，本身就是文化安全的刻度。

## 数字洪流：不是读者消失，而是阅读场景迁移

报方说读者习惯变了，这话不假，但只对了一半。更冷的现实是广告收入的溃散。《光明日报》的母公司世界华文媒体已连续14个季度亏损，2025/2026财年营收仅微增0.1%至约1.52亿美元，净亏损却扩大1.1倍至1603万美元；其马来西亚出版印刷板块营收降6.9%、由盈转亏。广告预算正从报纸涌向抖音国际版（TikTok）、脸书（Facebook）与直播带货。读者没有消失，只是把注意力搬了家。

## 最后的堡垒：为何马来西亚华文报仍是东南亚最坚韧的

放眼东南亚，华文报的处境天差地别。印尼曾长期禁绝华文报刊（1965—1994），泰国、越南的华文报业日渐式微，唯有马来西亚，华文报仍以大众媒体形态存活——《星洲日报》《中国报》《光明日报》《南洋商报》四份日报并立。此次晚报退场，更像是一次"瘦身"而非崩塌：日报与电子报仍在，倒下的是黄昏时段的细分市场。从区域比较看，马来西亚仍是海外华文纸媒最后的堡垒。

## 电子报能接住纸本的“文化重量”吗

《光明日报》表示，纸本停发后，将透过日报与"光明电子报"（每天下午6时上线）继续与读者见面。技术通道留住了，但文化重量未必留得住。算法推荐与平台依赖，意味着社区自我叙述的钥匙，正越来越多地交到抖音国际版、脸书这类外部守门人手中。电子报能递送新闻，却未必能递送那份"傍晚翻报"所承载的社区黏性与文化安定感。这，才是晚报落幕真正值得追问的地方。

## 延伸阅读

**华社三支柱（Three Pillars of Huashang）**：指马来西亚华人社会的华文报、华团与华文教育，被视为维系华人语言、身份与社区认同的核心制度。

**世界华文媒体有限公司（Media Chinese International）**：在港交所与马来西亚证交所双边上市的华文媒体集团，旗下拥有《星洲日报》《中国报》《光明日报》《南洋商报》，是马来西亚华文报业的主要操盘者。

**电子报（E-paper）**：传统报纸的数字版本，保留版面排版但改由网络与移动端分发，是纸媒应对数字冲击的主要转型路径。

# The last evening paper: Guang Ming Daily's closure and the turn of Malaysia's Chinese-language press

On October 31, Malaysia's Guang Ming Daily evening edition will publish its final issue and stop circulating in northern Peninsular Malaysia from November 1. With it, the country's last evening newspaper disappears. On the surface this is a routine adjustment to a paper's distribution plan. Look closer, and it reflects the collective turn of Chinese-language print media in the digital tide — and a small, telling loosening of the "cultural backbone" of Malaysia's Chinese community.

## The last evening paper: a North Malaysian ritual ends

The evening edition served readers in northern Malaysia, around Penang, where flipping through the paper at dusk had become part of daily life for some. That habit is now being displaced by the smartphone. The paper's stated reason is plain: readers have moved from "waiting for the paper at the door" to "picking up the phone for the latest news." The phrasing is restrained, yet it names a wider anxiety — when print is no longer the first portal to information, the rituals of life built around it are washed away as well.

## From Sin Pin to Guang Ming: a ninety-year community anchor

Guang Ming Daily traces its lineage to the Penang-based Sin Pin Daily, which closed in 1986 amid financial trouble; its original team launched Guang Ming in 1987 with support from a Gerakan politician. In 1992 it was acquired by the Heng Hup group linked to tycoon Tiong Hiew King, and in 2004 it joined Sin Chew Media, today part of Media Chinese International (HKEX: 00685). By Nielsen data in 2004, Guang Ming reached 424,000 daily readers, making it Malaysia's third-largest Chinese newspaper. In other words, it was never merely an evening paper — it was a nine-decade information anchor and emotional tie for the northern Malaysian Chinese community.

## One of three pillars: why the Chinese press is the cultural backbone of Huashang

In Malaysia, the Chinese-language press, Chinese associations and Chinese-medium education are known as the "three pillars" of the Chinese community. The press is not only a news carrier; it is a repository of language, identity and collective memory. When a paper contracts, what shrinks is not only its page count but one channel of the community's self-narration. For a people descended largely from immigrants, the survival of a mother-tongue press is itself a measure of cultural security.

## The digital tide: not readers vanishing, but reading migrating

The paper says reading habits have changed — true, but only half the story. The colder reality is the collapse of advertising revenue. Guang Ming's parent, Media Chinese International, has now posted losses for 14 consecutive quarters; in FY2025/26 revenue edged up just 0.1% to about US$152 million while net loss widened 1.1-fold to US$16.03 million, and its Malaysian publishing and printing segment saw revenue fall 6.9% and swing to a loss. Ad budgets are flowing from newspapers to TikTok, Facebook and livestream commerce. Readers have not vanished; they have simply moved house with their attention.

## The last fortress: why Malaysia's Chinese press stays Southeast Asia's most resilient

Across Southeast Asia the fate of Chinese-language newspapers diverges sharply. Indonesia banned Chinese-language publications for decades (1965–1994); Thailand and Vietnam saw their Chinese press wither. Only in Malaysia do Chinese dailies survive as mass media, with Sin Chew, China Press, Guang Ming and Nanyang Siang Pau standing side by side. The evening edition's exit looks less like a collapse than a "slimming down" — the dailies and e-paper endure, and what fell was merely the niche of the evening slot. By regional comparison, Malaysia remains the last fortress of overseas Chinese print media.

## Can the e-paper carry print's cultural weight?

Guang Ming says that after the print stop, it will stay in touch through its daily paper and the "Guang Ming E-Paper," going live at 6 p.m. each day. The technical channel is preserved; the cultural weight may not be. Algorithmic recommendation and platform dependency mean the key to the community's self-narration is increasingly handed to external gatekeepers such as TikTok and Meta. An e-paper can deliver news, but it may not deliver the community cohesion and cultural steadiness carried by the simple act of "flipping through the evening paper." That, rather than the closure itself, is what the last evening paper's end truly asks us to ponder.

## Further reading

**Media Chinese International**: a dual-listed (HKEX and Bursa Malaysia) Chinese-media group owning Sin Chew Daily, China Press, Guang Ming Daily and Nanyang Siang Pau — the principal operator of Malaysia's Chinese newspaper industry.

**E-paper**: the digital edition of a traditional newspaper, retaining its print layout but distributed via web and mobile — the main transformation path for print media amid the digital shock.
