# 柬埔寨中文教师缺口背后：一门语言正从侨社课堂走进公立学校

## 六成学生不是华人子弟

上周在北京（Beijing）举行的2026世界中文大会上，一位柬埔寨官员抛出的数字，足以让那些仍把东南亚（Southeast Asia）华文教育当成“华人家里的事”的人重新想一遍。柬埔寨教育、青年与体育部大臣助理黄子扬接受中新社专访时说，柬埔寨华校中60%以上的学生是柬埔寨当地人，而不是华人子弟。

再读一遍。这些由华人社群创办、出资、多年来靠侨社维系运转的学校，多数学生竟不是华人。这不是边缘性的变化，而是这件事的性质变了。柬埔寨的华文教育起初是文化传承的机制——让一个少数族群把语言、文字和习俗传下去。上世纪五六十年代，全柬华校一度达到230所。战乱与种族灭绝几乎把它们全部抹去；1990年柬华理事总会成立后从废墟上重建，如今统筹管理57所华校、在校生约5万人。

所以问题不再是这些学校能不能保住侨社认同，而是：当周边社会突然开始需要它们提供的服务，会发生什么。六成学生不是华人，“华文教育”就不再只是侨社内部的私族文化品，而开始具有公共属性——谁来出钱、谁来教书、谁有权参与决定它的未来，答案都不一样了。

## 缺口不只是数字问题

黄子扬没有回避另一半现实。他说，柬埔寨中文教师缺口“非常大”；会中文的柬埔寨人就业面很广，同样地，会柬埔寨语的中国人在柬埔寨就业面也很广。

数字能解释这种焦虑。今年国侨办选派143位外派教师，语合中心派出98位汉语志愿者，合计241人；另一头是约1200名本土中文教师，以及柬华理事总会每年逾百万美元（约合720万元人民币）的薪资补贴。该会师资培训中心2018年成立以来累计培养300余名本土教师，第四届近百名学员正在受训。

再把新增需求算进去：4个省份正在试点把中文课程纳入公立中学，学生和家长反应积极。241人的外派队伍撑不起一场全国性扩张，1200名本土教师摊到57所学校，在试点开始前就已经偏紧。

但真正的问题不是算术。派教师是救急，不是建制度。一位从中国派来的教师可能语言功底扎实，却未必适应柬埔寨的课堂——以高棉语为主的教学环境、学生对权威的期待、为另一类学习者编写的教材，都会成为障碍。柬埔寨需要的不是更多外来者，而是一条本土的人才管道：能招聘、培养、认证并留住自己的教师。外部援助可以给这条管道铺第一块砖，却代替不了它本身。北京今年继续增加派出人数，既是进展的证据，也说明本土机制还有多长的路要走。

## 从侨校到公立课程

四省试点其实是比教师缺口更重要的新闻，尽管它吸引的标题更少。中文正作为外语选修课写进柬埔寨国民教育体系。柬埔寨教育、青年与体育部副国务秘书洪金昌描述了背后的机制：签署合作备忘录、成立由部长牵头的协调委员会，并自主培育本土师资、编写适合当地情况的教材，而不是一味引进。

这是侨校凭一己之力迈不出去的制度性一步。一个社团办的学校可以教语言；只有国家才能把它排进课表、给它学分，让它在学生的升学中算数。据大会相关报道，与会者强调，一门语言课靠热情活不长久：政策、课程、合格师资、学习资源与考试评价必须作为一个完整生态协同运转。

但有个问题值得直说：列为选修课，不等于真正融入。选修课往往是学校课时或师资紧张时最先被砍掉的。如果中文不与可信的考试评价、被承认的证书、通往升学或就业的路径挂钩，试点就有沦为“有课表位置的语言社团”之虞。“开设”与“嵌入”之间的差别，就是一阵风与一项制度之间的差别。

## 双语转型是生存之道

制度化的压力并不抽象，它已经在重塑学校本身。柬华理事总会统筹的57所华校中，20所已同时开设高棉文正规课程与中文课程；30余所仍只教中文。3所已经关停，12所在校生不足百人，几乎只相当于语言学习班。

原因很朴素。随着柬埔寨国民学校全面推行全日制教学，侨办的华校白天生源被分流；家庭要的是国家承认的文凭，而不只是学会一门外语。柬华理事总会会长方侨生明确提出转型方向：把华校办成中柬双语学校，柬文正规课程与华文课程并行，让学生拿到国家认可的学历证书，打通升学通道。

与其说这是文化上的退让，不如说是务实。只靠一个不被承认的下午班来承载文化，撑不过下一代。完成转型的学校大概率会长大；死守单一语言学校模式的，只会不断缩水，到最后靠补贴而不是靠学生维持开门。

## 双向语言市场的真实含义

这场专访里最值得琢磨的一句，其实与缺口无关。“会中文的柬埔寨人就业面很广，”黄子扬说，“同样地，会柬埔寨语的中国人在柬埔寨就业面也很广。”

这是对双向劳动力市场的精确描述，也戳破了一种舒服的叙事。柬埔寨中文学习的扩张，主要不是文明魅力，也不是柬埔寨家庭对中国的浪漫情感，而是投资、供应链和薪水——是中资企业建厂修路，是柬埔寨工人想去里面谋职，也是中国公民需要在当地用高棉语办事。语言跟着资本走，而不是反过来。

这对两边都有政策含义。既然需求由就业驱动，供给就应当被当作劳动力基础设施来建设，而不是一个用节庆活动人次来考核的文化项目。这也意味着热潮自带风险：工具性的语言学习对经济周期极为敏感，投资一慢，动力就退。对冲这种波动的办法，恰恰就是试点和双语转型正在做的——把一门语言嵌进学校体系、被承认的证书和一支本土就业的教师队伍里，而不是让它浮在炽热劳动力市场的表面。

柬埔寨能否及时建成这一切，与其说明年北京再派多少人，不如看这1200名本土教师能不能得到让他们留下来的培训、地位与薪酬。学生已经用报名投了票。现在轮到制度追上来。

## 延伸阅读

**本土师资（local teacher capacity）**：东道国招聘、培养、认证并留住本国语言教师的能力，是外部支持型语言项目能否自我维持的决定性变量。

**中柬双语学校（Cambodia–China bilingual school）**：同时开设高棉文正规课程与中文课程的侨校，学生毕业可获国家承认的学历证书并升入高校。

**中文纳入国民教育体系（Chinese in national education systems）**：一门外语从社团自办课堂进入公立课程的过程，伴随正式地位、考试评价与学习通道的确立。

# Cambodia's Chinese Teacher Shortage: A Language Moves from Community Halls into Public Schools

## The sixty percent question

At the 2026 World Chinese Language Conference in Beijing last week, a Cambodian official offered a statistic that should make anyone who still thinks of Chinese-language education in Southeast Asia as a diaspora family affair pause and reconsider. Huang Ziyang, assistant to the Minister of Education, Youth and Sport, told China News Service that more than 60 percent of students in Cambodia's Chinese schools are local Cambodians rather than children of ethnic Chinese families.

Read that again. The majority of pupils in schools built, funded and historically sustained by the Chinese community are not Chinese. This is not a marginal shift. It is a change in the nature of the enterprise. Chinese-language education in Cambodia began as a mechanism of heritage preservation — a way for a minority community to keep a language, a script and a set of customs alive across generations. At its peak in the 1950s and 1960s, the country had some 230 Chinese schools. War and genocide erased almost all of them; the Association of Chinese Cambodians rebuilt from the rubble after 1990 and today runs 57 schools with roughly 50,000 students.

So the question is no longer whether these schools can preserve a diaspora identity. It is what happens when the society around them suddenly demands the service they provide. When six in ten students are non-Chinese, "Chinese education" stops being a private cultural good and starts behaving like a public one — and that changes who should pay for it, who should teach it, and who is entitled to a seat in the room when its future is decided.

## A shortage measured in more than numbers

Huang did not sugar-coat the other half of the picture. "The shortage of Chinese teachers is very large," he said, noting that Cambodians who speak Chinese find work easily — and, symmetrically, Chinese speakers of Khmer are equally sought after in Cambodia.

The numbers explain the anxiety. This year the Overseas Chinese Affairs Office dispatched 143 teachers and the Center for Language Education and Cooperation sent 98 volunteers — 241 reinforcements in total. Against them stand roughly 1,200 locally trained Chinese teachers, supported by annual subsidies of more than US$1 million from the Association of Chinese Cambodians. The association's teacher training centre, founded in 2018, has produced a little over 300 graduates; a fourth cohort of nearly 100 is currently in training.

Now add the new demand. Four provinces are piloting the inclusion of Chinese in public middle-school curricula, with students and parents reportedly enthusiastic. A 241-person external contingent cannot staff a national expansion, and 1,200 local teachers spread across 57 schools is thin even before the pilot begins.

But the real problem is not arithmetic. Dispatching teachers is a stopgap; it is not a system. A teacher sent from China may know the language perfectly and still struggle in a Cambodian classroom — with Khmer-dominant instruction, local expectations of authority, and textbooks written for a different kind of learner. What Cambodia needs is not more visitors but a domestic pipeline that can recruit, train, certify and retain its own teachers. External aid can lay the first bricks of that pipeline. It cannot replace it. That Beijing keeps increasing the number of dispatched teachers is evidence of progress, but also of how far the local mechanism still has to run.

## From community schools to the public curriculum

The pilot in four provinces is the more consequential piece of news, even if it drew fewer headlines than the teacher shortage. Chinese is being written into the national education system as an elective foreign language at secondary level. Hong Jinchang, a deputy secretary of state at the ministry, described the machinery behind it: memoranda of understanding, a coordination committee led by a minister, and an effort to cultivate local teachers and develop teaching materials suited to local conditions rather than simply importing them.

This is the institutional step community schools could never take on their own. A school run by an association can teach a language; only a state can put it on a timetable, attach credits to it, and make it count toward a student's progression. According to reporting from the conference, participants stressed that a language course cannot survive on enthusiasm alone: policy, curriculum, qualified teachers, learning resources and examination pathways have to work as one ecology.

There is a catch, and it is worth stating plainly. Being listed as an elective is not the same as being integrated. Electives are the first thing cut when a school runs short of hours or teachers. Unless Chinese is tied to credible assessment, recognised credentials and a route into further study or employment, the pilot risks becoming a language club with a timetable slot. The difference between "offered" and "embedded" is the difference between a fashion and an institution.

## The bilingual pivot as survival

The pressure to institutionalise is not abstract. It is already reshaping the schools themselves. Of the 57 Chinese schools under the association, 20 now offer the regular Khmer-language curriculum alongside Chinese; more than 30 still teach Chinese only. Three have closed, and 12 have fewer than 100 students — barely more than language classes.

The reason is prosaic. As Cambodia extended full-day schooling in public schools, community Chinese schools lost their daytime intake. Families want certificates the state recognises, not just literacy in a foreign tongue. Fang Qiaosheng, president of the association, has called for a deliberate pivot: turn Chinese schools into Cambodia–China bilingual institutions offering both curricula, so that graduates leave with a nationally recognised diploma and a real path to further study.

Call this pragmatic rather than sentimental. It is not a retreat from Chinese culture; it is an argument that culture carried only by an unaccredited afternoon class will not survive the next generation. The schools that make the transition will likely grow. Those that cling to a pure language-school model will keep shrinking until the subsidy, rather than the students, is what keeps the doors open.

## What a two-way language market really means

The most revealing sentence in the interview was not about shortages at all. "Cambodians who know Chinese have a wide range of employment options," Huang said, "and likewise Chinese people who know Khmer find wide employment in Cambodia."

That is a precise description of a two-way labour market, and it punctures a comfortable narrative. The expansion of Chinese-language learning in Cambodia is not primarily the result of civilisational charm or a romantic attachment to China among Cambodian families. It is driven by investment, supply chains and paycheques — by Chinese firms building factories and infrastructure, by Cambodian workers seeking positions in them, and by Chinese nationals who need Khmer to operate on the ground. Language follows capital, not the other way around.

This matters for policy on both sides. If demand is employment-led, then supply must be treated as workforce infrastructure, not as a cultural programme measured in festival attendance. It also means the boom carries a built-in risk. Instrumental language learning is notoriously sensitive to the economic cycle: when investment slows, motivation falls. The hedge against that volatility is exactly what the pilot and the bilingual pivot are trying to build — a language embedded in the school system, in recognised credentials and in a locally employed teaching profession, rather than floating on the surface of a hot labour market.

Whether Cambodia can build that in time depends less on how many volunteers Beijing sends next year than on whether the 1,200 local teachers get the training, status and pay that let them stay. The students have already voted with their enrolment. The system now has to catch up.

## Further reading

**Local teacher capacity**: The ability of a host country to recruit, train, certify and retain its own language teachers; the decisive variable in whether an externally supported language programme becomes self-sustaining.

**Cambodia–China bilingual school**: A community school offering both the national Khmer curriculum and Chinese-language instruction, enabling students to graduate with state-recognised certificates and a route into higher education.

**Chinese in national education systems**: The process by which a foreign language moves from community-run classes into a country's public curriculum, acquiring formal status, assessment and learning pathways.
