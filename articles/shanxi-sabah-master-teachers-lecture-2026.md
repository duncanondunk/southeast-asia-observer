# 亚庇教室里的10年桥：名师巡讲走进沙巴，照见马来西亚华文的「完整体系」

## 亚庇的一间教室——10年静水流深的桥

11月7日，2026年名师巡讲活动在马来西亚沙巴州亚庇的建国中学启幕。场面不算大：马来西亚农业及粮食安全部副部长陈泓缣、中国驻哥打基纳巴卢副总领事薛小宁、沙巴暨纳闽中华总商会总会长雷远生、山西大学巡讲团团长任国英，加上当地华文教师，约200人到场。可就是这间教室，折射出一条延续10年的民间文教通道。

据主办方介绍，山西与沙巴的华教交往已历时10年，2023年双方还曾在沙巴举办「中华文化大乐园」活动。本次巡讲并非一次性访问，而是同一段关系的延续。对一个远在婆罗洲北岸、与中国隔着南海的州而言，这种细水长流的学术往来，比任何高调宣言都更能说明民间对华文教育的真实需求。

## 三个方向：当代海外华文教育真正在焦虑什么

这次巡讲聚焦三个方向：课堂教学管理、人工智能赋能华文教学、青少年心理健康。这三点很有意思——它暴露出海外华教今天真正头疼的问题，早已不是「有没有老师教中文」，而是「怎么教得更好、更现代、更贴近学生」。

把人工智能写进华文教学大纲，意味着海外华校开始认真拥抱技术变革；把青少年心理健康单列为主题，则说明华教共同体意识到，传承语言文化的同时也要回应二代、三代移民子弟的真实困境。这不再是上世纪那种刻板的「背课文、写毛笔字」式传承，而是一种主动现代化的努力。

## 马来西亚「完整」的华文教育体系——离散世界的孤本

马来西亚副部长的致辞点出了一个关键事实：马来西亚拥有完整的华文教育体系。这句话分量很重。放眼全球离散华人社会，能在居住国公办教育之外，自成一套从小学、中学到独立中学（独中）的完整华文轨，且获得族群内部稳定投入、政府也予承认的，几乎只有马来西亚。

这不是偶然。马来西亚华社用近百年时间，靠社团、商会、校友会自筹经费，硬生生把华文教育守了下来。今天沙巴的建国中学便是这套体系的末梢之一。也正因体系完整，外界的「名师巡讲」才不是来「启蒙」，而是来「切磋」——双方是平等的专业交流，而非单向输出。

## 软实力，还是务实能力建设？解读北京的考量

中国驻哥打基纳巴卢副总领事把「名师巡讲」概括为中方扶持海外华教的品牌项目，称其已累计吸引近1万名教师参与。毋庸讳言，这带有软实力与侨务工作的底色。北京长期把华文教育视为维系与离散族群情感纽带、拓展文化影响力的抓手，这一点毋庸回避。

但把它简单读作「文化渗透」也失之偏颇。对沙巴当地华校而言，优质师资、教学方法和心理健康资源本就是稀缺品；山西大学送来的，是实打实的专业支持。是软实力也好，是务实合作也罢，最终要看它是否解决了华教一线的真问题——从现场200余名教师的到场热情看，需求是真实的。

## 沙巴的特别之处——东马与华人少数群体

最后一个值得点出的背景，是地点选在沙巴。作为东马一州，沙巴在族群结构、对华态度上与西马半岛存在差异；这里华人虽是少数，却组织紧密、商会与华校网络发达。选择沙巴，既是对东马华社的针对性支持，也暗合中国近年来「面向东盟」的周边外交节奏——华文教育与「一带一路」的人文通道，本就同气连枝。

一位联邦副部长与一位中国总领事同时现身东马一所学校的活动，本身也是信号：吉隆坡与北京都把华文教育视作国家间文化外交，而非边缘的族群事务。巡讲团此行还将前往吉打州与槟城，把这条文教走廊从婆罗洲延伸到马来半岛西岸。一间教室里的10年，或许正是离散华人文化根脉得以延续的注脚。

## 延伸阅读
**马来西亚华文独中（Chinese Independent High Schools）**：马来西亚华社自办、以华语为主要教学媒介的中学，全国约60余所，是当地华文教育体系的中坚，学费多靠华人社会捐助。
**中华文化大乐园（Chinese Culture Grand Paradise）**：中国侨务部门面向海外华裔青少年举办的文化体验品牌活动，内容涵盖书画、歌舞、武术等，自2009年起在各国巡回。
**华文教育（Chinese-language Education / Huawen Education）**：指居住国面向华侨华人子弟的母语与中华文化教育，在东南亚以马来西亚、新加坡、印尼等地最为兴盛，兼具语言传承与身份认同功能。

# A Decade-Bridging Classroom in Kota Kinabalu: China's Master Teacher Tour in Sabah and Malaysia's "Complete" Chinese Education System

## A classroom in Kota Kinabalu — and a decade of quiet bridge-building

On 7 November 2026, the 2026 Master Teacher Lecture Tour opened at Jianguo Secondary School in Kota Kinabalu, the capital of Malaysia's Sabah state. The gathering was modest in scale: Chen Hongjian, Malaysia's Deputy Minister of Agriculture and Food Security; Xue Xiaoning, China's Consul-General in Kota Kinabalu; Lei Yuansheng, president of the Sabah and Labuan Chinese Chamber of Commerce; Ren Guoying, head of the Shanxi University delegation; and around 200 local Chinese-language teachers. Yet this single classroom reflects a people-to-people educational corridor that has quietly run for a decade.

According to the organisers, educational exchanges between Shanxi and Sabah have now spanned ten years, and in 2023 the two sides jointly held the "Chinese Culture Grand Paradise" programme in Sabah. This lecture tour is not a one-off visit but a continuation of that relationship. For a state perched on the northern shore of Borneo, separated from China by the South China Sea, this steady, low-key academic exchange speaks more loudly than any grand declaration about the real demand for Chinese-language education at the grassroots. The presence of a federal deputy minister and a Chinese consul-general at a school event in East Malaysia is itself a signal: both Kuala Lumpur and Beijing treat Chinese-language education not as a marginal ethnic affair but as a matter of state-to-state cultural diplomacy.

## The three themes: what modern overseas Chinese education now worries about

The tour focused on three themes: classroom teaching management, AI-empowered Chinese-language instruction, and adolescent mental health. The choice is revealing — it shows that the pressing problems of overseas Chinese education today are no longer "whether there are teachers to teach Chinese" but "how to teach better, more modernly, and closer to students' needs."

Writing AI into the Chinese-teaching syllabus means overseas Chinese schools are beginning to take technological change seriously; singling out adolescent mental health signals that the community realises language and cultural transmission must now also address the genuine struggles of second- and third-generation immigrant children. This is no longer the rigid "recite texts, practise brush calligraphy" model of the last century, but a conscious effort at modernisation.

## Malaysia's "complete" system — a rare case in the diaspora

The Malaysian deputy minister's remarks highlighted a crucial fact: Malaysia possesses a complete Chinese education system. That statement carries weight. Across the global overseas Chinese world, it is almost unique for a diaspora community, outside the host country's public education, to maintain a full Chinese-language track from primary school through independent secondary school ("independent Chinese high school"), sustained by stable ethnic-Chinese funding and recognised by the government.

This did not happen by chance. Over nearly a century, Malaysian Chinese society — through associations, chambers of commerce and alumni bodies — self-financed and stubbornly preserved Chinese education. Jianguo Secondary School in Sabah today is one node in that system. Precisely because the system is complete, outside "master teacher tours" are not there to "enlighten" but to "exchange" — a relationship of professional equality rather than one-way delivery. For comparison, in Indonesia and Thailand Chinese-language instruction was suppressed for decades and has only recently been revived; in Singapore it survives within a bilingual state framework. Malaysia's ability to keep an autonomous, community-funded Chinese track is the exception that proves the rule.

## Soft power, or practical capacity? Reading Beijing's calculus

China's Consul-General in Kota Kinabalu described the Master Teacher Tour as a flagship project through which Beijing supports overseas Chinese education, saying it has attracted nearly 10,000 teachers in cumulative participation. It would be naive to deny that this carries the colouring of soft power and diaspora-affairs work. Beijing has long treated Chinese-language education as a tool to maintain emotional ties with diaspora communities and to extend cultural influence — a point that need not be avoided.

Yet reducing it to "cultural infiltration" is equally off the mark. For local Chinese schools in Sabah, quality teaching staff, pedagogical methods and mental-health resources are genuinely scarce; what Shanxi University delivers is real professional support. Whether one calls it soft power or pragmatic cooperation, the ultimate test is whether it solves frontline problems in Chinese education — and judging by the turnout of more than 200 teachers, the demand is real. Critics in the West often frame such programmes through the lens of great-power competition, but on the ground in Sabah the calculus of a headmaster or a parent is far more pedestrian: better teachers, better outcomes, a stronger sense of belonging for their children.

## Sabah's particularity — East Malaysia and a Chinese minority

A final point worth noting is the choice of Sabah. As an East Malaysian state, Sabah differs from peninsular Malaysia in ethnic composition and attitudes toward China; here the Chinese are a minority, yet tightly organised, with densely networked chambers of commerce and Chinese schools. Selecting Sabah is both targeted support for East Malaysian Chinese communities and a quiet alignment with China's recent "face ASEAN" peripheral-diplomacy rhythm — Chinese-language education and the Belt and Road's people-to-people corridors are, after all, branches of the same tree.

The delegation will also travel to Kedah and Penang, extending this cultural-educational corridor from Borneo to the western shore of the Malay Peninsula. A decade inside one classroom may well be the footnote to how the cultural roots of diaspora Chinese endure.

## Further reading
**Chinese Independent High Schools**: Self-run Malaysian Chinese secondary schools using Chinese as the main medium of instruction, numbering over 60 nationwide and forming the backbone of the local Chinese education system, largely funded by Chinese community donations.
**Chinese Culture Grand Paradise**: A cultural-experience brand activity organised by China's diaspora-affairs bodies for overseas Chinese youth, covering calligraphy, painting, song, dance and martial arts, toured across many countries since 2009.
**Chinese-language Education (Huawen Education)**: Mother-tongue and Chinese-culture education for overseas Chinese children in their countries of residence, most vibrant in Southeast Asia — Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia — serving both language transmission and identity formation.
