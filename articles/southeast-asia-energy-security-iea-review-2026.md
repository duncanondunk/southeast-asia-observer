# 电力饥渴遇上进口依赖：国际能源署为何给东南亚能源安全敲响警钟

10月5日，国际能源署（IEA）发布《东盟能源安全评估》（ASEAN Energy Security Review），两天后，这份报告被摆上在马尼拉举行的东盟能源部长会议的桌面。措辞克制，分量不轻。据南华早报报道，国际能源署警告：电力需求的快速攀升，正把东南亚的能源安全推向风险边缘。这个全球最具活力的地区之一，驱动增长所需的能源，正越来越依赖一条自己无法掌控的供应链。

## 一份在马尼拉递交的警告

这份评估覆盖石油、天然气、煤炭、电力与关键矿产五大板块，由国际能源署与东盟能源中心（ASEAN Centre for Energy）、东盟秘书处及成员国密切协调完成。国际能源署署长法提赫·比罗尔（Fatih Birol）把话说得直白：能源安全“必须被嵌入整个系统的每一个决策”，从石油储备、天然气基础设施到电网和能效，一个都不能少。

时机耐人寻味。今年2月28日美国与以色列对伊朗动武后，伊朗方面实际封锁了霍尔木兹海峡，中东能源通道剧烈震荡——这场现实中的压力测试，恰好为报告做了注脚。一份赶在部长会议前递交的安全评估，与其说是学术作业，不如说是一记迟到的警钟。

## 数字：年均5.4%的电力增速

报告最扎眼的数字是需求曲线：2026至2030年，东南亚电力需求预计以年均5.4%的速度增长。这不是新趋势——自2015年以来，该地区电力需求已保持年均约6%的增速，电力在终端能源消费中的占比达到23%。过去十年，东南亚人均能源需求增长了近30%。

驱动因素并不神秘：空调降温、工业扩张与数据中心，三台“需求发动机”并行运转。增量的大头在印度尼西亚——约占全部新增需求的40%，越南以26%紧随其后。可再生能源的扩张同样迅猛：2025年地区太阳能装机已接近38吉瓦，是十年前的15倍以上；到2030年，风电与光伏发电量预计分别以约26%和23%的年均速度增长，合计占比从约5%升至11%。

需要说清楚的是，需求增长是发展的勋章，不是罪状。真正的考题只有一个：这套系统跟不跟得上。

## 燃料困局：越增长，越依赖

答案藏在燃料账本里。报告指出，东南亚原油产量已较上世纪90年代的峰值下滑40%，原油进口自2015年以来增加了30%；液化天然气（LNG）进口依赖持续加深，多国直接暴露在全球天然气市场的波动之下。

2月28日之后的霍尔木兹海峡危机，把这种脆弱性演成了实景。危机前，东南亚约60%的原油进口和三分之一的天然气进口来自中东；地区炼厂加工的成品油中，约45%的原料是中东原油——即便不从海湾直接进口成品油，东南亚照样会被中东的风波波及。东盟能源中心测算，一旦中东供应严重中断，受威胁的石油规模相当于东盟最终石油消费量的近28%。

应急缓冲同样参差不齐：印度尼西亚有战略石油储备框架却未落地，菲律宾仍在规划，新加坡则没有战略储备，仅要求发电企业留存柴油备用。说白了，这个地区烧着别处的油、别处的气，手里却没攒下多少压舱石。

## 煤电退不出的现实

报告没有回避一个尴尬的事实：煤炭仍占东盟成员国电力供应的近一半，在印尼、菲律宾和越南占比尤其高；文莱、新加坡和泰国则以天然气为主。风电光伏增速虽猛，但煤电与气电的发电量在绝对值上仍将增长，煤电占比只是缓慢下滑。

这正是东南亚能源转型的两难：一边是气候承诺与融资压力，一边是工业化进程刚性的用电需求。国际能源署的措辞相当克制——“可靠的煤炭供应”与“可信的退出多元化路径”必须并举。翻译成大白话：短期内谁也别指望煤电退场，真正的问题是，退坡的坡道能修得多快、多稳。

## 电网：被低估的短板

比发电侧更被低估的，是电网。报告直言，多国输配电建设未能跟上需求与可再生能源装机的步伐，拥堵与弃电（curtailment）在增加；老化的辐射状网络又放大了局部故障的风险。约五分之一的东南亚电网还暴露在热带气旋风险之下。

投资缺口摆在那里：报告测算，区域电网与储能的年度投资需从目前的约130亿美元（约936亿元人民币）提升至2035年的约300亿美元（约2160亿元人民币）。越南已率先撞上更高阶段的系统整合难题——供需波动加大、可再生能源弃电增多。公众争论总是围着“建不建电厂”打转，但真正卡脖子的，是电流能否顺畅跑完最后一公里。

## 区域协作：说易行难

报告开出的药方并不新鲜：分散供应来源与运输路线、在经济可行的前提下多用本地资源、提升能效与电气化水平、明确应急程序、强化市场监测、定期演练。落点则压在两个既有机制上——《2026至2030年东盟能源合作行动计划》（ASEAN Plan of Action for Energy Cooperation）与《东盟石油安全框架协定》（APSA）。

难在执行。报告自己也承认，各国国情差异巨大：有的国家煤炭依赖深重，有的连战略储备都尚未建立。新加坡尤索夫伊萨克研究院（ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute）研究员乔安妮·林（Joanne Lin）向媒体指出，战略储备建设维护成本高昂，各国储油能力与财政资源悬殊，指望每个国家都自建大规模储备并不现实。东盟电网倡议喊了几十年，宣言与千瓦之间的距离，从来没有短过。

## 中国的位置与启示

对中国读者而言，这份报告有双重含义。其一，东南亚的能源转型与中国的产业出口深度绑定：地区新增的光伏、电池与储能装机，相当部分来自中国供应链；中国资本与工程力量也深度参与地区电力建设。能源安全的警钟敲得越响，中国绿色产能的需求逻辑反而越清晰——前提是中方产品能在价格之外，把可靠性与本地化做成真正的卖点。

其二，这是一面镜子。东南亚今天面对的“进口依赖叠加通道风险”组合，与中国长期面对的霍尔木兹海峡—马六甲困局结构相似。北京力推新能源、特高压输电与储备体系的逻辑，某种意义上正是这份报告在国家尺度的注脚。能源安全从来不只是买家与卖家的生意，而是一个地区如何在不确定的世界里给自己留后路的制度设计。

## 延伸阅读

- **电力系统灵活性（Power system flexibility）**：电网应对供需波动的能力，来源包括可调度电源、储能、需求响应与区域互联，是消化高比例风电光伏的关键。
- **东盟能源合作行动计划（ASEAN Plan of Action for Energy Cooperation）**：东盟在能源领域的区域性合作框架，涵盖电网互联、石油安全与能效等议程，是国际能源署本轮建议的落地载体。
- **战略石油储备（Strategic petroleum reserve）**：国家层面储备原油或成品油以应对供应中断的制度安排，东盟内部各国建设进度差异悬殊。

# Power hunger, imported fuel: the energy security trap Southeast Asia cannot ignore

On October 5, the International Energy Agency (IEA) published its ASEAN Energy Security Review. Two days later, the report was on the table at the ASEAN Energy Ministers' meeting in Manila. The language is measured; the substance is not. As the South China Morning Post reported, the IEA warns that rising electricity needs are putting Southeast Asia's energy security at risk. One of the world's most dynamic regions increasingly depends, for the energy that powers its growth, on supply chains it does not control.

## A warning delivered in Manila

The review covers oil, gas, coal, electricity and critical minerals, and was produced in close coordination with the ASEAN Centre for Energy, the ASEAN Secretariat and member states. IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol put it bluntly: energy security "must be built into decisions across the whole system", from oil stocks and gas infrastructure to electricity grids and energy efficiency.

The timing is telling. Since February 28, when the United States and Israel struck Iran and Tehran effectively blocked the Strait of Hormuz, the Middle East's energy arteries have been violently disrupted — a real-world stress test that footnotes the report's every page. A security review delivered ahead of a ministers' meeting is not an academic exercise; it is a belated alarm bell.

## The numbers: 5.4% a year

The report's most striking figure is the demand curve: between 2026 and 2030, Southeast Asia's electricity demand is projected to grow by about 5.4% a year. This is not a new trend — demand has grown roughly 6% annually since 2015, and electricity already accounts for 23% of the region's final energy consumption. Per-capita energy demand has risen nearly 30% over the past decade.

The drivers are no mystery: cooling, industrial expansion and data centres are three demand engines running in parallel. Indonesia alone is expected to absorb about 40% of the additional demand, with Vietnam contributing 26%. Renewables are scaling fast — solar capacity reached nearly 38 GW in 2025, more than fifteen times its level a decade earlier, and wind and solar generation are projected to grow by about 26% and 23% a year to 2030, lifting their combined share from roughly 5% to 11%.

To be clear, demand growth is a badge of development, not a sin. The exam question is singular: can the system keep up?

## The fuel trap: the more it grows, the more it imports

The answer lies in the fuel ledger. The review notes that Southeast Asia's crude output has fallen 40% from its 1990s peak while crude imports have risen 30% since 2015; reliance on imported liquefied natural gas (LNG) keeps deepening, leaving several countries directly exposed to swings in global gas markets.

The Hormuz crisis that followed the February 28 strikes turned that vulnerability into a live demonstration. Before it, about 60% of the region's crude imports and one-third of its natural gas imports came from the Middle East; around 45% of the refined products consumed in the region are derived from Middle Eastern crude — so even economies that import no finished fuel from the Gulf are hit by Middle Eastern shocks. The ASEAN Centre for Energy estimates that a severe Middle East disruption could put oil volumes equivalent to nearly 28% of ASEAN's final oil consumption at risk.

The buffers are just as uneven. Indonesia has a strategic reserve framework it has not implemented; the Philippines is still planning one; Singapore keeps no strategic petroleum reserve, relying instead on generator-held diesel stocks. In plain terms, the region burns other people's oil and gas while holding little ballast of its own.

## Coal will not quietly exit

The report does not dodge an awkward fact: coal still supplies nearly half of ASEAN's electricity, with particularly high shares in Indonesia, the Philippines and Vietnam, while gas dominates in Brunei, Singapore and Thailand. Wind and solar are expanding quickly, yet coal- and gas-fired generation will still grow in absolute terms; coal's share merely declines slightly.

This is Southeast Asia's transition dilemma in miniature: climate commitments and financing pressure on one side, the non-negotiable electricity needs of industrialisation on the other. The IEA's wording is deliberately careful — reliable coal supply and "credible pathways for diversification away from it" must proceed together. Translated into plain language: no one should expect coal to exit soon; the real question is how fast and how stable the off-ramp can be built.

## The grid: the underestimated weak link

More underestimated than generation is the grid. Transmission and distribution, the review says, have not kept pace with demand and renewable deployment in several countries; congestion and curtailment are rising, and ageing radial networks amplify the risk of local failures. Around one-fifth of Southeast Asia's grids face tropical-cyclone risk.

The investment gap is explicit: annual spending on grids and storage, the report calculates, must rise from around US$13 billion today to around US$30 billion by 2035. Vietnam is already hitting higher-phase integration problems — wider supply-demand swings and renewable curtailment. Public debate fixates on whether to build power plants; what actually throttles the system is whether electrons can complete the last mile.

## Regional cooperation: easier said than done

The prescriptions are familiar: diversify supply sources and routes, use domestic resources where economically viable, expand efficiency and electrification, clarify emergency procedures, strengthen market monitoring and rehearse responses. The operational anchors are two existing frameworks — the ASEAN Plan of Action for Energy Cooperation 2026–2030 and the ASEAN Framework Agreement on Petroleum Security (APSA).

Enforcement is the hard part, as the report itself concedes: national circumstances differ enormously. Joanne Lin of Singapore's ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute told local media that stockpiles are costly to build and maintain, and that countries' storage capacity and fiscal resources vary so widely that expecting every state to hold large reserves alone is unrealistic. The ASEAN Power Grid has been discussed for decades; the distance between communiqués and kilowatts has never been short.

## Where China fits in

For Chinese readers, the report carries a double meaning. First, Southeast Asia's energy transition is deeply wired into China's industrial exports: much of the region's new solar, battery and storage capacity comes from Chinese supply chains, and Chinese capital and engineering are embedded in the region's power build-out. The louder the energy-security alarm rings, the clearer the demand-side logic for Chinese green industries — provided that reliability and localisation, not just price, become genuine selling points.

Second, it is a mirror. The combination Southeast Asia now faces — import dependence compounded by chokepoint risk — is structurally similar to the Hormuz–Malacca dilemma that has long shaped Chinese policy. Beijing's push into renewables, ultra-high-voltage transmission and strategic reserves is, in a sense, the national-scale footnote to this report. Energy security is never just a buyer-seller transaction; it is the institutional design by which a region insures itself against an uncertain world.

## Further reading

- **Power system flexibility**: a grid's ability to absorb swings in supply and demand through dispatchable plants, storage, demand response and interconnection — the key to integrating high shares of wind and solar.
- **ASEAN Plan of Action for Energy Cooperation**: ASEAN's regional energy framework covering grid interconnection, petroleum security and efficiency; the vehicle meant to carry the IEA's recommendations.
- **Strategic petroleum reserve**: state-held stocks of crude or refined products to cushion supply disruptions; build-out across ASEAN remains highly uneven.
