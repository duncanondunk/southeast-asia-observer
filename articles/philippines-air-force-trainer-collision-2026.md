# 菲律宾（Philippines）空军两架教练机空中相撞 四名飞行员殉职

10月23日下午3时51分，两架菲律宾空军SF-260型初级教练机在吕宋岛八打雁省利帕市的费尔南多空军基地上空进行训练飞行时，于半空中迎头相撞，双双坠毁在基地范围之内。机上四名飞行员全部遇难。菲律宾空军次日在通报中证实了这起事故，发言人卡平吉安上校表示，两架飞机均落在基地范围内，暂未波及其他人员伤亡；作为预防措施，空军已暂时停飞全部SF-260机队，事故原因仍在调查中。

一场再普通不过的训练飞行，转眼变成四人殒命的悲剧。问题是：为什么偏偏是菲律宾空军，这类事故会一而再、再而三地发生？

## 一台老飞机，撑起一国空军的起点

SF-260是意大利在1960年代设计的轻型螺旋桨教练机，主要用途是为新飞行员打基础。它皮实、便宜、好养，因此被许多中小国家空军当作入门机大量装备。菲律宾空军的SF-260机队正是如此——它承担的是最基础、也最频繁的训练科目。

可问题恰恰出在这里。当一个国家的空军把最日常的训练，压在一批设计于半个多世纪前的老机身上，安全的余量天然就薄。2026年5月，另一架SF-260在吕宋岛北部的本格特省训练时坠毁，两名飞行员丧生，当时空军就曾停飞其余16架同型机；仅仅五个月后，同样的机型再度以最惨烈的方式折翼。把这两起事故并排看，已经不是「偶发」两个字能轻轻带过的了。

## 致命坠机，已成一种模式

近年来菲律宾军用飞机致命事故频发，这绝非孤例。从运输机到教练机，从训练空域到转场航线，坠机阴影反复笼罩菲律宾空军。每一起事故的官方口径都高度一致：成立调查组、暂停相关机型、慰问家属、承诺整改。可整改之后，下一次坠机往往又来。

这背后折射的，是一支长期处于「低水平均衡」的空军：机队老化、维护能力有限、训练强度与安全投入之间难以兼顾。事故不断，说明体系层面的短板并没有因为一次次的道歉和停飞令而真正补齐。换言之，停飞容易，重建一支可信赖的空中力量，难得多。

## 南海（South China Sea）升温，菲律宾却先在自己天空里「掉链子」

更值得玩味的是时机。近年来，菲律宾在南海问题上姿态高调，与美国及地区伙伴的军事互动明显增多，外界对其空中侦巡与存在感的需求水涨船高。菲律宾也确实在花钱更新装备：从韩国引进FA-50轻型战斗机，从巴西采购超级图卡诺螺旋桨攻击机，并陆续接收更多现代化平台。表面看，这是一支正在「强军」的空军。

可现实给了当头一棒。就在外部安全压力上升、对合格空勤人员的需求空前迫切之际，空军却连最基础的训练飞行都保障不稳。四名飞行员的生命，折损在和平时期的例行科目里，而非任何一次对峙之中。这不能不让人发问：一支连自身训练都难以确保安全的空军，拿什么去撑起它所被赋予的宏大战略期待？

## 一架停飞，牵动的是整条训练链路

菲律宾2026年的国防预算约为3360亿比索（约600亿元人民币），较十年前大幅增长，现代化雄心不可谓不大。但预算数字不等于战斗力。SF-260机队全员停飞，意味着新飞行员的入门训练被迫中断、整个飞行员的养成节奏被打乱。对一个本就飞行员短缺、训练体系脆弱的空军来说，这种内伤比一次坠机更难在短时间内愈合。

说到底，这起相撞事故，表面是训练意外，根子却是一道结构性难题：菲律宾空军的硬件更新、维护体系与训练安全文化，远远跑不过它正在背负的地缘政治野心。没有扎实的底子，再响亮的口号也托不起真正的空中力量。四名年轻飞行员的牺牲，应当成为倒逼体系革新的警钟，而不只是又一条「暂停机型、成立调查」的新闻通稿。

**飞行安全（Aviation Safety）**：军事飞行训练中的人员、装备与流程保障体系；在高强度训练与老旧机队并存时，安全余量往往最易被挤压。

# Four dead as two Philippine Air Force trainers collide mid-air

At 3:51 p.m. on 23 October, two Philippine Air Force SF-260 primary trainers collided in mid-air while conducting a training sortie over Fernando Air Base in Lipa City, Batangas province, on the island of Luzon. Both aircraft crashed within the perimeter of the base. All four pilots on board were killed. The Air Force confirmed the accident the next day; Colonel Alvin Capinguian, its spokesman, said both planes went down inside the base and there were no other casualties reported. As a precaution, the entire SF-260 fleet has been temporarily grounded while the cause is investigated.

A perfectly routine training flight turned, in an instant, into a tragedy that claimed four lives. The uncomfortable question is this: why does this keep happening to the Philippine Air Force?

## A vintage airframe at the root of a nation's flying

The SF-260 is a light, single-engine propeller trainer designed in Italy in the 1960s, built chiefly to give new pilots their first hours at the controls. It is rugged, cheap and easy to maintain, which is precisely why smaller air forces adopted it in large numbers as their introductory airframe. The Philippine Air Force's SF-260 fleet is exactly that — it carries the most basic and most frequent training syllabus.

And that is precisely where the trouble lies. When an air force loads its most everyday training onto airframes designed more than half a century ago, the margin for safety is thin by construction. In May 2026, another SF-260 crashed during a training flight in Benguet province in northern Luzon, killing two pilots; the Air Force then grounded its remaining 16 aircraft of the type. Barely five months later, the same airframe has again met the most violent of ends. Put the two accidents side by side, and "one-off" no longer suffices as an explanation.

## Fatal crashes have become a pattern

Deadly accidents across the Philippine military aviation fleet have been distressingly frequent in recent years — and this is no isolated case. From transport aircraft to trainers, from training airspace to transit routes, the shadow of crashes has repeatedly fallen over the Air Force. The official script after each incident is near-identical: convene a board of inquiry, suspend the type, console the families, promise reform. Yet after the reforms, the next crash tends to come anyway.

What this reveals is an air force stuck in a low-level equilibrium: an aging fleet, limited sustainment capacity, and a chronic inability to reconcile training tempo with safety investment. The recurrence shows that the systemic shortfalls were never truly closed by the apologies and stand-down orders. Grounding a fleet is easy; rebuilding a trustworthy air arm is far harder.

## As the South China Sea heats up, Manila stumbles in its own skies

The timing is what makes this worth dwelling on. In recent years the Philippines has struck a high-profile posture in the South China Sea, deepened military interaction with the United States and regional partners, and faced rising external demands for airborne patrol and presence. Manila has indeed been spending to refresh its kit: FA-50 light fighters from South Korea, A-29 Super Tucano turboprop attack aircraft from Brazil, and a steady stream of more modern platforms. On the surface, here is an air force "modernizing" in earnest.

Reality delivered a jolt. At the very moment external security pressure is climbing and the need for qualified aircrew has never been greater, the Air Force cannot even keep its most basic training flights safe. Four pilots lost their lives in a peacetime routine sortie — not in any standoff. One cannot help but ask: an air force that cannot secure its own training, what exactly is it meant to deliver on the grand strategic expectations now placed upon it?

## One grounding, an entire training pipeline disrupted

The Philippines' 2026 defense budget stands at around 336 billion pesos (about US$5.7 billion), sharply up from a decade earlier; the modernization ambition is real. But a budget line is not combat power. Grounding the entire SF-260 fleet means the introductory training of new pilots is interrupted and the whole pipeline of pilot development is thrown off schedule. For an air force already short of pilots and fragile in its training system, this kind of self-inflicted wound heals far more slowly than a single crash.

In the end, this mid-air collision looks like a training mishap on the surface, but its roots are structural: the Air Force's hardware renewal, sustainment system and safety culture lag far behind the geopolitical ambitions now weighing on it. Without solid foundations, no slogan, however loud, can lift a real air force. The sacrifice of four young pilots should be a reckoning that forces systemic reform — not just another press release that reads "suspend the type, convene a board of inquiry."

## Further reading

**SF-260 (SIAI-Marchetti SF.260)**: An Italian light propeller trainer designed in the 1960s, used by numerous air forces as a primary flight-training platform; prized for ruggedness, low cost and ease of maintenance, but of considerable vintage.

**Defense Modernization**: The Philippines' armed-forces modernization programme, launched in 2013, intended to replace aging equipment and strengthen maritime and air patrol and deterrence; progress has been uneven, constrained by budget, sustainment and institutional capacity.

**Aviation Safety**: The personnel, equipment and procedural system that underwrites military flight training; where intense training coexists with an aging fleet, the safety margin is often the first thing squeezed.
