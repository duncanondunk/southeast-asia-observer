# 吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）颁出的奖：刘震云、花踪文学奖与中文书写的静默"去中心"

2026年10月24日晚，在马来西亚（Malaysia）雪兰莪州，中国小说家刘震云于第18届《星洲日报》花踪文学奖颁奖典礼上，接过了第13届古润金花踪世界华文文学奖。对走马观花的旁观者而言，这不过是一则"大陆作家又去海外领了一个奖"的文坛花絮。可如果只停留在这一层，便错过了此事真正抛出的问题：为何华文世界最具分量的文学荣誉之一，不在北京（Beijing）、也不在台北，而安放在吉隆坡？这又说明了中文文化今天究竟安身于何处？

## 一位大陆作家，一座马来亚奖项

刘震云绝非边缘人物。作为当代"乡土"书写的代表性声音，他凭《一句顶一万句》拿下茅盾文学奖，作品也被冯小刚搬上银幕。他的小说在海外流转——被翻译、被评论、被讲授。因此，当他在雪兰莪说出那句话——在诸多海外奖项中，花踪世界华文文学奖之所以"特别"，是因为评审直接阅读华文原著，而非借由译本评判文学成就——这并非客套。他点出的是自己所处文坛一个结构性的事实。

这个事实简单，却让"以大陆为中心"的想象有点坐不住：为华文写作世界中最稀缺的全球性荣誉背书的机构，并不坐落于任何一个中国首都。它由马来西亚的华侨华人社群运作，依托一份自1929年便持续出版的华文报纸——《星洲日报》。该奖两年一届，在整个华文写作世界里，每两年只遴选出一位得主。"两年一人"的稀缺本身，就是一种地位的宣示。

## 一个读"原著"的奖

刘震云特意点出的细节——评审阅读华文原著——听起来像礼貌，实则是一种立场。中国作家所获的国际文学认可，大多经由翻译抵达：外国出版商、外国评论家、外国评奖委员会，读的是英译本或法译本。在那种模式下，认可的闸门设在翻译的另一侧，而翻译从来不是中立的。刘震云的话，把通行的方向倒了过来：评判的中心，是一个以华文为工作语言的社群，而非把华文当作需要中介的异质对象来对待的异邦。

评委会的组成把这个论点落到了实处。第13届由世华媒体集团旗下七家华文媒体的总编辑裁定：马来西亚的《星洲日报》《南洋商报》《中国报》《光明日报》，连同香港的《明报》《明报月刊》与《亚洲周刊》。换言之，为刘震云成就"认证"的，是在华文世界里讨生活的记者与编辑——正是东南亚（Southeast Asia）这一职业阶层，一个世纪来维系着华文出版的根脉。这不是国家文化机构，而是异国土地上、以华文为载体的公民社会。

## 马来西亚：中文书写的"守夜人"

这一层最值得停留。马来西亚拥有中国境外最具韧性的华文生态之一——既非偶然，也非没有代价。它的华文独立中学、华文报章、文学社团与书店，构成了一套文化延续的基础设施，而许多其他地区的族群早已丢失了这套东西。花踪文学奖由《星洲日报》于1991年创办，绵延三十余载，正是这套基础设施的产物。

学者、《世界华文文学论坛》主编李良说得很尖锐：这样一个奖的存在本身，就是"对单一中心观念的持续冲击"。在其框架里，东南亚不只是中华文化的接收端、一个被动消费外来书籍的市场；它已然成为策源地——华文写作在此被生产、被评判、被以自身尺度经典化。本届凭《故乡无用》摘得马华文学大奖的马来西亚华文作家马尼尼为，恰恰是这种生态所能孕育的作者：在马来西亚、用华文、为不必等待北京首肯便承认其为文学的读者而写。

这里有一重安静的反讽，值得大陆的想象好好消化。二十世纪的大部分时间里，默认的故事是中华文化从母土向外辐射，辐辏于忠诚却终究"派生"的离散族群。花踪奖却暗示，辐射早已不是单向。当一位大陆巨匠把一份马来西亚的荣誉，视作因"以本语读己"而格外有意义时，"中心—边缘"所暗含的等级，其实已经松动。

## 这个奖真正丈量的是什么

人们很容易用"软实力"的透镜去读此事——把它归入"中华文化走出去"便算了事。这读法不算错，却单薄。花踪奖并非国家工具，它早于任何政府文化部门而存在，也独立于其运作。它真正丈量的，是某种更难被武器化的东西：一个离散社群文化资本的厚度。

文化资本，借用布尔迪厄的提法，是一个群体生产、辨认并加冕文化价值的累积能力。马来西亚约七百万名华侨华人，依靠华校、报章与社团，建起了恰好能在华文世界里做这件事的制度。这正是吉隆坡颁出的奖项能承重的原因——换作一个不具备此类基础设施的社群里的同类机构，便承不住同等分量。与其说这是中华力量的投射，不如说它丈量的是马来西亚华人社会自身的密度。北京与台北的观察者都该看清这一分别：这种地位，不是靠文化输出预算就能买到的，因为它由从未领取相关首都护照的当地人，在本地生成。

这也以一种有益的方式，搅动了软实力的叙事。"走出去"框架预设了发送方与接收方。花踪的案例却呈现了第三种位置：一个既非发送方、也非单纯接收方，而是评判华文文化之标准的"共同生产者"的离散社群。对于身处大国文化竞逐敏感地带的东南亚而言，这一个自主的重心，值得被认真对待——不是当作他人影响力版图上的某个节点，而是就其自身。

## 新桥梁：新加坡与下一代写作者

若说大奖是头条，配套节目才是方向所在。本届花踪与新加坡《联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）》合作，增设微型小说奖，明确意在深化马新两地的华文文学交流。两个比邻的城市国家，各自拥有关读度极密的华文阅读公众，为最短的严肃文体共建一处竞技场——这是为区域华文文学"公地"铺设基础设施，而非一次性的姿态。

大奖层级之外的得主，同样要紧。与马尼尼为的大奖并列，邱向红（小说组）、范俊奇（散文组）、胡玖洲（新诗组）、牛油小生（报告文学组）分获首奖。一座健康的奖，其下层应挤满大陆读者从未听闻的名字——因为这份拥挤，正是一种文学生命力的活体组织。任一文学中心真正的危险，从来不是竞争，而是空场；而依此证据，马来西亚的华文文学场，并不空。

## 尾声：人工智能时代，"发明文学"而非"利用文学"

刘震云在雪兰莪以一句话收束，这句话理应比典礼活得更久。论及人工智能，他主张机器能够"计算世界"，人却以"血肉之躯"经历世界，而那血肉里诸多难以名状的心绪，恰是文学最珍贵的东西。他对写作者下了尖锐的判词：伟大的任务，是"发明文学，而非利用文学"，每一部作品都须重新有所发现、有所发明。

这恰是一个离散奖项的应有收尾。马来西亚的华文文学计划，一个世纪以来，本就是在约束中"发明"的练习——在一个华文并非国语的国家写华文，在没有文化部撑腰的情况下维系一部经典。若说华文书写的中心正在静默位移，那并非吉隆坡取代了北京，而是关于"华文文化是什么、由谁定义"最有意思的问题，此刻正在不止一处同时被提出。刘震云去马来西亚领奖，或许无意间，描述了他自己语言之未来的地理。

## 延伸阅读

**马华文学（Mahua literature）**：指马来西亚华人以华文进行的文学创作，自二十世纪以来形成独立脉络，既与大陆文学相联又自成一格；马尼尼为、黎紫书等是其当代代表。

**《星洲日报》（Sin Chew Daily）**：创于1929年的马来西亚华文报章，1991年创办花踪文学奖，是海外华文文学重要制度性平台之一。

**世界华文文学（World Chinese literature）**：泛指中国境外以华文写作的文学总称，马来西亚、新加坡、印尼（Indonesia）等地各有脉络，共享汉语这一表达媒介。

# A Prize in Kuala Lumpur: Liu Zhenyun, the Huazong Award, and the Quiet Re-Centring of Chinese Letters

On the evening of 24 October 2026, in the Malaysian state of Selangor, the Chinese novelist Liu Zhenyun received the 13th Gurunjin Huazong World Chinese Literature Award (古润金花踪世界华文文学奖) at the 18th Sin Chew Daily Huazong Literary Awards ceremony. To a casual observer, this is a literary-awards story — a mainland author collecting another trophy abroad. To look only at that surface, however, is to miss the more interesting question the event poses: why does one of the most prestigious honours in the Chinese-language literary world live in Kuala Lumpur rather than Beijing or Taipei, and what does that tell us about where Chinese culture now actually resides?

## A mainland novelist, a Malaysian prize

Liu Zhenyun is not a fringe figure. A leading voice of the so-called "native soil" (乡土) tradition in contemporary Chinese fiction, he won the Mao Dun Literature Prize for his novel *One Sentence Worth Ten Thousand* (《一句顶一万句》) and has seen his work adapted by the director Feng Xiaogang. His novels travel: they are translated, reviewed, and taught abroad. So when he says, as he did in Selangor, that among the many overseas prizes he has received, the Huazong award carries a "special significance" because its jury reads the Chinese original rather than evaluating through translation, he is not offering flattery. He is pointing at a structural fact about the literary world he inhabits.

That fact is simple and a little uncomfortable for the mainland-centred imagination: the institution that confers one of the rare global honours for Chinese-language writing is not located in a Chinese capital. It is run out of Malaysia, by a diaspora Chinese community, through a newspaper — Sin Chew Daily (《星洲日报》) — that has published continuously since 1929. The award is biennial, and across the entire Chinese-writing world it names exactly one laureate every two years. Scarcity of that order is itself a statement of standing.

## A prize that reads the original

The detail Liu highlighted — that the jury reads the Chinese original — is the kind of thing that sounds like a courtesy but is in fact a doctrine. Most international literary recognition for Chinese authors arrives through translation: a foreign publisher, a foreign critic, a foreign prize committee reading the work in English or French. The gate of recognition, in that model, sits on the other side of translation, and translation is never neutral. Liu's remark inverts the usual direction of travel. Here the centre of judgment is a community that reads Chinese as a working language, not as an exotic object to be mediated.

The jury composition makes the point concrete. The 13th edition was decided by the chief editors of seven Chinese-language media under Media Chinese International (世华媒体集团): Malaysia's Sin Chew Daily, Nanyang Siang Pau, China Press and Guang Ming Daily, together with Hong Kong's Ming Pao, Ming Pao Monthly and Asia Weekly. In other words, the people who certified Liu Zhenyun's achievement are working journalists and editors in the Chinese language — the same occupational stratum that, in Southeast Asia, has kept Chinese-language publishing alive for a century. This is not a state cultural agency. It is civil society, in Chinese, in a non-Chinese country.

## Malaysia as custodian of Chinese letters

This is the part worth dwelling on. Malaysia hosts one of the most resilient Chinese-language ecosystems outside China — not by accident, and not without cost. Its Chinese-medium independent schools (华文独中), its Chinese-language press, its literary societies and bookshops form an infrastructure of cultural continuity that many ethnic Chinese communities elsewhere have lost. The Huazong award, founded by Sin Chew Daily in 1991 and sustained for more than three decades, is a product of exactly that infrastructure.

The scholar Li Liang (李良), editor of the journal *World Chinese Literature Forum* (《世界华文文学论坛》), put the matter sharply: the very existence of such a prize is "a sustained challenge to the notion of a single centre." Southeast Asia, in his framing, is not merely the receiving end of Chinese culture, a passive market for books shipped from the mainland. It can be — it has become — a point of origin, a place where Chinese-language writing is produced, judged, and canonised on its own terms. The Malaysian Chinese writer Minima (马尼尼为), who took this year's Mahua Literature Grand Prize (马华文学大奖) for her book *Useless Hometown* (《故乡无用》), is precisely the kind of author such an ecosystem grows: writing in Chinese, in Malaysia, for readers who do not need Beijing's permission to call the work literature.

There is a quiet irony here that the mainland imagination should probably sit with. For much of the twentieth century, the assumption was that Chinese culture radiated outward from the homeland to a loyal but derivative diaspora. The Huazong prize suggests the radiation is no longer one-way. When a mainland master accepts a Malaysian honour as uniquely meaningful because it reads him in his own tongue, the hierarchy implied by "centre and periphery" has already loosened.

## What the prize really measures

It is tempting to read this through the lens of soft power — to file it under "Chinese culture going out" and leave it there. That reading is not wrong, but it is thin. The Huazong award is not a tool of state; it predates and operates independently of any government cultural bureau. What it actually measures is something harder to weaponise: the depth of a diaspora community's cultural capital.

Cultural capital, in the Bourdieu sense, is the accumulated capacity of a group to produce, recognise, and consecrate cultural value. Malaysia's ethnic Chinese, numbering perhaps seven million, have built — through schools, newspapers, and associations — the institutions required to do exactly that in Chinese. That is why a prize conferred in Kuala Lumpur carries weight that a prize conferred by a comparable institution in, say, a community without such infrastructure could not. The award is less a projection of Chinese power than a measurement of Malaysian Chinese society's own density. Observers in Beijing and Taipei would do well to notice the distinction: you cannot simply purchase this kind of standing with cultural-export budgets, because the standing is generated locally, by people who were never issued a passport by the relevant capital.

This also complicates the soft-power narrative in a useful way. The "going out" framework assumes a sender and a receiver. The Huazong case shows a third position: a diaspora community that is neither sender nor mere receiver but a co-producer of the very standards by which Chinese culture is judged. For a region as sensitive as Southeast Asia to great-power cultural competition, that autonomous centre of gravity is worth taking seriously on its own terms, not merely as a node in someone else's influence map.

## New bridges: Singapore, and the next generation

If the prize is the headline, the supporting programme is the signal of where things are heading. This year's Huazong awards added, in cooperation with Singapore's Lianhe Zaobao (《联合早报》), a micro-fiction category explicitly meant to deepen literary exchange between Malaysia and Singapore. Two neighbouring city-states, both with dense Chinese-reading publics, building a shared competitive space for the shortest form of serious writing — that is infrastructure for a regional Chinese literary commons, not a one-off gesture.

And the laureates outside the world-award tier matter too. Alongside Minima's Grand Prize, first prizes went to Qiu Xianghong (fiction), Fan Junqi (prose), Hu Jiuzhou (new poetry) and Niuyou Xiaosheng (reportage). A healthy prize is one whose lower tiers are crowded with names most mainland readers have never heard of — because that crowding is the living tissue of a literary culture. The danger for any literary centre is not competition but emptiness; Malaysia's Chinese literary field, by this evidence, is not empty.

## Coda: invention over imitation in an AI age

Liu closed his remarks in Selangor with a line that deserves to outlive the ceremony. On artificial intelligence, he argued that machines can "calculate the world," but human beings experience it "with flesh and blood" — and that the fleeting, nameless moods carried in that flesh are what is most precious to literature. His charge to writers was pointed: the great task is to "invent literature, not use it." Every work, he said, must discover and invent anew.

That is a fitting note on which a diaspora prize should end. The Malaysian Chinese literary project has, for a century, been an exercise in invention under constraint — writing Chinese in a country where Chinese is not the language of state, sustaining a canon without the backing of a cultural ministry. If the centre of Chinese letters is quietly re-locating, it is not because Kuala Lumpur has displaced Beijing, but because the most interesting questions about what Chinese culture is, and who gets to define it, are now being asked in more than one place at once. Liu Zhenyun went to Malaysia to collect an award. He may have inadvertently described the geography of his own language's future.

## Further reading

**Mahua literature:** Malaysian Chinese-language writing, with its own lineage since the twentieth century — distinct from yet connected to mainland literature; contemporary names include Minima and Li Zishu.

**Sin Chew Daily (《星洲日报》):** A Malaysian Chinese newspaper founded in 1929 that established the Huazong awards in 1991, a key institutional platform for overseas Chinese literature.

**World Chinese literature:** The umbrella term for Chinese-language writing outside China, with distinct Malaysian, Singaporean and Indonesian strands sharing the Chinese language as a medium.
