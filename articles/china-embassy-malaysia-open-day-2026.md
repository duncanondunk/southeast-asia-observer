# 使馆开门迎客：北京对东南亚青年的温和外交攻势

2026年9月15日，中国驻马来西亚大使馆做了一件使馆鲜少做的事：把大门向约八十名马来西亚大中学生敞开。活动以中秋节为名，但其编排透露出更大的图谋。在大国竞争日益转向争夺年轻人注意力的当下，这座地处东南亚心脏的外交机构，在一下午里同时变成了教室、文化沙龙与中国科技的展示厅。

## 短暂打开的一扇门

这场开放日选在农历中秋前后，本身就有讲究。但更值得玩味的是"开门"这个动作。使馆历来是签证与礼宾的机器，是普通人难以接近的"国家"象征。把学生请进来，而非在会议厅里见他们，等于在"国家"与"青年"之间拆掉了一段距离——也让原本紧闭的堡垒，变成了一个可以走进去的场所。

## 谁被请来，为何重要

宾客名单意味深长。使馆邀请了三所院校的学生——玛拉工艺大学（UiTM）、亚太科技大学，以及霹雳州打巴一所理科中学。他们并非精英外交的常客，而是普通的马来西亚青年，其中不少是华裔。这个群体历来是中马之间的人桥。把他们请进门，比在大会见他们，更能消解"国家"的疏离感，也更能把双边关系"年轻化"。

## 学生们看到了什么

馆内的安排将传统与未来搅拌在一起。学生们在外交官引导下参观介绍中马建交历史的展陈，观看关于中秋习俗、中国人工智能进展以及亚太经合组织"中国年"的主题影片，试穿汉服，动手制作并品尝月饼，体验投壶、中秋灯笼制作等中华传统项目，还试用了科大讯飞的人工智能产品。信号再清楚不过：中国既是古老文明，也是前沿科技——而马来西亚的青年被邀请同时看见两者。

## 为何开放日成了外交

我们不应把这场活动误读为单纯的待客之道。在整个东南亚，中国使领馆正悄然扩大公共外交的足迹——社区活动中的领事摊位、文化周、奖学金展，乃至如今的开放日。其逻辑是战略性的。在华盛顿与北京竞相争取影响力的当下，社交媒体上关于中国的误读充斥，塑造年轻一代对中国的认知便是一项长期投资。一座让人熟悉、愿意走近的使馆，远比一座紧闭的堡垒更难被妖魔化。

## 文化：柔软的切入口

中秋这个抓手很巧妙。这个节日由马来西亚各地的华裔社群共同庆祝，却也是能连接而非割裂的文化自豪之源。以月饼、灯笼与汉服为中心，使馆诉说的是一份共享的文化记忆，而不索取政治忠诚。这是最舒服的软实力——以文化作为公地。对一个警惕被卷入大国博弈的多元族群马来西亚而言，节日比条约更安全。

## 科技：更硬的卖点

科大讯飞的演示，是同一策略更锋利的一面。中国的AI崛起在西方常被框定为安全威胁；而在这里，它被当作一件青少年可以亲手触摸的科技产品来呈现。对照是刻意的。若马来西亚未来的工程师与消费者对中国的AI形成第一印象，不是来自新闻标题，而是来自一次亲手体验，那么软实力的红利将在十年里复利累积。

## 姿态的边界

但我们也应把这场姿态放在恰当的比例里。八十名学生是一个象征，而非结构性的转向。马来西亚自身的族群政治意味着，对华裔青年的高调接触，可能被一部分人解读为干涉——这是北京此前也曾踩过的敏感点。而开放日再温暖，也无法单凭自身推动贸易、化解争端或赢得政策。它的价值在于氛围：为更硬的国家行为，在危机时刻储备一笔善意。

吉隆坡的使馆开放日很小。但若把它放在北京在整个区域更大的侨务与公共外交推进中来看，它标示着外交如何被重新接地——不只发生在首都与峰会厅，也发生在教室与文化室里，一块月饼一块月饼地积累。对东南亚未来的争夺，将更少由宏大的宣言赢得，而由谁持续地、为它的青年"出现"来赢得。

## 延伸阅读

- **公共外交（Public Diplomacy）**：国家运用文化与信息手段塑造外国公众认知的实践，区别于传统的国家间外交，重在争取民心而非仅达成协议。
- **软实力（Soft Power）**：由约瑟夫·奈提出，指以吸引与同化而非强制取胜的能力；本文体现为文化与科技两条线的运用。
- **马来西亚华裔（Ethnic Chinese in Malaysia）**：约占人口两成三的少数族裔，长期充当马来西亚与中国之间经济与文化联系的桥梁，也是公共外交的关键受众。

# When an Embassy Opens Its Doors: Beijing's Quiet Bid for Southeast Asia's Next Generation

On 15 September 2026, the Chinese Embassy in Kuala Lumpur did something embassies rarely do: it threw its doors open to roughly eighty Malaysian school and university students. The occasion was an "open day" timed to the Mid-Autumn Festival, but the choreography told a larger story. In an age when great-power competition is increasingly waged for the attention of the young, a diplomatic post in the heart of Southeast Asia turned itself, for an afternoon, into a classroom, a cultural salon, and a showroom for Chinese technology.

## A door, briefly opened

Holding the event around the lunar Mid-Autumn festival was no accident. But the more telling move was the act of "opening the door" itself. An embassy is, by habit, a machine for visas and protocol — a symbol of "the state" that ordinary people rarely approach. Inviting students in, rather than meeting them in a convention hall, erased a stretch of distance between "the state" and "the youth," turning a closed fortress into a place one could simply walk into.

## Who was invited — and why it matters

The guest list was revealing. The embassy brought in students from three institutions — Universiti Teknologi MARA (UiTM), Asia Pacific University, and a science secondary school in Tapah, Perak. These are not the usual fixtures of elite diplomacy; they are ordinary Malaysian youth, many of them ethnic Chinese, the demographic that has historically served as the human bridge between Malaysia and China. Bringing them inside, rather than receiving them at a conference, dissolved the remoteness of "the state" and, just as importantly, refreshed the bilateral relationship by reaching its young.

## What the students saw

Inside, the programme braided the traditional and the futuristic. Guided by diplomats, the students toured exhibitions on China–Malaysia diplomatic history, watched films on Mid-Autumn customs, China's advances in artificial intelligence, and the APEC "China Year," tried on hanfu, made and tasted mooncakes, played the ancient pitch-pot game (投壶), crafted festival lanterns, and tested artificial-intelligence products from iFlytek (科大讯飞). The signal could not have been clearer: China is at once an ancient civilisation and a frontier technology — and Malaysia's youth were invited to see both at once.

## Why an embassy open day is now diplomacy

We should not mistake this for mere hospitality. Across Southeast Asia, Chinese missions have been quietly expanding their public-diplomacy footprint — consular booths at community events, culture weeks, scholarship fairs, and now open days. The logic is strategic. With Washington and Beijing competing for influence, and with misinformation about China rife on social media, shaping how the next generation perceives China is a long-term investment. An embassy that feels familiar and welcoming is far harder to demonise than one that stays a closed fortress.

## Culture as the soft edge

The Mid-Autumn hook is shrewd. The festival is celebrated by ethnic Chinese communities across Malaysia, yet it is also a source of cultural pride that connects rather than divides. By centring mooncakes, lanterns, and hanfu, the embassy speaks to a shared cultural memory without demanding political loyalty. This is soft power at its most comfortable — culture as common ground. For a multireligious, multiethnic Malaysia wary of being pulled into great-power rivalry, a festival is a safer vehicle than a treaty.

## Technology as the harder sell

The iFlytek demo is the sharper edge of the same strategy. China's AI rise is routinely framed in the West as a security threat; here it was presented to teenagers as a gadget they could touch. The contrast is deliberate. If Malaysia's future engineers and consumers form their first impression of Chinese AI not from a headline but from a hands-on session, the soft-power dividend compounds for a decade.

## The limits of the gesture

Yet we should keep the gesture in proportion. Eighty students is a symbol, not a structural shift. Malaysia's own ethnic politics mean that overt outreach to ethnic Chinese youth can be read, by some, as interference — a sensitivity Beijing has stumbled on before. And open days, however warm, do not by themselves move trade, resolve disputes, or win policy. Their value is atmospheric: building the reservoir of goodwill that harder-edged statecraft draws upon when a crisis arrives.

The embassy open day in Kuala Lumpur was small. But read alongside Beijing's broader diaspora and public-diplomacy push across the region, it marks how diplomacy is being re-grounded — not only in capitals and summit halls, but in classrooms and cultural rooms, accumulated one mooncake at a time. The contest for Southeast Asia's future will be won less by grand declarations than by who shows up, consistently, for its young.

## Further reading

- **Public diplomacy**: The use of cultural and informational tools by states to shape foreign publics' perceptions — distinct from traditional state-to-state diplomacy, it aims to win minds rather than merely strike deals.
- **Soft power**: Coined by Joseph Nye, the ability to attract and co-opt rather than coerce; here exercised along both a cultural line and a technological one.
- **Ethnic Chinese in Malaysia**: A minority of roughly 23 per cent of the population that has long mediated Malaysia's economic and cultural ties with China, and a key audience for public diplomacy.
