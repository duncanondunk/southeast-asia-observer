# 印尼启动100吉瓦太阳能大计：普拉博沃的"三年不进口一滴油"豪赌

## 一句话：不是玩笑，是国宴级的承诺

8月25日，印度尼西亚总统普拉博沃在峇厘岛为一座太阳能项目主持动工仪式时，甩出一句近乎宣言的话："这不是开玩笑。我们将建设100吉瓦……目标是三年内完成建设。"对一个煤炭仍占发电量八成以上的国家而言，这像是一句政治豪语，但结合他本月的国情咨文与能源部的配套表态，它更像是一份被摆上台面的国家时间表。

## 数字：从1.5吉瓦到100吉瓦的鸿沟

鸿沟之大，几乎需要用对数来丈量。印尼当前108吉瓦的发电量中，太阳能仅有1.5吉瓦，其余大部分来自煤炭；截至今年4月，含水电与地热在内的可再生能源占比仅为17.9%。换言之，普拉博沃要在三年内把太阳能装机从1.5吉瓦拉升近70倍。动工的三个项目位于中爪哇与东爪哇，每座约130兆瓦峰值；政府还将在峇厘岛与西爪哇为另外六个、合计约4.7吉瓦的项目启动招标。从1.5到100，不是修补，而是重建。

## 算盘：每年省下42亿美元补贴

普拉博沃算的是一笔能源主权的账。能源与矿产资源部长巴赫里尔透露，100吉瓦计划需投入730亿美元（约927亿新元，约合5260亿元人民币）；建成后每年可为印尼节省42亿美元（约302亿元人民币）的能源补贴。印尼是石油净进口国，国内燃油长期依赖高额补贴。"有了100吉瓦太阳能，加上地热、新发现的天然气田与提高的原油产量，我们应该连一桶石油都不再进口。"这句话的潜台词是：把补贴的漏勺，换成自主的电源。他在8月14日国情咨文中还提出，年内先建30吉瓦，并为每座村庄配一座1兆瓦太阳能站，顺带提前淘汰13吉瓦柴油机组。

## 隐忧：土地、电网与路线图三道坎

但智库基本服务改革研究所（IESR）首席执行长法比泼了冷水：成败取决于清晰的规划与详细路线图，包括年度目标、资金来源与有效监督。他向《日经亚洲》指出，除输电网络与容量的技术挑战外，融资与监管改革仍是主要障碍——"大规模发展太阳能需要大量土地，而取得并最终利用这些土地都需要时间"。在最初的"起飞"阶段，政府应优先推进能迅速见效的项目，以削减柴油使用、释放投资潜力、扩大清洁电力供应并提振公众信心。换言之，豪言能否落地，不在于口号，而在于征地、并网与招标能否跑赢政治周期。

## 延伸阅读

**能源补贴改革（Energy subsidy reform）**：印尼长期以财政补贴压低汽油与柴油价格，既是民生缓冲，也是财政负担；可再生能源扩张常被寄望于削减这笔支出。

**地热资源（Geothermal energy）**：印尼坐拥全球最大地热储备之一，与太阳能并列为普拉博沃"去进口"组合拳的关键清洁能源支柱。

**基本服务改革研究所（Institute for Essential Services Reform, IESR）**：总部雅加达的能源政策智库，长期追踪印尼能源转型与补贴改革的落地瓶颈。

# Indonesia Launches 100GW Solar Plan: Prabowo's Bet to "Not Import a Single Barrel" in Three Years

## The headline: no joke, a state-level pledge

On 25 August, at a groundbreaking ceremony for a solar project in Bali, Indonesian President Prabowo Subianto delivered what sounded like a manifesto: "This is not a joke. We will build 100GW… the target is to complete construction within three years." For a country where coal still supplies more than 80% of generation, it reads like political bravado. Yet paired with this month's State of the Nation address and the energy ministry's supporting statements, it looks more like a national timetable placed squarely on the table.

## The numbers: the chasm from 1.5GW to 100GW

The gap is so wide it almost demands a logarithm. Of Indonesia's current 108GW of capacity, solar accounts for just 1.5GW, the rest mostly coal; as of April, renewables including hydro and geothermal made up only 17.9% of total generation. In other words, Prabowo wants to lift solar capacity nearly seventyfold in three years. The three projects breaking ground sit in Central and East Java at about 130MW peak each; the government will also tender six more projects totalling roughly 4.7GW in Bali and West Java. Going from 1.5 to 100 is not a patch — it is a rebuild.

## The arithmetic: US$4.2 billion in subsidies saved each year

Prabowo is running a ledger of energy sovereignty. Energy and Mineral Resources Minister Bahlil Lahadalia said the 100GW plan needs US$73 billion (about S$92.7 billion) in investment; once built it would save Indonesia US$4.2 billion a year in energy subsidies. A net oil importer, Indonesia props up domestic fuel with heavy subsidies. "With 100GW of solar, plus our geothermal, newly found gas fields and higher crude output, we should not import even one barrel of oil," he said. The subtext: swap a leaking subsidy bucket for a home-grown power source. In his 14 August address he also pledged 30GW this year and a 1MW solar station in every village, while retiring 13GW of diesel plants early.

## The catch: three hurdles of land, grid and roadmap

But Fabby Tumiwa, chief executive of the Institute for Essential Services Reform (IESR), poured cold water on the optimism: success hinges on clear planning and a detailed roadmap — annual targets, funding sources and effective oversight. He told Nikkei Asia that beyond technical challenges of transmission and capacity, financing and regulatory reform remain the main obstacles — "large-scale solar needs a lot of land, and acquiring and ultimately using it takes time." In the initial "take-off" phase, the government should prioritise quick-win projects to cut diesel use, unlock investment, expand clean supply and build public confidence. In short, whether the pledge lands depends less on slogans than on land acquisition, grid connection and tenders outrunning the political cycle.

## Further reading

**Energy subsidy reform**: Indonesia has long used fiscal subsidies to keep petrol and diesel cheap — a cushion for households but a drain on the budget; renewable expansion is often pitched as a way to shrink that bill.

**Geothermal energy**: Indonesia sits on one of the world's largest geothermal reserves, alongside solar a pillar of Prabowo's "no-import" clean-energy combo.

**Institute for Essential Services Reform (IESR)**: A Jakarta-based energy-policy think tank that tracks the bottlenecks of Indonesia's energy transition and subsidy reform.
