# 马来西亚华人青年的"数字归乡"：一个AI寻根平台如何连接南洋与故土

## 跨越山海的归乡

"一百多年前，太公跟着母亲下南洋，再没能回到故土。如今，我终于能替太公说一句：我回来了。"站在福建安溪的土地上，马来西亚第四代华人江靖琳在札记里写下这句话。她的归乡之旅，起点不是一张机票，而是一串由留学生写下的代码。

江靖琳是复旦大学新闻学院的研究生。2025年末，她在导师指导下与来自湖南、台湾和马来西亚的同学组队，参加留学生创新创业比赛，萌生了用人工智能帮华侨华人寻根的想法。他们把平台命名为"寻根平台（RootLink）"，意为链接根脉。江靖琳和身边的马来西亚华人朋友成为第一批用户：他们翻找祖宅里的旧物、向亲友收集来自安溪的侨批，最终在人工智能对话中锁定了故乡最可能的位置。随后，她带着四位长辈踏上了回乡路。

## 寻根为什么这么难

表面看，寻根只需"回去找人"；真相是，这条路卡在两道与人有关的关口上。

其一在"中间人"。福建安溪民俗爱好者协会会长施桑镇是这次寻亲的"中间人"之一。他说，帮海外乡亲查祖籍，常遇到口述线索与族谱文字对不上的难题。清末远赴南洋的先辈大多没受过系统教育，闽南语在海外流传数代后发音已悄然变化，转成文字便产生音译偏差。以江靖琳为例，太公的名字口口相传被记为"麦粿"，族谱上其实写作"海问"，必须结合出生时间、下南洋时间与亲人名讳反复核对，很费工夫。

其二在"寻根人"自身。团队曾访谈二十位有意寻根的海外乡亲，不少人年事已高，既缺少使用人工智能对话的经验，能提供的有效信息也有限。归乡心切之下，技术设计不仅要帮人检索，更要照顾情绪：人工智能需要清楚告知还缺哪些资料、现在走到哪一步、下一步该做什么，并主动推送提醒，避免半途放弃。

## 当人工智能接管"中间人"

负责技术后台的同学介绍，要把零散信息和老物件精准定位到远方的家，人工智能对话机器人要攻克两个关键环节。先是祖籍地判断：接入公开数据库，让人工智能结合姓氏、地名、方言等信息给出祖籍地址，并附上置信度。再是"中间人"匹配：通过分析社交平台上成功帮人寻亲的人，总结其特质，为对接本土乡贤、开展线下寻亲提供画像。

把"中间人"交给算法，是这代青年最不一样的地方。过去，寻根靠的是宗族长老的记忆与同乡的口耳相传；如今，一套能检索、会提示、可记录进度的数字系统，把这份"热肠"标准化了。江靖琳说，这次回家"早已不是一家人的私事，而是多方力量促成的团圆"——当地融媒体、族谱保管者、宗族族长、侨联干部纷纷伸手。技术没有取代人情，反而把人情放大了。

## 不止一个"寻根平台"

江靖琳的项目并非孤例。在福建泉州，南洋华裔族群寻根谒祖综合服务平台已收录上万册数字化族谱，侨胞可在线一键检索姓氏源流与家族迁徙轨迹；浙江温州瓯海打造"侨谱"数字化系统，把纸质谱牒搬上云端；广东汕头的"潮汕家园"依托侨批数据库，上线以来已成功帮助约四十宗寻亲寻根。数字技术正搭起一座座寻根桥，让散居海外的华人在云端触摸故土根脉。

这说明一件事：离散群体的"根"正在被系统地数字化。当族谱、侨批、地名与方言被转成可计算的数据，"从哪里来"不再只依赖某位长者的记忆，而成了一项可以被服务、被规模化的公共产品。这背后，是侨乡政府把"留根"当作治理任务的自觉。

## 青年为何回头看

江靖琳说，寻根一方面是为完成老一辈的心愿，更重要的是回答"我从哪里来"。这趟回乡同行的四位老人，年龄相加超过300岁，但回乡的念头从未褪色。

值得玩味的是"回头看"的恰恰是青年。江靖琳们在中国读书、创业，项目已入驻上海虹口区青年科创空间，获得免费场地与侨联资源对接。她说："中国不仅是我们家族的根，也是我们这一代海外青年学习、创业的热土。"当东南亚华人新生代把求学与创业的目的地选在祖籍国，离散叙事便从"向外漂泊"转向"双向往返"——根在故土，路在脚下，桥在云端。

中秋将至，江靖琳接到阿嬷布置的新任务：给福建的叔公们准备节日礼物。每天收到叔公叮嘱"好好吃饭"的消息，她说，被远方的亲人牵挂着，这种感觉真好。这或许正是数字寻根最温柔的落点：技术把人带回家，而家把人留下。

## 延伸阅读

**侨批（Qiaopi）**：东南亚华侨寄回家乡的汇款与家书，是记录离散群体跨国联结的"海上文书"，现多已数字化存档。
**下南洋（Migration to Nanyang）**：近代中国东南沿海民众渡海南迁东南亚的历史浪潮，构成今日海外华人的主体来源。
**华裔新生代（New Generation of Chinese Descent）**：在居住国出生成长的年轻一代华人，其与祖籍国的重新连接正成为侨务新议题。

# A Malaysian Chinese Millennial's "Digital Homecoming": How an AI Roots Platform Reconnected Nanyang and the Ancestral Village

## The journey home

"More than a hundred years ago, my great-grandfather followed his mother south to Nanyang and never made it back. Today, I can finally say for him: I have returned." Standing on the soil of Anxi County in China's Fujian province, Jiang Jinglin, a fourth-generation ethnic Chinese from Malaysia, wrote those words in her journal. Her journey home did not begin with a plane ticket. It began with a string of code written by overseas students.

Jiang is a graduate student at Fudan University's School of Journalism in Shanghai. At the end of 2025, under her supervisor's guidance, she teamed up with fellow students from Hunan, Taiwan and Malaysia for an innovation contest for international students, and the idea of using artificial intelligence to help overseas Chinese trace their ancestry took shape. They named the platform RootLink — "linking the roots." Jiang and her Malaysian Chinese friends became its first users: they rummaged through old objects in ancestral homes, collected remittance letters sent from Anxi by relatives, and eventually pinned down the most likely location of their hometown through an AI conversation. She then took four elderly family members back to the village.

## Why roots-seeking is hard

On the surface, finding one's roots is just "going back to look for people." In reality, the path is blocked by two human bottlenecks.

The first is the "intermediary." Shi Sangzhen, head of Anxi's folk-culture association, was one such intermediary in Jiang's case. He says that helping overseas villagers locate their ancestral homes often runs into a mismatch between oral clues and written genealogies. Most ancestors who left for Nanyang in the late Qing dynasty had little formal schooling; the Southern Min dialect, passed down for generations abroad, had shifted in pronunciation, and transcribing it produced errors. In Jiang's case, her great-grandfather's name was orally remembered as "McGoi" but recorded in the genealogy as "Haiwen" — reconciling the two required cross-checking birth dates, the timing of the southward migration, and the names of other relatives, a painstaking process.

The second bottleneck is the seekers themselves. The team interviewed twenty overseas villagers who wanted to trace their roots; many were elderly, lacked experience with AI chat tools, and could provide only limited information. Desperate to return, they needed a design that did more than retrieve data — it had to tend to emotions: the AI had to spell out what documents were still missing, where the process stood, and what to do next, while sending proactive reminders so people would not give up halfway.

## When AI plays the matchmaker

The student in charge of the backend explained that to pinpoint a distant home from fragmentary information, the chatbot had to crack two tasks. First, inferring the ancestral location: by plugging into public databases, the AI combines surname, place name and dialect to suggest a hometown with a confidence score. Second, matching the "intermediary": by analyzing people who had successfully helped others reconnect on social media, the system distills their traits to produce a profile for linking seekers with local elders and enabling offline searches.

Handing the "intermediary" to an algorithm is what makes this generation different. In the past, roots-seeking relied on the memory of clan elders and word-of-mouth among fellow villagers. Today, a digital system that searches, prompts and logs progress standardizes that warmth. Jiang says this homecoming "was never just one family's private affair, but a reunion made possible by many hands" — local media workers, genealogy keepers, clan leaders and overseas Chinese federation officials all reached out. Technology did not replace human kindness; it amplified it.

## RootLink is not alone

Jiang's project is no exception. In Quanzhou, Fujian, a comprehensive service platform for overseas Chinese of Nanyang origin has digitized tens of thousands of genealogies, letting users search surname origins and migration trails with one click. Wenzhou in Zhejiang built a "qiaopu" digital system that moves paper genealogies to the cloud. Shantou in Guangdong, leaning on a remittance-letter database, has helped about forty reunion and roots cases since launch. Digital technology is building bridge after bridge, letting the far-flung Chinese touch their homeland's roots through the cloud.

This points to something larger: the diaspora's "roots" are being systematically digitized. As genealogies, remittance letters, place names and dialects turn into computable data, the question of "where we come from" no longer rests solely on one elder's memory but becomes a public good that can be serviced and scaled. Behind it lies the qiaoxiang governments' conscious turn to treating "keeping the roots" as a governance task.

## Why the young look back

Jiang says tracing roots is, on one hand, about fulfilling the elders' wishes, and more importantly about answering "where do I come from." The four elders who returned with her have a combined age above three hundred, yet the desire to go home has never faded.

What is striking is that it is precisely the young who look back. Jiang and her peers study and start businesses in China; the project has moved into a youth innovation space in Shanghai's Hongkou district, with free office space and resources channeled through the overseas Chinese federation. "China is not only the root of our family," she says, "but also the fertile ground where our generation of overseas youth learns and builds." When the new generation of Southeast Asian Chinese choose the ancestral country as a destination for study and enterprise, the diaspora narrative shifts from "drifting outward" to "moving both ways" — roots in the old home, roads underfoot, bridges in the cloud.

As the Mid-Autumn Festival approached, Jiang received a new assignment from her grandmother: prepare holiday gifts for the uncles back in Fujian. Receiving daily messages from them asking her to eat well, she said, it feels wonderful to be cared for by distant kin. Perhaps that is the gentlest endpoint of digital roots-seeking: technology brings people home, and home keeps them.

## Further reading

**Qiaopi (Overseas Chinese Remittance Letters):** The money orders and family letters Southeast Asian Chinese mailed home, "maritime documents" recording the diaspora's transnational ties, now largely digitized and archived.
**Migration to Nanyang:** The historical wave of southeastern coastal Chinese who crossed the sea to settle in Southeast Asia, forming the main source of today's overseas Chinese.
**New Generation of Chinese Descent:** Young ethnic Chinese born and raised in their countries of residence; their re-connection with the ancestral homeland is emerging as a fresh diaspora-affairs agenda.
