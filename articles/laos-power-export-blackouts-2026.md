# 卖电给邻居，自己却在黑暗中摇扇子：老挝"东南亚蓄电池"悖论

一个国家把八成电力卖给邻国换外汇，首都医院却要靠发电机托住呼吸机——这叫发展，还是叫卖血？联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）近日一篇报道，把老挝"东南亚蓄电池"光环下的另一面摊在了台面上。

## 一个数字，两副面孔

老挝被称为"东南亚蓄电池（Battery of Southeast Asia）"，靠数十座水电站向泰国、越南、柬埔寨、中国和缅甸输电。可光鲜的数字背后是刺眼的落差：老挝国家电力公司一名前技术专家估计，全国约八成发电量用于出口。官方数据显示，截至2024年的十年间，年度电力出口量从12太瓦时增至38太瓦时，翻了两倍多，相当于巴黎一年半的用电量。2023年电力出口创收26.5亿美元（约188亿元人民币，约合33.7亿新元）。

换汇是真金白银，可代价也真金白银地砸在本国人头上。

## 专线直供：电网为何绕开本国人

问题不在发电，而在"怎么送"。能源经济与金融分析研究所（IEEFA）亚洲顾问豪伯点破关键：大多数电力项目的输电线路是"专线直供（dedicated transmission line）"特定客户，压根没和国内消费者连上。换句话说，坝一建好，电就沿着专用线路直奔泰国或越南的买家，老挝城乡的配网根本不在回路里。

这背后是长期购电协议（Power Purchase Agreement, PPA）的"照付不议（take-or-pay）"逻辑：外国买家按合同锁定电量，旱季本地缺水时，老挝不仅得照常外送，还得反手高价从邻国买电补自家缺口。电网从设计之初，就把"创收"摆在了"保民生"前面。

## 旱季的水利困局

水力发电占老挝总发电量的八成，这意味着丰枯完全看天。每年3月至8月旱季，河水下落、用电却因酷热和空调激增，供需立刻倒挂。世界银行近期报告指出，老挝已多次出现"既供不上国内、又对邻国违约"的窘境，不得不高价进口能源。研究机构估算，2023年干旱年里老挝进口电力的花费约2.4亿美元（约17亿元人民币）。

卖电赚外汇、买电花外汇，一来一回，被旱季吃掉的恰是原本该投进配网的钱。

## 借电谋势：越南与中国的影响力角力

电力从来不只是瓦特和伏特，更是杠杆。尤索夫伊萨东南亚研究院（ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute）资深研究员黎洪和指出，越南近年加大从老挝购电，除了制造业用电吃紧，也有政治算计——借能源纽带对冲中国对万象的影响力。老挝去年投运的孟松风电项目，是区域最大风电场，电却几乎全输往越南，本地"一度不留"。

一边是中国资本深度参与的老挝水电版图，一边是越南用购电悄悄撬动地缘天平。老挝这座"蓄电池"，正极被用来给大国博弈充能。

## 通电率升了，可靠性没跟上

必须承认，老挝在电力普及上确有成就：全国约800万人口几乎全部通电，而1995年这一比例仅15%。但"接得上"不等于"用得稳"。老挝配网大多建于1970年代，早就跟不上旅游、基建、城市扩张和空调普及的节奏。万象一条原供单户的电线下，如今扛着整栋公寓，老变压器不堪重负；农村一棵树压线，整村甚至数村断电，因为没有备用回路。

负责维护电网的老挝国家电力公司还背着扩张债务和消费补贴的双重包袱。通电率上去了，可靠性却原地踏步。

## 蓄电池的账该怎么算

老挝的难题，是"资源诅咒"在能源维度的缩影：把最不发达国家最稀缺的资本品——稳定电力——优先出口，把脆弱和风险留给本国。出口创收是真实成就，可当医院呼吸机要靠柴油发电机续命、当酷暑里村民举着手机走出门避闷热，这套"以电养国"的账就得重算。

要破局，无非三件事：把配网投资与民生可靠性写进能源战略，而不只是盯着对外售电；在旱季短板处补上风光储与跨境互济，稀释对单一水电的依赖；在购电协议里留出自用裕度，别让"专线"把本国人永远挡在回路之外。否则，"东南亚蓄电池"的招牌越亮，本国民众在黑暗里摇的扇子，也只会越摇越久。

## 延伸阅读

**专线直供（Dedicated Transmission Line）**：出口电力项目常建专用输电线路直连外国买家，不接入本国配网，使发电与本国用电在物理上"脱钩"。

**购电协议（Power Purchase Agreement, PPA）**：老挝水电出口多按长期照付不议合同售电，旱季短缺时仍需履约外送，并高价回购补国内。

**湄公河委员会（Mekong River Commission, MRC）**：监测湄公河水电开发与跨境影响的政府间机构；老挝多座大坝由中国及泰越资本参与建设。

# Powering the Neighbors, Left in the Dark: Laos and the "Battery of Southeast Asia" Paradox

A country that sells 80 per cent of its electricity to its neighbors to earn foreign exchange, yet whose capital-city hospitals must keep ventilators running on diesel generators — is that development, or is it bleeding the nation dry? A recent Lianhe Zaobao report lays bare the other face of Laos's vaunted "Battery of Southeast Asia" label.

## The numbers: two faces of one story

Laos brands itself the "Battery of Southeast Asia," shipping power from dozens of hydropower dams to Thailand, Vietnam, Cambodia, China and Myanmar. Behind the glossy figure sits an awkward gap: a former technical expert at the state utility Electricité du Laos (EDL) estimates that roughly 80 per cent of national output is exported. Official data show that over the decade to 2024, annual exports more than tripled, from 12 TWh to 38 TWh — enough to power Paris for a year and a half. In 2023 those exports earned US$2.65 billion (about S$3.37 billion).

The foreign exchange is real money. So is the cost, paid in full by Lao citizens at home.

## Dedicated lines: why the grid bypasses locals

The problem is not generation but delivery. Grant Hauber, an adviser at the Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis (IEEFA), names the crux: most export projects run "dedicated transmission lines" wired to specific foreign buyers, never connected to domestic consumers. In plain terms, the moment a dam is built the power flows down a private line to a Thai or Vietnamese off-taker, while Lao towns and villages sit outside the circuit entirely.

Underpinning this is the take-or-pay logic of long-term Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs): foreign buyers lock in volumes by contract, so even in a dry-season shortfall Laos must still ship power out — and then buy it back at a premium to cover its own homes. From the drawing board, revenue was placed ahead of reliability.

## The dry-season hydropower trap

Hydropower supplies about 80 per cent of Lao generation, which means output rises and falls entirely with the weather. Every year from March to August, as river levels drop, demand surges on the back of heatwaves and air-conditioning, and the balance inverts. A recent World Bank report notes that Laos has repeatedly struggled to meet both domestic needs and its export commitments, forced to import energy at significant cost. One estimate puts the bill for imported power in the drought year 2023 at around US$240 million.

Sell power for foreign exchange, buy it back for foreign exchange — and the round trip quietly diverts to importers the money that should have gone into the distribution grid.

## Power as leverage: Hanoi's hedge against Beijing

Electricity was never only about watts and volts; it is leverage. Le Hong Hiep, a senior fellow at the ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute, argues that Vietnam's recent ramp-up in Lao purchases is driven not just by its own manufacturing crunch but by politics — using energy ties to counter China's sway in Vientiane. Laos's Monsoon Wind project, the region's largest wind farm, became commercial last year and sends almost all its output to Vietnam, "not a single watt" for local use.

On one side sits China's deep capital footprint across Lao hydropower; on the other, Vietnam quietly tilting the geopolitical scale through power purchases. This "battery" is being used to recharge a great-power contest.

## Electrification up, reliability left behind

To be fair, Laos has real achievements in access. Nearly its entire population of eight million now has electricity, against just 15 per cent in 1995. But "connected" is not "reliable." Most of the distribution grid dates to the 1970s and long ago fell behind tourism, infrastructure, urban sprawl and the spread of air-conditioning. In Vientiane a line built for a single household now feeds a whole apartment block, overloading aging transformers; in the countryside a tree on a line can black out an entire village — or several — for lack of any backup loop.

EDL, tasked with maintaining the grid, also carries twin burdens of expansion debt and consumer subsidies. Access rose; reliability stood still.

## How to reckon the battery's bill

Laos's dilemma is the resource curse rewritten in energy: a least-developed country exports its scarcest capital good — steady power — and keeps the fragility and risk for itself. Export earnings are a genuine achievement, yet when hospital ventilators depend on diesel generators and villagers step outside with phone lights to escape the heat, the "power-the-nation-by-selling-power" ledger needs rewriting.

The fix is straightforward in outline. Write grid resilience and household reliability into the energy strategy, not only outward sales. Diversify beyond single-source hydropower with solar, wind and storage, and deepen cross-border mutual support to soften the dry-season trough. And reserve a domestic margin inside PPAs, so dedicated lines never permanently lock Lao citizens out of the circuit. Until then, the brighter the "Battery of Southeast Asia" brand shines, the longer its own people will wave their fans in the dark.

## Further reading

**Dedicated transmission line**: Export projects often build lines wired straight to foreign buyers, bypassing the domestic grid, so generation is physically disconnected from local consumption.

**Power Purchase Agreement (PPA)**: Laos's hydro exports are mostly sold under long-term take-or-pay contracts; in dry-season shortfalls it must still ship power out and buy back at high cost to cover home demand.

**Mekong River Commission (MRC)**: The intergovernmental body monitoring Mekong hydropower and transboundary impacts; many Lao dams involve Chinese and Thai–Vietnamese capital.
