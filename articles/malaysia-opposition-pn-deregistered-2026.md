# 一纸撤销令，三道裂痕：马来西亚国盟被注销，谁在笑到最后

马来西亚社团注册局（Registrar of Societies，简称 RoS）在10月20日一纸公告，撤销了国民联盟（Perikatan Nasional，简称国盟）的注册。这个决定来得极巧——距离马六甲州选提名日只剩11天，距离11月14日投票日也不过三周多。国盟本是马来西亚最大的反对派阵营，旗下有伊斯兰党（PAS）、土团党（Bersatu）等政党；如今它失去的，是共同参选的“一张皮”——党徽、统一旗帜与联合动员的能力。

表面看，这是一起内政部的行政动作；往深里看，它揭开的是马来西亚政治的三道旧伤：反对派内部的夺权之争、东南亚“以机构之手消解对手”的区域惯性，以及安华·依布拉欣（Anwar Ibrahim）那面“改革”大旗下愈来愈明显的制度倒退。本文不替任何一方站台，只想把这场闹剧的来龙去脉掰开讲清，并顺带回答一个对华侨华人尤为要紧的问题：制度公正，到底还靠不靠得住？

## 程序合法，时机蹊跷

先说程序。社团注册局给出的理由并不离谱：国盟在10月12日提交的说明，未能驳斥9月22日“说明理由通知”里列明的指控。核心指控只有一条——国盟在7月16日的一場紧急最高理事会上，把创始成员土团党挡在门外，违反了自家章程。这封投诉，恰恰是土团党自己于8月11日递上去的。

也就是说，扳倒国盟的，不是政府，而是国盟自己人。伊斯兰党在6月已终止与土团党的政治合作，两党为“谁才是国盟老大”缠斗不休；土团党（前总理穆希丁·亚辛 Muhyiddin Yassin 的政党）一怒之下向注册局告了状。注册局据此认定，一个把创始成员拒之门外的联盟，已不具备合法注册的资格。

程序上，注册局或许站得住脚。但时机，太像一把精心计算过的刀。说明理由通知，恰好在马六甲州议会解散当天送达；次日，国盟活动被勒令暂停。而正式撤销令，又卡在国盟申请暂缓暂停的高等法院聆讯前两天落地。反对派说，政府律师10月19日还在法庭上称“程序仍在进行”——两天后却直接摘牌。这般节奏，等于亲手把“我们被政治打压”的话柄递到对手手里。

## 内部裂痕：伊斯兰党与土团党的夺权之争

这场内斗，根子在“马来右翼谁说了算”。伊斯兰党如今是国盟的主力，也是马来西亚222个国会议席中单一政党席位最多的一个；土团党则是穆希丁的老班底，自认“建盟有功”。两党在6月撕破脸后，伊斯兰党逐渐把国盟主导权收入囊中，成员还扩及民政党（Gerakan）、土著团结系的小党等。

被踢出局的土团党秘书长塔基丁·哈山（Takiyuddin Hassan，亦为伊斯兰党要员）放话，将在周四的聆讯上挑战撤销令，并可在30天内向内政部长上诉。穆希丁的回应则耐人寻味：他把这一裁决称作对所有“领导联盟者”的“提醒”——要公平对待成员党。一句话，把皮球踢回国盟内部：是你们自己不讲规矩，怪不得旁人。

可政治不是法庭。国盟失去的只是“共同旗帜”，伊斯兰党、土团党及各小党照样存在、照样能派人上阵。马来西亚人对此并不陌生：2018年4月，同一个注册局曾在大选前“暂时解散”土团党；那一次，反对派借友党党徽参选，照样赢了。旗帜可以借，信任却难补——这一回，国盟的信任窟窿，是自己挖的。

## 区域镜鉴：当反对派倒在机构手中

把镜头拉远，马来西亚这一幕有着挥之不去的区域影子。2024年8月，泰国宪法法院解散了在2023年大选中拿下最多席位的远进党（Move Forward），并禁止其高层参政十年；2017年11月，柬埔寨最高法院解散了主要反对党救国党（CNRP），118名从政者被禁五年。在东南亚，反对派常常不是败在票箱，而是倒在法院、选举委员会或注册机构手里。

马来西亚的不同在于：这一次的触发点，是联盟成员自己递的状子，而非针对“反对思想”的官司。这让它和柬埔寨的剧本划清了界线。但区域国家读的是同一组信号——当制度可以被用来清理对手，邻国只会更坚信“实力即正义”。有意思的是，连不少评论者也承认：就窄义的法律而言，注册局未必错；错在时机，时机本身就是证据。

更刺眼的是制度设计的硬伤：对注册局撤销令的最终上诉，竟落在一个内阁政客——内政部长——手里。一个足以重塑选情的决定，本应交由法院、并尽快了结。把生杀大权放在行政官僚手上，是对“改革”二字最直白的讽刺。

## 安华的悖论：改革旗号下的制度倒退？

说到改革，就不能不提安华。他当年正是喊着“改革运动（Reformasi）”的口号登上总理之位，华人社会、城市中产、马来温和派曾对他寄予厚望。可这一次，被摘牌的是他的主要对手；受益的，却可能是他自己的联邦伙伴——国民阵线（Barisan Nasional，简称国阵）的主力巫统（UMNO）。

分析人士普遍指出，巫统才是这纸撤销令的潜在赢家。眼下巫统正与伊斯兰党在马六甲谈合作，若两党联手对抗安华的阵营，国阵便可在夹缝中坐大。更有意思的是，安华领导的政府本就是“多块拼图”：他自己的希望联盟（Pakatan Harapan，希盟）加上巫统主导的国阵；可到了州级，希盟与国阵仍是死对头。安华一方面要倚仗巫统保住中央，另一方面又要在地方与它短兵相接——这种“中央合伙、地方互咬”的拧巴，正是马来西亚政治最真实的写照。

全国大选最迟2028年2月举行，安华也暗示可能提前。在一个反对派刚被“注销”的节点上提前大选，是借机巩固，还是引火烧身？答案，马六甲会先给出一半。

## 华人政治的算盘：制度公正才是定心丸

对马来西亚华人而言，这场戏不只是一种“看热闹”。华人主导的民主行动党（DAP）是希盟的支柱之一，华人选票在历次大选里都是撬动格局的关键。国盟若被削弱，马来右翼的选票压力会部分释放，短期内或减轻对华人议题的围攻；但另一方面，一个“连对手都能用行政手段摘牌”的印象，会直接动摇华社对制度公正的信任——而这份信任，恰恰是2022年许多华人把票投给安华的底气。

说到底，华人要的从来不是某一党的胜负，而是一套“输了也能再赢回来”的规则。当反对派可以因为内斗被注销、却也可能因为政治需要被注销时，少数族裔的安全感就会被悄悄抽走。马来西亚若能证明：无论谁在台上，注册局都只按章程办事、上诉归于法院而非部长，那才是对多元社会最好的定心丸。否则，今天被摘的是国盟的牌，明天寒心的，可能就是整个华社。

## 接下来看什么

第一，看周四的高等法院聆讯——国盟能否成功暂缓撤销、乃至翻案；第二，看各成员党能否在10月31日提名日前敲定一个“共用党徽”，毕竟旗帜可以借；第三，看巫统与伊斯兰党是否真的在马六甲结成选战同盟，这桩交易将预示更大范围的政治重组。这三件事，会在未来一个月里决定马来西亚反对派的生死，也为2028年大选写下注脚。

一场本可避免的内耗，把马来西亚的民主成色摊在了阳光下。程序或许合规，时机却写满了算计；对手或许自爆，制度却也因此蒙尘。对看客而言，最该记住的不是谁赢谁输，而是一个老理：当机构开始替选票做主，最先失去安全感的，永远是那些本就声音不大的人。

## 延伸阅读

**国民联盟（Perikatan Nasional）**：马来西亚以马来右翼为主的反对派政治联盟，核心成员为伊斯兰党与土团党，2020年成立后一度组过联邦政府，现为主要反对阵营。

**社团注册局（Registrar of Societies）**：马来西亚内政部下辖机构，依《1966年社团法》负责社团与政党的注册、监管与撤销，其行政决定可直接影响政党参选资格。

**改革运动（Reformasi）**：1998年安华遭罢黜后兴起的马来西亚政治改革浪潮，主张反贪污、司法独立与制度公正，是其后来重返权力中心的思想旗帜。

# A cancellation, three fractures: Malaysia's opposition de-registered — and who really wins

On 20 October, Malaysia's Registrar of Societies (RoS) issued a single notice that cancelled the registration of Perikatan Nasional (PN) — the country's largest opposition coalition, built around the Islamic Party (PAS) and the Bersatu party. The timing was almost theatrical: it landed just 11 days before nominations for the Melaka state election and barely three weeks before polling on 14 November. What PN lost was not its constituent parties but its "one skin" — the shared logo, the common flag, the ability to campaign as a single banner.

On its face, this is an administrative act by the Home Ministry. Look closer and it exposes three old wounds in Malaysian politics: a power struggle inside the opposition itself, a regional habit of dissolving rivals through institutions, and the growing democratic backsliding beneath Anwar Ibrahim's "reform" banner. This commentary takes no side. It simply untangles the farce and answers the question that matters most to ethnic Chinese Malaysians: can you still trust the rules?

## Lawful on paper, suspicious in timing

Start with procedure. The RoS's grounds are not absurd. PN's explanation, submitted on 12 October, failed to rebut the charges set out in a show-cause notice dated 22 September. The core charge is narrow: at an emergency supreme council meeting on 16 July, PN denied its founding member Bersatu a seat at the table, breaching its own constitution. That complaint was lodged by Bersatu itself, on 11 August.

In other words, it was PN's own people who brought the coalition down. PAS had ended political cooperation with Bersatu in June, and the two have fought over who leads the bloc ever since; Bersatu — the party of former prime minister Muhyiddin Yassin — complained to the registrar. On that basis the RoS concluded that a coalition locking out a founding member no longer qualified for registration.

Legally, the registrar may well be on solid ground. But the timing looks like a carefully calculated blade. The show-cause notice arrived on the very day the Melaka state assembly was dissolved; the next day PN's activities were suspended. The formal cancellation landed just two days before a High Court hearing on PN's bid to pause that suspension. The opposition notes that government lawyers told the court on 19 October the process was still "ongoing" — and then deregistered the bloc two days later. That rhythm hands opponents the grievance they wanted: we are being politically crushed.

## The internal rift: PAS versus Bersatu

The infighting is really about who commands Malaysia's Malay-right. PAS is now PN's anchor and holds the most seats of any single party in the 222-member Parliament; Bersatu is Muhyiddin's old machinery and considers itself a co-founder wronged. After the June split, PAS steadily consolidated control of the coalition, with smaller allies such as Gerakan folded in.

Bersatu secretary-general Takiyuddin Hassan — also a PAS leader — vowed to challenge the order at Thursday's hearing and can appeal to the home minister within 30 days. Muhyiddin's response is telling: he called the ruling a "reminder" to anyone leading a coalition to treat component parties fairly. In one sentence he shifted the blame inward: you broke your own rules, blame no one else.

But politics is not a courtroom. What PN loses is only the shared flag; PAS, Bersatu and the smaller parties still exist and can still field candidates. Malaysians have seen this before. In April 2018 the same registrar provisionally dissolved Bersatu weeks before a general election — and that opposition alliance simply contested under a partner's logo and won. A symbol can be borrowed; trust cannot be rebuilt so easily. This time, the hole in PN's trust was dug by its own hands.

## A regional echo: when opponents fall to institutions

Zoom out and Malaysia's scene carries an unmistakable regional shadow. In August 2024 Thailand's Constitutional Court dissolved Move Forward — the party that won the most seats in the 2023 election — and banned its leaders from politics for a decade. In November 2017 Cambodia's Supreme Court dissolved the main opposition CNRP and barred 118 politicians for five years. Across Southeast Asia, opposition forces are too often removed not at the ballot box but by courts, election commissions or registration bodies.

Malaysia's case differs in kind: the trigger was a complaint from within the coalition, not a case against an oppositional idea. That draws a line from Cambodia's script. Yet the region reads the same set of signals — when institutions can be used to clear rivals, neighbours only grow more convinced that might makes right. Tellingly, even sympathetic analysts concede the registrar may be legally correct in the narrow sense; the problem is timing, and timing is itself evidence.

The starker flaw is structural. The final appeal against a RoS cancellation sits with a cabinet politician — the home minister. A decision that can reshape an election should rest with the courts and be settled fast. Handing life-or-death power over political participation to an executive official is the bluntest possible mockery of the word "reform."

## Anwar's paradox: backsliding under a reform banner

Speaking of reform, Anwar cannot be left out. He reached the premiership on the strength of the Reformasi slogan, and ethnic Chinese voters, the urban middle class and moderate Malays invested real hope in him. Yet the bloc just deregistered is his main rival — and the likely beneficiary is his own federal partner, the United Malays National Organisation (UMNO), the anchor of the Barisan Nasional (BN) coalition.

Analysts broadly agree UMNO is the潜在 winner of this order. UMNO is now in talks with PAS for the Melaka polls; a pact against Anwar's camp would let BN thrive in the gap. The irony is thick: Anwar's government is a mosaic of blocs — his own Pakatan Harapan (PH) plus UMNO-led BN — yet at the state level PH and BN remain deadly rivals. Anwar must lean on UMNO to hold the centre while fighting it on the ground. That "partner in Putrajaya, enemy in the states" contradiction is the truest portrait of Malaysian politics today.

A general election is due by February 2028, and Anwar has hinted it could come earlier. Calling one right after an opponent is "cancelled" could consolidate his position — or backfire. Melaka will reveal half the answer.

## The ethnic-Chinese calculus: fair institutions as the real reassurance

For Malaysia's Chinese community, this drama is not mere spectacle. The Chinese-led Democratic Action Party (DAP) is a pillar of PH, and ethnic-Chinese votes have repeatedly swung the national result. A weakened PN may ease some pressure from the Malay right in the short run. But the impression that an opponent can be administratively erased also erodes the Chinese community's trust in institutional fairness — and that trust was precisely what moved many Chinese Malaysians to back Anwar in 2022.

What the community has never wanted is victory for any single party; it is a set of rules under which you can lose and still win again. When an opposition can be deregistered over an internal feud — or, just as worrying, might be deregistered because it is politically convenient — the sense of security of minority communities is quietly drained. If Malaysia can show that the registrar acts only by its own rulebook regardless of who holds power, and that appeals go to courts rather than ministers, that is the best reassurance a plural society can ask for. Otherwise, the banner taken down today is PN's; the confidence lost tomorrow may be the whole Chinese community's.

## What to watch next

First, Thursday's High Court hearing — whether PN can pause or overturn the cancellation. Second, whether the component parties agree on a borrowed common logo before nominations close on 31 October; flags, after all, can be lent. Third, whether UMNO and PAS actually forge a Melaka electoral alliance, a deal that would signal a broader political realignment. These three moves will decide the opposition's survival in the coming month and write the opening footnote for the 2028 election.

A self-inflicted implosion has laid Malaysia's democratic quality bare under the sun. The procedure may comply; the timing reeks of calculation. The rivals may have blown themselves up; the institutions are diminished all the same. For onlookers, the lesson is not who wins or loses, but an old truth: the moment institutions start deciding for the ballot, the first to lose their sense of security are always those who were never loud to begin with.

## Further reading

**Perikatan Nasional**: Malaysia's mainly Malay-right opposition coalition, anchored by PAS and Bersatu; formed in 2020, it briefly commanded a federal government and is now the leading opposition bloc.

**Registrar of Societies (RoS)**: A body under Malaysia's Home Ministry that registers, regulates and may cancel societies and political parties under the Societies Act 1966; its administrative decisions can directly affect a party's eligibility to contest.

**Reformasi**: The Malaysian political reform movement that surged in 1998 after Anwar's dismissal, advocating anti-corruption, judicial independence and institutional fairness — the intellectual banner behind his later return to power.
