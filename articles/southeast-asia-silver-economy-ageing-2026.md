# 当老龄化变成生意：新加坡的“辅助生活村”与东南亚银发经济

据《南华早报（South China Morning Post）》报道，新加坡女子海伦·冯在丈夫去世后，拒绝了子女让她迁居伦敦的提议——她受不了伦敦阴冷的冬天，转而住进武吉知马一处由独立洋房改造的“辅助生活村”。今年，她在那里过了92岁生日。她的选择，正是东南亚银发经济从无到有、由慢变快的一个缩影。当“老去”不再只是家庭私事，而变成一门横跨地产、医疗与护理的大生意，这个地区正被迫回答一个棘手的问题：谁来为这场长寿埋单？

## 现场：一间辅助生活村里的92岁生日

圣伯纳德生活辅助村藏在新加坡武吉知马绿树成荫的住宅区深处，是一栋翻新的两层洋房。据《南华早报》记者走访，护理人员用轮椅推着或搀扶着八位住客来到餐桌前，齐声唱起生日歌。海伦·冯的感言很简短：“我很高兴能住在这里。”对这位患有早期骨质疏松、却还能在房间里写日记的老人来说，最重要的不是医疗，而是融入社区、保持独立、被当做人而不是病人对待。同住的九旬住客郑素素说，这里的单人间与从前养老院的六人间“有天壤之别”，“我在这里可以做自己的事，从不无聊”。这类画面正在从孤例变成趋势。

## 数字：新加坡正式进入“超老龄”社会

这并非偶然。今年新加坡正式跨过“超老龄社会”门槛——65岁以上人口占比超过五分之一。这意味着，传统“养儿防老、居家终老”的模式，正迎头撞上少子化与核心家庭的小型化。联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）援引亚洲老龄化协会报告称，新加坡银发经济规模目前约835亿美元（约6000亿元人民币），到2030年有望增至1221亿美元，即约1550亿新元。一个长期被视作“负担”的群体，正在释放出惊人的消费力，而资本已经闻风而动。

## 中间的空白：机构与居家之间的第三种选择

真正值得玩味的，是这类设施填补的“中间地带”。许多老人已无法完全自理，却又不需要疗养院那种全天候医疗监护。辅助生活提供了折中：工作人员帮忙发药、写信，部分机构设记忆照护单元，应对认知退化。自2021年以来，新加坡政府已提供超过1100套此类公共住屋，私人地产运营商也加速进场。说白了，这是把“孝道”的一部分外包给了市场化服务——价格不菲，每月数千新元（约数万人民币）的月费，注定它先惠及有资产的阶层，再谈普惠。

## 生意经：从组屋补贴到私人资本涌入

银发经济之所以被资本盯上，在于它同时踩中三条主线：地产（适老化改造与退休社区）、医疗（慢性病管理与预防保健）、劳动力（护理员培训与输出）。泰国凭借医疗旅游与“养老签证”，把清迈、普吉变成国际退休者的“候鸟”目的地；马来西亚的柔佛—新加坡经济特区，则试图承接狮城外溢的养老需求。这背后是明确的商业逻辑：当本地中产与日、澳、欧的高净值长者都愿意为“有尊严的老年”付费，供给自然会跟上。

## 区域镜鉴：泰国的医疗旅游与“养老签证”

把镜头拉到泰国，故事更直白。泰国以远低于西方的心脏科、骨科手术价格，叠加温暖气候与英语服务，把“医疗”与“旅居”焊在一起，形成独特的康养产业。据区域研究，2025年赴泰进行康养疗程的国际老人超过280万人次，人均消费约4200美元（约3万元人民币）。对东南亚而言，这既是收入，也是隐忧——当“养老”成为出口品，政策的天平就可能在“赚外汇”与“保公平”之间摇摆。

## 隐忧：谁来为护理员养老？

最尖锐的矛盾藏在人力端。据《南华早报》另一篇报道，许多东南亚护理员本身就是中年人；一位在吉隆坡做居家照护的50岁菲籍护工，昼夜轮班时薪约25林吉特（约40元人民币，约合6美元）。马来西亚往往是她们的“中转站”：先考到证书、攒点经验，再转往日本、加拿大或欧洲拿更高薪。换句话说，东南亚在出口“照护”，却可能先耗尽自己的护理人力。当护工自己也会老去，谁来接棒？这是银发经济光鲜报表背后，最难算清的一笔账。

## 延伸阅读

**银发经济（Silver Economy）**：指围绕60岁以上人口形成的消费与产业体系，涵盖养老住区、医疗康养、金融保险与适老科技。

**辅助生活（Assisted Living）**：介于独立居家与全日制疗养院之间的照护模式，强调保留长者自主与社区参与。

**医疗旅游（Medical Tourism）**：以跨境就医带动的康养旅居产业，泰国、新加坡为该区域代表。

# From burden to business: Southeast Asia's silver economy takes shape

According to the South China Morning Post, after her husband died last October, Helen Fung turned down her children's invitation to move to England — put off by London's grey winters — and checked herself into an assisted-living facility tucked away in Singapore's leafy Bukit Timah estate. This year she celebrated her 92nd birthday there. Her choice is a small but telling snapshot of how Southeast Asia's "silver economy" — the goods and services built around an ageing population — is moving from the margins to the mainstream. When growing old stops being a private family affair and becomes a sprawling business spanning real estate, healthcare and caregiving, the region is forced to confront an awkward question: who pays for all this longevity?

## The scene: a 92nd birthday in an assisted-living village

St Bernadette Lifestyle Village hides in a quiet residential pocket of Bukit Timah, a refurbished two-storey bungalow. SCMP reporters described carers wheeling and walking eight residents to the dining table before breaking into song. Fung's speech was brief: "I'm very happy to be here." For a woman in the early stages of osteoporosis who still writes in her journal, what matters is belonging to a community, keeping her independence, and being treated as a person rather than a patient. A 90-year-old co-resident, Zheng, said the private room was a world apart from the six-bed ward she once endured: "I can do my own things here, and I'm never bored." Scenes like this are shifting from exception to trend.

## The numbers: Singapore crosses into "super-aged" territory

This is no isolated case. This year Singapore officially became a "super-aged" society, with more than one-fifth of its population over 65. The old model — raise children to support you in old age, and die at home — is colliding with falling birth rates and shrinking nuclear families. Citing an Ageing Asia report, Singapore's silver economy is now worth about US$83.5 billion and is projected to reach US$122.1 billion (around S$155 billion) by 2030. A cohort long treated as a "burden" is revealing startling purchasing power, and capital has already caught the scent.

## The gap in the middle: a third option between home and nursing home

What is interesting is the "middle ground" these facilities fill. Many elderly can no longer live fully independently, yet do not need the round-the-clock medical supervision of a nursing home. Assisted living offers a compromise: staff help with dispensing medication and writing letters, and some facilities run memory-care units for cognitive decline. Since 2021, the government has supplied more than 1,100 such public housing units, while private property operators move in fast. In plain terms, part of filial duty is being outsourced to the market — and at a price: monthly fees running into several thousand Singapore dollars (about US$2,500) mean it first benefits those with assets, before any talk of universality.

## The business case: from public housing subsidies to private capital

Why capital loves the silver economy is that it rides three veins at once: real estate (age-friendly retrofits and retirement communities), healthcare (chronic-disease management and preventive care), and labour (caregiver training and export). Thailand, leveraging medical tourism and a "retirement visa", has turned Chiang Mai and Phuket into "snowbird" destinations for international retirees. Malaysia's Johor–Singapore economic zone is trying to catch the overspill of Singapore's ageing demand. The logic is clear: when both the local middle class and wealthy seniors from Japan, Australia and Europe will pay for "dignified old age", supply follows.

## Regional mirror: Thailand's medical tourism and "retirement visa"

Shift the lens to Thailand, and the story is blunter. With cardiac and orthopaedic procedures priced far below the West, plus warm weather and English service, Thailand has fused "medicine" and "living abroad" into a distinctive wellness industry. Regional studies suggest more than 2.8 million international seniors undertook wellness treatment in Thailand in 2025, spending around US$4,200 each. For Southeast Asia this is both revenue and risk — when "ageing" becomes an export product, policy may tilt between "earning foreign exchange" and "protecting fairness".

## The worry: who cares for the carers?

The sharpest tension sits in the workforce. In a separate SCMP report, many Southeast Asian carers are themselves middle-aged; a 50-year-old Filipino carer working live-in shifts around Kuala Lumpur earns about 25 ringgit (US$6) an hour. Malaysia is often a way station: get certified, gain experience, then move on to better-paid work in Japan, Canada or Europe. In other words, Southeast Asia exports "care" but may first exhaust its own caregiving labour. When the carers themselves grow old, who takes over? That is the hardest line to balance on the silver economy's glossy balance sheet.

## Further reading

**Silver Economy**: the consumer and industrial system built around people aged 60 and above, spanning retirement housing, health and wellness, insurance and age-tech.

**Assisted Living**: a care model between independent home living and full-time nursing homes, emphasising autonomy and community participation.

**Medical Tourism**: the wellness-and-migration industry driven by cross-border treatment, with Thailand and Singapore as the region's exemplars.
