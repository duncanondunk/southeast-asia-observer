# 阿努廷执政周年：泰国转向中国资本求解增长焦虑

2026年9月7日，泰国总理阿努廷迎来执政一周年。按惯例，周年讲话多谈政绩；但今年外界更在意的是一组刺眼的数字：泰国把全年经济增长目标定在百分之三以上，可2026年第二季度的实际国内生产总值（GDP）同比仅增百分之一点九，比一季度还回落零点九个百分点，在东南亚主要经济体里几乎垫底。阿努廷政府没有回避问题，反而把答案押在了一个方向上——引进中国资本。据日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）报道，出于对半导体等尖端产业落后的焦虑，泰国正日益将经济复苏的希望寄托于中资。

## 数字：被邻居甩开的增速

把泰国放进区域坐标系里看，落差更明显。同为上世纪八十年代后起飞的新加坡和马来西亚，靠半导体与数字产业吸纳人工智能（AI）需求，2026年同期增速分别达百分之五点九和百分之六点零；而泰国只有百分之一点九。泰国并非没有高端制造，英伟达（Nvidia）的供应商台达电子（Delta Electronics）就在当地设厂，但政府官员也承认，由于泰国对中间产品进口依赖度高，这类企业的本土拉动效应相当有限。换句话说，别人吃到了智能化红利，泰国却还停在组装和代工的旧赛道。

## 从丰田到小米：两代外资的接力

泰国与外资的缘分，最早写在上世纪八十年代。彼时丰田（Toyota）等日本企业大举涌入，带来了技术转移、就业和产业化的模板，也让泰国落下"对日友好"的标签。四十年后，剧本换了主角。阿努廷7月访华，与中国领导人会谈、出席世界人工智能大会（WAIC），并与小米（Xiaomi）等中国企业高管会面，当场拿下总计七百亿泰铢（约一百四十亿元人民币）的投资承诺。曾几何时，是日本车企定义泰国的制造能力；如今，是中国科技与新能源企业被视为产业升级的突破口。

## 七百亿泰铢承诺背后的算盘

这七百亿泰铢不是小数目。泰国财政部部长艾尼迪（Ekniti Nitithanprapas）公开表态，期待"中国新一轮投资浪潮"带动人工智能、机器人等产业的升级。数据也撑得起这份期待：2025年泰国吸收的中国直接投资同比增长百分之十四，达到一千九百八十一亿泰铢（约三百九十六亿元人民币）的历史新高。对增长乏力、家庭债务高企、又急于摆脱"中等收入陷阱"的泰国而言，中资既是增量资本，也是技术叙事的载体。

## 本地化难题：技术转移为何失灵

但悬念也正在于此。泰国想借中资升级，核心前提是中资企业愿意本土化，把产业链和技能真正留下来。日经亚洲点出的矛盾是：泰国长期对中间品进口依赖，外资企业"下蛋"却难"孵窝"。前车之鉴不远——当年日资也没能完全把泰国推上高附加值台阶，否则今天的增速落差不会这么大。若中资仍停留在建厂、出口、收回利润的老循环，泰国不过是换了一任"房东"，并没有真正掌握技术。能否把外资的"流量"变成本土产业的"存量"，将决定这笔赌注的成色。

## 两条战线：向东拥抱，向西安抚

耐人寻味的是，阿努廷在向东拥抱中国资本的同时，并没有冷落日本。9月21日，他在东京公开否认"日企将全面撤离泰国"的说法，强调日本是紧密伙伴，投资"在变、而非消失"，并特别点名电动汽车（EV）与清洁能源的合作空间。这反映出泰国精明的两面下注：一边用中国资本填补增长缺口、撬动升级，一边安抚传统投资者、避免产业资本集中外逃。在美中博弈的大背景下，这种"两端通吃"的平衡术，既是现实所需，也考验泰国的外交功力。

## 评级企稳，但结构性隐忧未解

短期看，泰国争取到了喘息。9月18日，惠誉（Fitch）将泰国评级展望从负面上调至稳定，确认其BBB+主权评级；标普（S&P）与穆迪（Moody's）也均给出稳定展望。可评级企稳掩盖不住结构性隐忧：人口老龄化加速、家庭债务高企、制造业连续多年萎缩、政治不确定性未散。资本可以一夜涌入，制度与人力资本的积淀却需要一代人的时间。中国资本能带来厂房和订单，却带不来现成的工程师与高效的官僚体系。

## 结语：中国资本能否真正落地

泰国把增长的故事讲给中国听，中国资本也乐于在东南亚寻找新市场与新产能。但观察泰国历次"外资救国"的循环，真正决定成败的从来不是承诺金额的大小，而是本土产业能否借此长出筋骨。阿努廷的七百亿泰铢豪赌，是这一轮循环的起点；它究竟是泰国产业升级的跳板，还是又一次漂亮的财务账本，时间会给出答案。

## 延伸阅读

- **东部经济走廊（EEC）**：泰国在东部春武里、罗勇、北柳三府划设的经济特区，重点承接高端制造、数字与新能源投资，是中资与日资企业聚集的核心地带。
- **中等收入陷阱（Middle-Income Trap）**：指一国人均收入达到中等水平后，因成本优势丧失、创新不足而长期增长停滞的现象；泰国被学界视为典型样本。
- **外商直接投资（FDI）**：指境外资本在一国设厂、并购或持股以获取长期经营收益的投资，是泰国平衡增长与产业升级的关键变量。

# Anutin's first anniversary: Thailand turns to Chinese capital to cure its growth anxiety

On 7 September 2026, Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul marked his first year in office. Anniversary speeches usually tout achievements; this year the headlines were stolen by an uncomfortable set of numbers. Bangkok has set a growth target above 3% for 2026, yet actual GDP in the second quarter expanded just 1.9% year on year — down 0.9 percentage points from the first quarter and close to the bottom of the ranking among Southeast Asia's major economies. Rather than dodge the question, the Anutin government has pinned its answer on a single direction: bringing in Chinese capital. As Nikkei Asia reported, anxious about falling behind in semiconductors and other frontier industries, Thailand is increasingly betting its economic recovery on investment from China.

## The numbers: left behind by the neighbours

Viewed against its regional peers, the gap is starker. Singapore and Malaysia, which like Thailand took off after the 1980s, have ridden AI demand through semiconductors and digital industries to growth of 5.9% and 6.0% over the same period, against Thailand's 1.9%. Thailand is not without high-end manufacturing — Nvidia supplier Delta Electronics operates local plants — but officials concede that, because Thailand depends heavily on imported intermediate goods, such firms generate only limited spillover to the domestic economy. In short, others are cashing in on the AI dividend while Thailand remains stuck on the old track of assembly and contract manufacturing.

## From Toyota to Xiaomi: two generations of foreign capital

Thailand's romance with foreign capital was written in the 1980s, when Toyota and other Japanese firms arrived en masse, bringing a template of technology transfer, jobs and industrialisation that earned the country its "Japan-friendly" label. Four decades later the lead role has changed. In July, Anutin visited China, met Chinese leaders, attended the World AI Conference in Shanghai, and sat down with executives from Xiaomi and others, walking away with investment commitments totalling 700 billion baht (roughly US$2.1 billion). Where Japanese carmakers once defined Thailand's manufacturing capability, Chinese technology and new-energy firms are now cast as the breakthrough for industrial upgrading.

## The 700-billion-baht bet behind the courtship

That 700 billion baht is no small sum. Finance Minister Ekniti Nitithanprapas has openly voiced expectations that "a new wave of Chinese investment" will lift industries such as artificial intelligence and robotics. The data support the hope: in 2025, Chinese direct investment into Thailand rose 14% to a record 1.981 trillion baht (about US$5.9 billion). For a Thailand beset by sluggish growth, high household debt and an urgent urge to escape the middle-income trap, Chinese capital is both incremental funding and a carrier of a new technology narrative.

## The localisation trap: why technology transfer stalls

The real suspense lies here. Thailand's plan to upgrade via Chinese capital hinges on one precondition: that Chinese firms localise and genuinely leave behind supply chains and skills. Nikkei Asia points to the contradiction that Thailand's long-standing dependence on imported intermediates means foreign investors "lay eggs" but rarely "hatch" them. The precedent is close at hand — Japanese capital never fully lifted Thailand onto the high-value-added rung, or today's growth gap would not be so wide. If Chinese investors repeat the old cycle of building plants, exporting and repatriating profits, Thailand merely changes landlords without mastering the technology. Whether foreign "flow" becomes domestic "stock" will decide the quality of this wager.

## Two fronts: embracing the east, reassuring the west

Tellingly, even as Anutin embraces Chinese capital, he has not cold-shouldered Japan. On 21 September in Tokyo, he publicly rejected reports of a "full Japanese manufacturing withdrawal," stressing that Japan is a close partner and that investment is "changing, not disappearing," while highlighting cooperation in electric vehicles and clean energy. This reveals Thailand's shrewd two-way hedge: on one side it uses Chinese capital to fill the growth gap and pry open upgrading; on the other it reassures traditional investors to prevent a concentrated capital flight. Against the backdrop of US–China rivalry, this "play both ends" balancing act is both a practical necessity and a test of Thai statecraft.

## Ratings stabilise, but structural worries remain

In the short run, Thailand has bought itself breathing room. On 18 September, Fitch revised Thailand's outlook from negative to stable, affirming its BBB+ sovereign rating; S&P and Moody's also assign stable outlooks. Yet the steadier rating masks structural worries: an ageing population, high household debt, a manufacturing sector that has contracted for years, and unresolved political uncertainty. Capital can pour in overnight, but the accumulation of institutions and human capital takes a generation. Chinese capital can deliver factories and orders, but not ready-made engineers or an efficient bureaucracy.

## Conclusion: can Chinese capital truly take root

Thailand is telling China a growth story, and Chinese capital is only too willing to find new markets and new capacity in Southeast Asia. But if you study Thailand's recurring cycles of "foreign capital saves the day," what truly decides success has never been the size of the commitment, but whether the domestic industry grows real muscle from it. Anutin's 700-billion-baht gamble is the starting line of this round. Whether it becomes a springboard for Thailand's industrial upgrading or merely another flattering set of financial accounts is a question only time will answer.

## Further reading

- **Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC):** Thailand's special economic zone covering Chonburi, Rayong and Chachoengsao, designed to attract high-end manufacturing, digital and new-energy investment, and the main gathering ground for Chinese and Japanese firms.
- **Middle-Income Trap:** The phenomenon whereby a country, having reached middle-income status, stagnates for lack of cost advantage and insufficient innovation; Thailand is a textbook case in the literature.
- **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI):** Capital from abroad used to build plants, acquire or take stakes in domestic firms for long-term returns — the key variable in Thailand's bid to balance growth and upgrading.
