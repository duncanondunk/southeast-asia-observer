# 印尼接管雅万高铁股权重组债务：拆了违约的弹，债却留给了子孙

## 一条高铁，两种账本

印尼首条高速铁路——雅加达—万隆高铁（雅万高铁，印尼语称 Whoosh）自2023年通车以来，始终是普拉博沃政府“互联互通”叙事里的门面工程。但光鲜的运营数据背后，是一笔长期难算清的账。9月15日，印尼政府宣布由财政部辖下的专项任务机构接管运营公司印尼方股权与债务责任，试图把这条“政治高铁”从违约边缘拉回。账本被摊开，问题却被重新分配，而非真正消解。

## 违约风险为何在此时骤升

触发重组的直接诱因，是运营主体现金流持续承压。雅万高铁虽客流可观，但票收难以覆盖高昂的折旧、利息与维护成本。更棘手的是，项目公司多层控股结构下，印尼方股东多为负债率本就偏高的国企，再融资空间有限。国际评级机构此前已多次警告，若偿债安排不变，相关债务将在2026至2027年迎来偿付高峰。政府此时出手，与其说是主动优化，不如说是抢在违约触发前“拆弹”——用国家信用置换企业信用，避免单一项目违约外溢至更广的国企融资链。

## 重组怎么改：从企业债到国家信用

根据公开方案，重组的核心是“厘清产权、压实责任”。雅万高铁由印尼方企业 PSBI（印尼国企联合体）与中国铁路国际有限公司合资组建的印中高铁公司（KCIC）运营，PSBI 由四家印尼国企组成、持有项目六成股份。如今，国有企业部将整合原先分散在数家国企的权益，由财政部以注资或代偿方式接管部分到期本息。简言之，过去由项目公司“自己还”，现在改为“国家先顶上、再慢慢理顺”。这种路径在东南亚基建里并不鲜见——当项目具有强公共属性，政府兜底往往被视为唯一可行解，代价则是把企业债转化为更隐形的财政负担。

## 债务去哪了：账面腾挪而非消失

重组最值得警惕的，是债务的“位移”而非“消失”。财政部接手偿债，意味着未来数年财政预算中将多出一笔刚性支出；若以新增发债覆盖，则转化为长期国债本息。对普通印尼民众而言，这条高铁的代价不会因账面腾挪而减少，只是从可见的“项目亏损”变成分散的“财政压力”。批评者因此形容为“债留子孙”——当代人享受了通车便利与政治红利，偿还义务却沿着时间轴向后传导，留给下一届政府更大的腾挪难题。

## 中方角色与再谈判空间

作为最大债权方，中国国家开发银行（China Development Bank，简称国开行）提供了45.5亿美元（约合人民币323亿元）、年利率2%、期限40年的贷款，占初始建造成本60亿美元（约合人民币426亿元）的大头。债务延期至80年、每年偿还1万亿印尼盾（约合人民币4.3亿元），对中国方面意味着短期避免违约冲击、保住账面资产，但本金回收的不确定性被拉长。再谈判的空间，并不在于“是否追债”，而在于利率可否进一步下探、期限可否再弹性，以及能否通过沿线商业开发——车站物业、票务联运——把债权转化为可持续收益。值得指出的是，此案由印尼主动接管与重组，并非中方强制，这也为“债务陷阱”叙事提供了一则反例；但中国作为最大出资方，风险与收益终究共担。

## 八十年摊还：债留子孙的算术

把40年还款期拉长到80年，表面缓解了年偿付压力，却意味着利息总额成倍增加。前财长普尔巴亚曾称，专项任务机构每年可获得约4万亿印尼盾（约合人民币17亿元）回报，足以覆盖每年1万亿印尼盾的还款。但学者西瓦格（新加坡尤索夫伊萨东南亚研究院）提醒，专项任务机构虽能提供较确定的资金流、让项目公司财务更稳定，长期仍须靠更高的客运量、房地产开发与站内收入来夯实盈利。八十年，意味着今天坐上 Whoosh 的乘客，其孙辈仍在为这条铁路付息。

## 延伸至泗水：更大的赌注

普拉博沃政府有意将高铁向东延伸约700公里至东爪哇省省会泗水。西瓦格警告，考虑到既有路段成本已大幅超出最初估算，延伸线恐导致超支与债务风险暴增，须先做可行性研究、可靠的需求预测，并明确建设与商业风险分配。若盈利模式不先理顺，延伸不过是把今天的难题放大到明天的版图上。雅万高铁的经验恰恰说明：互联互通的政治红利易得，财务的可持续却难求。

## 写在最后：通车易，算账难

雅万高铁的故事，给基础设施建设热提供了一面镜子。项目本身无疑提振了印尼的互联互通与区域自信，但债务重组暴露的，是政治工程与财务可持续之间的永恒张力。对中方而言，最大债权方的身份意味着风险与话语权同步上升——既要维护资产安全，又须避免"债务陷阱"叙事被坐实。对印尼而言，真正的考题不是能否建成高铁，而是能否让这条铁路在八十年摊还期内持续产生现金流。基础设施的荣景，终究要回到最朴素的财政常识：借来的繁荣，迟早要还。

## 延伸阅读

**雅万高铁（Whoosh）**：东南亚首条高速铁路，2023年10月通车，全长142公里，将雅加达至万隆逾三小时车程压缩至约40分钟，由中国与印尼合资建设和运营。

**债务重组（Debt Restructuring）**：指债务人通过延长还款期限、降低利率或减免本金等方式重新安排偿债计划，以缓解短期流动性压力，但往往伴随长期利息成本上升。

**达南塔拉（Danantara）**：印度尼西亚主权财富基金，统管多家国有企业股权，在雅万高铁股权重组中扮演协调角色。

# Indonesia Takes Over Jakarta–Bandung HSR Equity to Restructure Debt: Defusing Default, Passing the Bill to the Next Generation

## Two ledgers behind one railway

Since it opened in 2023, Indonesia's first high-speed railway — the Jakarta–Bandung line, known in Indonesian as Whoosh — has been a showcase project in President Prabowo Subianto's "connectivity" narrative. Behind the glossy ridership figures, however, lies a ledger that has long been hard to balance. On 15 September, the Indonesian government announced that a special-task agency under the Ministry of Finance would take over the Indonesian side's equity in the operating company and its debt obligations, pulling this "political railway" back from the brink of default. The ledger has been opened up, yet the problem has been redistributed rather than truly resolved.

## Why the default risk spiked now

The immediate trigger for the restructuring is the operating entity's persistently strained cash flow. Whoosh draws respectable passenger numbers, but ticket revenue cannot cover its heavy depreciation, interest and maintenance costs. More troubling is the multilayered shareholding structure: Indonesia's state-owned shareholders already carry high debt-to-asset ratios and have limited room to refinance. International rating agencies had repeatedly warned that, absent a change in the repayment arrangements, the project's related debt would hit a repayment peak in 2026–2027. The government's move, then, looks less like proactive optimisation than a pre-emptive "defusing of a bomb" — swapping corporate credit for sovereign credit to stop a single project's default from spilling into the wider state-owned financing chain.

## How the restructuring works: from corporate debt to state credit

Under the public plan, the core of the restructuring is to "clarify ownership and fix accountability." Whoosh is operated by Kereta Cepat Indonesia China (KCIC), a joint venture between Indonesia's PSBI — a consortium of state-owned enterprises — and China Railway International. PSBI holds 60 per cent of the project. Now the Ministry of State-Owned Enterprises will consolidate the stakes previously scattered across several SOEs, while the Finance Ministry takes over part of the maturing principal and interest through capital injection or repayment on behalf of the project. In short, what was once "repaid by the project company itself" becomes "the state steps in first, then sorts things out slowly." This path is hardly unusual in Southeast Asian infrastructure: when a project is strongly public in nature, a government backstop is often seen as the only viable option — at the cost of turning corporate debt into a more invisible fiscal burden.

## Where the debt went: relocated, not erased

The most worrying aspect of the restructuring is the relocation rather than the disappearance of debt. When the Finance Ministry takes over repayment, a rigid expenditure appears in the budget for years to come; if covered by new borrowing, it converts into long-term sovereign principal and interest. For ordinary Indonesians, the cost of this railway does not shrink through accounting manoeuvres — it merely shifts from a visible "project loss" to a diffuse "fiscal pressure." Critics therefore describe it as "debt left to the grandchildren": the current generation enjoys the convenience of service and the political dividend, while the obligation to repay travels backward along the timeline, leaving the next administration a harder puzzle to solve.

## Beijing's role and room for renegotiation

As the largest creditor, China Development Bank provided a 40-year loan of US$4.55 billion at 2 per cent annual interest — the bulk of the US$6 billion initial construction cost. Extending repayment to 80 years, with Indonesia owing 1 trillion rupiah (roughly US$61 million) a year, means China avoids an immediate default shock and preserves its book assets in the short term, yet stretches the uncertainty of principal recovery. The room for renegotiation lies not in "whether to collect" but in whether the interest rate can be lowered further, the tenor made more flexible, and the debt converted into sustainable returns through commercial development along the line — station real estate, integrated ticketing and the like. It is worth noting that this case was initiated by Indonesia's own takeover and restructuring, not imposed by Beijing, offering a counterexample to the "debt-trap" narrative — yet, as the largest financier, China ultimately shares both the risk and the reward.

## 80-year amortisation: the arithmetic of "debt to the grandchildren"

Stretching the 40-year schedule to 80 years eases annual pressure on paper, but multiplies total interest. Former finance minister Purbaya had argued that a special-task agency could earn about 4 trillion rupiah (roughly US$242 million) a year in returns, enough to cover the 1 trillion rupiah (about US$61 million) annual repayment. But Siwage Dharma Negara of the ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute in Singapore cautions that while such an agency provides a more certain cash flow and stabilises the project company's finances, the line's long-term sustainability still depends on higher passenger volumes, property development and in-station revenue. Eighty years means that a child boarding Whoosh today may have grandchildren still paying interest on this railway.

## Extending to Surabaya: a bigger bet

The Prabowo government intends to extend the line roughly 700 kilometres east to Surabaya, capital of East Java. Siwage warns that, given the existing section already cost far more than first estimated, the extension could trigger cost overruns and a surge in debt risk; it must first undergo feasibility studies, reliable demand forecasts and a clear allocation of construction and commercial risk. If the profit model is not fixed first, the extension merely enlarges today's problem onto tomorrow's map. The Whoosh experience shows precisely this: the political dividend of connectivity is easy to reap, but financial sustainability is hard to earn.

## In the end: easy to build, hard to balance the books

The Whoosh saga holds up a mirror to the infrastructure boom. The railway has plainly lifted Indonesia's connectivity and regional confidence, yet the debt restructuring lays bare the perennial tension between political prestige and financial sustainability. For China, as the largest creditor, risk and leverage rise together — it must protect its assets while avoiding confirmation of the "debt-trap" narrative. For Indonesia, the real test is not whether it can build a high-speed line, but whether the line can generate cash flow across an eighty-year amortisation. The glow of infrastructure, in the end, always returns to the simplest fiscal truth: borrowed prosperity is eventually repaid.

**Debt Restructuring:** The rescheduling of repayment — through longer tenors, lower rates or principal relief — to ease short-term liquidity pressure, usually at the cost of higher long-term interest.
