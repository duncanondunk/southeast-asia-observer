# 柬埔寨首届华文教育研讨会：从“复课兴学”到直面办学痛点

当地时间9月5日，柬埔寨首届华文教育研讨会在金边举行。据中新社报道，这场会议由中国驻柬埔寨大使馆与柬华理事总会联合主办，柬华理事总会会长方侨生、中国使馆公使衔参赞赵海燕均到场出席。说它是“柬埔寨华文教育发展史上的一次重要会议”，并不为过——毕竟，这是一个曾被连根拔起、又从废墟里重新长出来的教育网络，第一次坐下来系统性地谈论“怎么办下去”。这背后，是柬埔寨华人社会用三十五年攒下的家底，也是一道绕不开的承传考题。

## 一场迟来的“行业大会”

柬埔寨的华文教育，命运多舛。上世纪七十年代，红色高棉时期对华文学校的打压近乎毁灭性，整个体系几近归零。据方侨生在会上回顾，柬埔寨华文教育“走过百年风雨”，直到1990年才在柬华理事总会的推动下“复课兴学”。换句话说，今天我们能看到的57所华校、5万多名学生，是在三十五年里从近乎空白中一砖一瓦重建起来的。

一个值得玩味的事实是：这样一张网络，过去竟从未有过一次全行业的研讨会。各地华校各自办学、各找门路，痛点相似，却长期缺少一个把经验摊开来、把难题摆上桌的机制。这次研讨会，本质上是把这个离散社群的教育网络，第一次当作“一个行业”来认真对待。这本身就说明，柬埔寨华文教育已经从“活下来”的阶段，走到了“怎么活得更好”的关口——从求生存，转向求质量。

## 数字：57所华校、5万学生

方侨生给出的数字很硬：目前全柬由柬华理事总会统筹管理的华校已达57所，在校学生5万余众。

这组数字放在东南亚华文教育的版图里看，意味深长。马来西亚的华文教育有完整的小学、中学乃至独立大学体系，是“制度性存在”；泰国的华文教育则更多依附于王室与政府的合作框架。柬埔寨的特殊之处在于：它的华文教育曾几乎被完全抹去，今天的规模是“失而复得”。57所、5万人，不是自然延续的结果，而是两代人接力抢救的产物——是废墟上的重建，而非老树发新枝。

但数字也藏着隐忧。5万名学生散落在全国57所学校，平均一所才八百多人，其中不少还是周六日才开的补习式教学。规模有了，集中度与稳定性却仍薄弱，离“体系化”还差着一口气。

## 痛点：人才、师资、经费三道坎

方侨生不回避问题，把办学痛点归纳为三条：管理人才紧缺、师资力量薄弱、办学经费不足。

这三道坎，几乎是海外华人学校走到第二代、第三代时都会撞上的“结构性天花板”。第一代移民勒紧裤腰带把学校办起来，靠的是热情和同乡网络；可要谈“可持续发展”，光有热情远远不够。管学校的人从哪来？老师谁来当、靠不靠得住？钱又从哪来、能不能长久？柬埔寨把这三件事摆到桌面上，反而比粉饰太平更有价值——正视问题，才谈得上解决，也才对得起过去三十五年的苦心经营。

## 转向：从单一语言学校到中柬双语

方侨生提出的四个发展方向里，最关键的信号，是“加快从单一华文语言学校，向中柬双语学校转型，深度融入柬国民教育体系”。

这一步棋走得很清醒。过去华文学校本质上是华侨社群的“母语补习班”，需求来自族裔认同。但柬埔寨今天学中文的孩子，越来越多是冲着实打实的就业机会来的——中资企业遍布柬埔寨，懂中文意味着更好的薪水。把华文学校变成中柬双语、并镶进柬埔寨自己的国民教育，等于把“族裔需求”升级成“国家需求”，让华文教育有了不依赖华人社区兴衰的韧性。说白了，绑上柬埔寨的发展列车，比只靠侨社输血活得更久，也更能抵御风云变幻。

## 根上的事：本土师资才是长久之计

赵海燕代表使馆提的三点希望中，有一条最该被记住：积蓄本土力量，夯实华教根基。

这戳中了一个长期被忽视的软肋：柬埔寨华校高度依赖从中国外派或短期支教的教师，外派老师来了又走，教材和教法未必贴地。真正能扎下来的，是本土培养、拿柬埔寨工资、把教中文当成本职的那批人。柬华理事总会这些年推的“师范班”“师资培训班”，方向是对的，但规模还远远不够喂饱57所学校。研讨会把“本土师资培养”单列出来强调，说明业者终于把焦点从“有没有老师”挪到了“老师是不是自己人”——这才是治本。

首届研讨会落幕，真正的工作才刚开始。57所华校、5万名学生，是柬埔寨华人社会用三十五年攒下的家底；而管理人才、师资、经费这三道坎，能不能跨过去，决定这份家底是传下去，还是再次随风飘散。华文教育在柬埔寨的命，从来不在风口上，而在每一间教室、每一本教材、每一位留下来的老师手里。把它当成一场需要久久为功的“行业建设”，柬埔寨的华文教育才真正立得住。

## 延伸阅读

**柬华理事总会（Cambodian Chinese Association）**：柬埔寨华人最高侨团协调机构，统筹全柬华校网与华社事务，1990年起主导华文教育复课兴学。

**中柬双语教育（Sino-Khmer bilingual education）**：将华文教学嵌入柬埔寨国民教育体系的转型路径，意在把族裔语言需求升级为国家发展需求。

**本土师资培养（local teacher cultivation）**：由柬埔寨本地培养、长期留任的华文教师梯队，被视为华文教育可持续的根基，用以替代对外派教师的高度依赖。

# Cambodia's First Chinese-Education Seminar: From Rebuilding Schools to Confronting the Hard Truth

On September 5, the first-ever seminar on Chinese-language education in Cambodia convened in Phnom Penh. Co-hosted by the Chinese Embassy and the Cambodian Chinese Association — the country's peak ethnic-Chinese coordinating body — the meeting drew federation chairman Pung Kheav Se and embassy minister-counselor Zhao Haiyan. Calling it "a milestone in the history of Chinese education in Cambodia" is no exaggeration. This is an education network that was uprooted and has been rebuilt from the rubble, sitting down for the first time to ask, systematically, how to keep going. Behind the scene lies the patrimony Cambodian Chinese society spent thirty-five years assembling — and an unavoidable question of succession.

## A long-overdue "industry summit"

Cambodian Chinese education has had a brutal history. During the 1970s, the Khmer Rouge era all but annihilated Chinese schools; the entire system was reduced to near zero. As Pung Kheav Se recalled at the meeting, the sector "weathered a century of storms" and only resumed classes in 1990 under the federation's push. In other words, the 57 schools and 50,000-plus students we see today were rebuilt from almost nothing in just 35 years.

Here is the telling part: a network this size had, until now, never held an all-sector seminar. Individual schools ran on their own, each finding its own way, sharing similar pain points but lacking any mechanism to lay experience on the table. This seminar is, in effect, the first time the diaspora's education network is being treated as an industry. That fact alone shows Cambodian Chinese education has moved from the "survive" stage to the "how do we thrive" stage — from mere survival to a quest for quality.

## The numbers: 57 schools, 50,000 students

Pung's figures are hard: 57 federation-managed schools now operate nationwide, with over 50,000 students enrolled.

Set against the wider Southeast Asian map, the numbers are revealing. Malaysia's Chinese education is an institutional presence, with a full ladder from primary to independent university; Thailand's rides on royal and government cooperation frameworks. Cambodia is different — its Chinese education was almost erased, and today's scale is "lost and recovered." Those 57 schools and 50,000 students are the product of two generations' rescue effort, not natural continuity. It is a reconstruction from ruins, not an old tree sprouting new branches.

But the numbers hide a fragility. Fifty thousand students spread across 57 schools averages barely 800 per campus — and many are weekend remedial-style classes. Scale exists; concentration and stability do not yet, and the sector is still a breath away from true systematization.

## The pain points: talent, teachers, funding

Pung did not dodge the problems, naming three: a shortage of management talent, weak teaching staff, and insufficient funding.

These three hurdles are the structural ceiling that overseas Chinese schools hit at the second or third generation. The first generation scraped schools together on passion and hometown networks; but sustainability demands more than zeal. Who runs the schools? Who teaches, and can they be retained? Where does the money come from, and will it last? By putting these on the table, Cambodia did something more useful than papering over cracks — naming the problem is the precondition for solving it, and for honoring thirty-five years of painstaking effort.

## The pivot: from language school to bilingual

Among Pung's four directions, the key signal is the call to "shift from single-language Chinese schools to Sino-Khmer bilingual schools, deeply integrated into Cambodia's national education system."

This is a shrewd move. Historically, Chinese schools were the ethnic community's mother-tongue cram schools, their demand rooted in identity. But many of today's Chinese learners in Cambodia are chasing real jobs — Chinese firms are everywhere, and Chinese fluency means better pay. Turning these schools bilingual and embedding them in Cambodia's own system upgrades "ethnic demand" into "national demand," giving Chinese education a resilience independent of the ethnic community's fortunes. In plain terms: hitching a ride on Cambodia's development train beats relying on the community's drip-feed, and weathers political winds far better.

## The root issue: local teachers are the only durable fix

Among embassy minister-counselor Zhao Haiyan's three hopes, one deserves to be remembered: accumulate local strength and consolidate the foundation of Chinese education.

This hits a long-neglected soft spot: Cambodian Chinese schools lean heavily on teachers dispatched or short-term volunteered from China. They come and go; textbooks and methods do not always fit the ground. What truly sticks is the locally trained cohort — paid Cambodian wages, treating Chinese teaching as a career. The federation's teacher-training classes point the right way, but their scale is far from enough to feed 57 schools. By singling out "local teacher cultivation," the seminar shows the sector is finally shifting focus from "do we have teachers" to "are the teachers our own people" — which is the real cure.

The inaugural seminar has closed; the real work is just beginning. Fifty-seven schools and 50,000 students are the patrimony Cambodian Chinese society spent 35 years building. Whether the three hurdles — management talent, teachers, funding — can be crossed will decide if that patrimony is passed on or scatters again. The fate of Chinese education in Cambodia was never in the wind; it rests in every classroom, every textbook, every teacher who stays. Treating it as an "industry build-out" that demands sustained effort is what will make Cambodian Chinese education truly stand on its own.
