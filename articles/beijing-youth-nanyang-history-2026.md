# 当北京的青少年走进"南洋"：中国如何把华侨记忆重铸成桥

9月16日，在北京的中国华侨历史博物馆里，一群孩子围着陈列书信、照片与电影道具的展柜站定——他们当中有来自城郊社区的新市民儿童，有在中国留学的马来西亚学生，也有志愿者。这场以"侨心传史·丝路连心"为主题的志愿服务活动，由北京市侨联马来西亚归侨联谊会发起，表面目标朴素：让青少年感受华侨先辈厚重的"家国情怀"。而这场活动本身，恰是一幅清晰的切片，映出当代中国如何对待其华侨记忆——不是私家的家族珍藏，而是公共的、教育性的、可作外交资产加以运用的记忆。

## 现场：一座博物馆、一场迁徙、一场战争

孩子们走过了华侨历史展，也参观了电影《给阿嬷的情书》的道具与侨批专题展——侨批，是当年跨越南海、同时寄送银钱与家书的汇兑信函。循着一件件实物，他们被带回华侨下南洋的艰辛开篇：凶险的远洋渡海、苦力契约、在荒地白手起家的聚落。

接着是战争记忆。南侨机工后人的讲述把历史拉近：1939年，为应对日本切断中国海上补给线，三千二百余名南洋华侨机工响应号召，奔赴滇缅公路抢运物资；三年间运送军需五十万吨，一千多人再未归来。在官方记忆里，南侨机工始终是离散群体爱国情怀最常被引用的符号。

## 南洋：离散群体的奠基神话

"南洋"与其说是地理，不如说是一则奠基叙事。对马来西亚、新加坡、印度尼西亚、泰国、菲律宾的华裔社群而言，这个词浓缩了两个世纪的离乡、劳作、歧视与不完全的归属。站在一座北京的博物馆里重述这场离乡，实质上是宣示：中华人民共和国，才是这则主要发生在国境之外的故事的当仁不让的讲述者。

把移民群体的记忆收归己有，并非中国独有。每一处输出劳动力的社会都会讲述自己的移民史。但中国的特殊之处在规模与意图：它拥有一整套专门的侨务官僚体系——侨联组织一直延伸到区县与社区——并始终把离散历史当作可随时取用的政策工具。北京的这场活动正是如此：它将移民史、战时牺牲与当代青年教育，折叠成一条无缝的训诫。

## 南侨机工：最锋利的爱国符号

南侨机工的故事为何占据核心？因为它化解了离散政治中一个长久的焦虑：离乡者是否"不够中国"。1939年的归国，把"离开"扭转为"证明"。这些华侨在国家危难时回返，死在一条山路上。再没有哪种叙事比这更能服务于"桥"的隐喻。

值得注意谁在讲述：不是史学家，而是一位外孙。记忆正以家族为介质横向传递，而国家只提供框架。这种组合——民间亲缘记忆裹上制度化的典礼外衣——正是那份家国情怀能够黏住人心的原因。

## 一所熬过国家的课堂

在场的马来西亚留学生给出了第二课，也更不轻松。一位清华大学的留学生说，自己能熟练使用中文并来华读书，得益于马来西亚"从幼儿园开始"的华文教育体系；他提到了林连玉——那位坚持中文教学、却因此被剥夺公民权的教育家。

此处，官方叙事擦过了一段更硬的历史。马来西亚的华文学校得以存续，并非因为国家，而是顶着国家：靠社群筹款、政治抗争，以及甘愿被边缘化的代价。北京听众听到的版本是"看，中华文化在海外多么坚韧"；而潜台词是，这份坚韧是顶着压力、有时正是顶着自己所属国族的压迫才赢来的。略去这道张力，故事会干净得多，却也远不诚实。

## 新市民儿童与他们离开的故乡

最安静的参与者是新市民儿童——中国内部移民的子女，从乡村来到北京边缘长大的孩子。他们的在场，重新框定了整场活动。这不仅是关于"海外的中国人"，也是关于"本土的中国人"学习把自己看作一张全球亲缘网络的一环。

这是侨务工作的国内面向。当中国的城市化把家庭连根拔起，侨联的"侨之家"项目同时也是面向进城儿童的融合计划——教他们自信、归属，以及一个大于自己户口的爱国故事。换言之，离散记忆正被重新征用为社会的黏合剂。

## 记忆作桥，记忆作工程

组织者的雄心说得很直白。马来西亚归侨联谊会会长谈到"以侨为桥、联通中外"，要从民间外交的视角讲好新时代中马友好的故事；一位合作者则介绍了服务新市民儿童的全国性公益项目。

合起来看，这场活动显出一个连贯的工程：取一段分散、甚至部分被建构的"南洋"过往，以机工的牺牲为锚，再配上马来西亚华文教育这一活生生的现实，然后同时交付给两群听众——必须保持"文化上是中国人"的离散青年，以及必须感到"国家意义上是中国人"的国内移民青年。两者正被构成为同一则爱国故事的两种主体。

对于一个志在边界之外的国家，这并不稀奇。令人惊讶的是它的精确：一座博物馆、一部电影、一座书信档案馆、一段吉隆坡的课堂证词，全被编排进一个午后关于"家国情怀"的演出。"桥"的隐喻确当——但桥，总是由那些决定两岸应在何处相接的人筑成。

## 延伸阅读

**南洋（Nanyang）**：历史上华人对东南亚移民目的地的称谓；如今常见于离散研究与文化语境。

**侨批（Qiaopi）**：华侨寄回国内、银信合一的汇兑家书，2013年入选联合国教科文组织《世界记忆名录》。

**南侨机工（Nanqiao Jigong）**：抗战时期回国的南洋华侨机工，在滇缅公路抢运物资、维系抗战生命线。

# When Beijing's Youth Walk Into "Nanyang": How China Recasts Overseas-Chinese Memory as a Bridge

On 16 September, inside the Museum of Overseas Chinese History in Beijing, a small group of children — "new-citizen" kids from a suburban community, Malaysian students studying in China, and volunteers — gathered around glass cases of letters, photographs and film props. The occasion was a service activity themed "Passing on history with a migrant heart, connecting minds along the Silk Road" (侨心传史·丝路连心), organized by the Beijing Returned Overseas Chinese Association's Malaysia fellowship. The stated aim was simple: let young people feel the "homeland affection" (家国情怀) of earlier Chinese migrants. What unfolded is a revealing snapshot of how the People's Republic treats its diaspora memory today — not as a private family keepsake, but as a public, pedagogic and diplomatic asset.

## The scene: a museum, a migration, a war

The children walked through an exhibition on overseas-Chinese history and a special display of props from the film "Letter to Grandma" (《给阿嬷的情书》) alongside a qiaopi (侨批) section — the remittance letters that once carried money and news across the South China Sea. Following the artefacts, they were led back into the harsh opening story of Chinese migration to Nanyang: the perilous sea crossings, the coolie contracts, the makeshift communities built from nothing.

Then came the war memory. A descendant of the Nanyang Volunteer Drivers and Mechanics (南侨机工) — the 3,200-odd overseas-Chinese mechanics who answered a 1939 call to ferry supplies on the Burma Road after Japan cut China's sea lanes — told the story his grandfather had lived. Over three years they moved 500,000 tons of materiel; more than 1,000 never came home. The Volunteer Drivers remain the single most quoted symbol of diaspora patriotism in official memory.

## Nanyang as founding myth

"Nanyang" — the "Southern Ocean" — is less a geography than a founding narrative. For the ethnic-Chinese communities of Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and the Philippines, the phrase compresses two centuries of departure, labour, discrimination and partial belonging. To stand in a Beijing museum and rehearse that departure is, in effect, to assert that the PRC is the rightful curator of a story that happened largely outside its borders.

Recapturing the memory of a migrant population is not unique to China. Every sending society narrates its own emigration. But the Chinese case is distinctive in scale and intent: a state with a dedicated diaspora bureaucracy — the qiaolian, or overseas-Chinese federations, reaching down to district and community level — that treats diaspora history as a living instrument of policy. The Beijing activity did exactly that: it folded migration history, wartime sacrifice and contemporary youth education into one seamless lesson.

## The Volunteer Drivers: patriotism's sharpest symbol

Why does the Volunteer Drivers story dominate? Because it resolves a perennial anxiety in diaspora politics: the suspicion that those who left are less than fully "Chinese." The 1939 homecoming converts absence into proof. These were Overseas Chinese who, in the homeland's hour of need, returned to die on a mountain road. No narrative serves the "bridge" metaphor better.

It is worth noting who told it: not a historian, but a grandson. The memory is being transmitted laterally, through families, even as the state provides the frame. That combination — vernacular kinship memory wrapped in institutional ceremony — is what gives the patriotism its stickiness.

## A classroom that survived the state

The Malaysian students present offered a second lesson, and a more uncomfortable one. One, from Tsinghua University, said his Chinese fluency came from Malaysia's Chinese-medium schools, sustained "from kindergarten." He named Lim Lian Geok (林连玉), the educator who fought to keep Chinese instruction alive and was, as punishment, stripped of his citizenship.

Here the official narrative brushes against a harder history. Malaysia's Chinese schools survived not because of the state but despite it — through community fundraising, political resistance and a willingness to be marginalised. The lesson the Beijing audience heard was "see how Chinese culture endures abroad"; the subtext is that this endurance was won against pressure, sometimes from the very nation-states the diaspora now calls home. Skipping that tension would make the story cleaner and far less honest.

## New-citizen children and the homeland they left

The quietest participants were the "new-citizen children" (新市民儿童) — the sons and daughters of China's own internal migrants, rural kids now growing up on Beijing's periphery. Their presence reframes the entire exercise. The activity was not only about Chinese abroad; it was about Chinese at home learning to see themselves as connected to a global kin network.

This is the domestic face of diaspora work. As China's urbanization uproots families, the qiaolian's "Qiaozhijia" (侨之家, "home of the overseas Chinese") projects double as integration programmes for migrant children — teaching them confidence, belonging and a patriotic story larger than their household registration. Diaspora memory, in other words, is being repurposed as social adhesive.

## Memory as bridge, memory as project

The organizers were explicit about the ambition. The fellowship's leader spoke of "using the overseas Chinese as a bridge, connecting China and the world" (以侨为桥、联通中外) and "telling the new-era China–Malaysia friendship story from the perspective of people-to-people diplomacy." A partner described a national public-welfare project serving new-citizen children.

Read together, the activity reveals a coherent project: take a dispersed, partly invented "Nanyang" past; anchor it in the Volunteer Drivers' sacrifice; pair it with the living fact of Malaysian Chinese education; and deliver it to two audiences at once — diaspora youth who must remain "culturally Chinese," and domestic migrant youth who must feel "nationally Chinese." Both are being constituted as subjects of the same patriotic story.

None of this is unusual for a state with ambitions beyond its borders. What is striking is the precision: a museum, a film, a letter archive, a classroom testimony from Kuala Lumpur, all choreographed into a single afternoon of "homeland affection." The bridge metaphor is apt — but bridges are built by those who decide where the two shores ought to meet.

## Further reading

**Nanyang (南洋)**: The historical Chinese term for Southeast Asia as a destination of migration; now often used in diaspora and cultural-studies contexts.

**Qiaopi (侨批)**: Remittance-cum-letter packages sent by overseas Chinese to families in China, inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World register in 2013.

**Nanqiao Jigong (南侨机工)**: The Nanyang Volunteer Drivers and Mechanics who returned to China during the War of Resistance to keep the Burma Road supplied.
