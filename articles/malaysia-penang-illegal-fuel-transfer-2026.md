# 槟城外海拦截燃油转运：马来西亚如何清扫马六甲"影子船队"

## 一次拦截：70万升柴油的"海上搬运"

马来西亚海事执法机构（MMEA）近期在槟城巴加阿南（Bagan Ajam）外海拦截两艘并排锚泊的油轮，涉嫌进行未经批准的船对船（STS）柴油转运。据槟城MMEA主任穆罕默德·苏菲（Muhammad Suffi Mohd Ramli）通报，涉案约70万升欧5柴油，船上柴油总储量约80万升，案值约543万令吉（约907万元人民币）；两船及22名多国船员一并被扣。

## 更大的案值：年初至今的多起重案

这并非孤例。2026年1月，MMEA在槟城以北24海里处查获两艘油轮非法STS原油转运，原油估值逾5.12亿令吉（约8.55亿元人民币），两船总价值约7.18亿令吉（约12.0亿元人民币），53名船员来自中国、缅甸、伊朗、巴基斯坦与印度。4月那桩更大：两船STS柴油转运被截，整体查扣估值高达23.3亿令吉（约38.9亿元人民币），为年内最瞩目的海上执法行动之一。

## 法规收紧：从"方便之门"到强制审批

马来西亚的强硬有其制度背景。2025年7月，外交部宣布更严格执行STS规则，新规划定：船只须事先获海事局批准方可进行STS或锚泊，并须全程开启AIS船舶识别系统。柔佛州外海长期是灰色转运的温床，新规旨在防止本国水域被用作未经授权的油品移动通道。

## 影子船队：制裁规避的海上灰色链条

为何马六甲成为全球焦点？这里水浅锚地多、毗邻近东与东亚航线，便于船只在港界外完成货权与文件"洗白"。在美伊战争引发的能源短缺与制裁扩容下，影子船队借STS模糊油品来源，已成为规避制裁的常用手段。马来西亚的拦截，因而带有维护海事主权与配合国际制裁执行的双重意味。

## 执法代价：兵力摊薄与外交张力

打击力度加大，代价同样沉重。MMEA资产本就紧张，要在漫长海岸线与繁忙航道间常态巡逻并非易事；船长个人亦面临刑责，彰显"合规即红线"的信号。与此同时，制裁施加方可能施压要求更严执法，而涉事船舶的旗国与货主模糊，又使外交层面复杂化。小国在大国博弈的海上延伸段，左右为难。

## 区域含义：马六甲治理的试金石

槟城外海的一再拦截，是东南亚海上治理的缩影。当燃油不再是单纯商品，而沦为制裁战与地缘竞争的载体，沿海国能否守住规则，将检验区域的海事执法与多边协作成色。对马来西亚而言，这既是主权宣示，也是一场旷日持久的耐力赛。

## 延伸阅读

**海上安全（Maritime Security）**：维护领海、航道与海洋权益免受非法活动威胁的综合治理，涵盖执法、护航与情报共享。

**走私（Smuggling）**：违反管制跨境转移货物或资金的行为；海上燃油走私常与制裁规避、税务漏洞交织。

**能源安全（Energy Security）**：以合理价格稳定获取能源供给的能力；区域燃油短缺会放大走私与灰色贸易的诱因。

# Malaysia foils fuel transfers off Penang: cleaning up the Strait of Malacca's 'shadow fleet'

## One interception: 700,000 litres moved at sea

Malaysia's Maritime Enforcement Agency (MMEA) recently intercepted two tankers anchored side by side off Bagan Ajam, Penang, suspected of an unauthorised ship-to-ship (STS) transfer of diesel. According to Penang MMEA director Captain Muhammad Suffi Mohd Ramli, about 700,000 litres of Euro 5 diesel were involved, with roughly 800,000 litres aboard; the fuel alone was valued at about RM5.43 million (around US$1.35 million), and the two vessels with 22 multinational crew were detained.

## Bigger hauls: a string of major busts this year

This was no isolated case. In January 2026, MMEA seized two tankers conducting illegal STS crude transfers some 24 nautical miles north of Penang; the crude was valued at over RM512 million (about US$127 million), the two ships at roughly RM718 million (around US$178 million), with 53 crew from China, Myanmar, Iran, Pakistan and India. An even larger April bust followed: two vessels' STS diesel transfer was stopped, with total seizures reaching RM2.33 billion (about US$579 million), among the year's most high-profile maritime enforcement actions.

## Tighter rules: from an open door to mandatory approval

Malaysia's tough stance has an institutional backdrop. In July 2025, the foreign ministry announced stricter STS enforcement: vessels must obtain prior Marine Department approval for any STS operation or anchoring within Malaysian waters and must keep their AIS transponders on at all times. Waters off Johor had long been a haven for grey transfers; the new rules aim to stop national waters being used as unauthorised oil-movement channels.

## Shadow fleet: the grey chain of sanctions evasion

Why has the Malacca Strait become a global flashpoint? Its shallow anchorages and proximity to Middle Eastern and East Asian routes let vessels "launder" cargo origin and documents beyond port limits. Amid energy shortages and expanded sanctions from the US–Iran war, shadow fleets use STS to obscure oil provenance — a common sanctions-evasion tactic. Malaysia's interceptions thus carry both a maritime-sovereignty and a sanctions-enforcement undertone.

## The cost of enforcement: stretched assets and diplomatic strain

Greater enforcement comes at a heavy price. MMEA assets are already thin; sustained patrols along a long coastline and busy lanes are no easy task, and ship captains now face personal criminal liability, signalling that compliance is a red line. Meanwhile, sanctioning powers may pressure for stricter action, while the unclear flag states and owners of involved vessels complicate the diplomatic picture. A small state on the maritime extension of great-power rivalry is caught in the middle.

## Regional meaning: a touchstone for Malacca governance

The repeated interceptions off Penang are a microcosm of Southeast Asian maritime governance. When fuel ceases to be a mere commodity and becomes a vehicle for sanctions wars and geopolitical competition, a coastal state's ability to uphold the rules tests the region's enforcement and multilateral cooperation. For Malaysia, this is both a sovereign statement and a protracted endurance race.

## Further reading

**Maritime Security**: The composite governance of protecting territorial waters, sea lanes and maritime rights from unlawful threats, spanning enforcement, escort and intelligence sharing.

**Smuggling**: The cross-border movement of goods or funds in violation of controls; maritime fuel smuggling often intertwines with sanctions evasion and tax loopholes.

**Energy Security**: The ability to obtain energy supply stably at reasonable prices; regional fuel shortages amplify the incentives for smuggling and grey trade.
