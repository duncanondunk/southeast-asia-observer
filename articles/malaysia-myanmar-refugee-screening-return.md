# 第三国的门关上之后：马来西亚为四千缅甸人启动"遣返式筛查"

2026年8月9日，马来西亚内政部长赛夫丁在为移民局车队巡游主持挥旗礼后，向记者说了一句很直白的话：随着全球地缘政治格局变化，滞留马来西亚的难民经联合国难民署（UNHCR）安置到美国等第三国的机会已经"非常渺茫"。据联合早报援引《中国报》报道，正因如此，政府开始积极推进难民身份筛查，为遣返原籍国铺路。首批目标，是目前关押在移民局扣留营内、人数超过4000名的缅甸公民。

这条新闻很容易被读成一次例行的执法通报。但它其实是一个信号：在国际保护体系收缩之后，过境国正在把"等待安置"的模式，换成"组织遣返"的模式。

## 一句"非常渺茫"背后的算术

赛夫丁的判断不是外交辞令，是算术结果。

根据联合国难民署的数据，截至2026年2月底，在马来西亚登记的难民与寻求庇护者约21.56万人，其中来自缅甸的约19.38万人——包括约12.61万罗兴亚人、1.58万钦族，以及3.3万来自缅甸其他冲突地区的族群；余下约2.18万人来自50多个国家。马来西亚政府自己在今年7月的国会书面答复中给出的口径略高：截至4月共21.9472万人登记，含15.2979万名难民与6.6493万名寻求庇护者，但政府特别声明该数据由难民署单方审核，未必反映实况。

关键的对照数字在另一份文件里：难民署马来西亚办事处的进度报告显示，2025年全年完成安置或遣返的难民为2585人。用它去除20万人的存量，一年消化约1.2%。按这个速度，把现有登记人口清空需要约80年——而这还没算每年新到的人。

再看资源。难民署为在马来西亚的行动申请的年度经费约2180万美元（约1.47亿元人民币），摊到20万人身上，人均一年约100美元（约合675元人民币）。这个数字本身就说明，所谓"国际保护"在马来西亚从来不是一个财政上认真的方案。

美国等传统安置国近年大幅收紧配额之后，这条本就细窄的管道进一步收缩。当出口关闭，压力必然回流到入口国。赛夫丁的"非常渺茫"，说的是这件事。

## 从扣留营开始筛查，意味着什么

值得注意的是筛查的起点：移民局与警方联合行动的首批对象，是关在扣留营里的4000多名缅甸人；此后才扩大到营外群体，政府将建立主动接触机制，呼吁他们现身登记，预计今年第四季度可得出初步数据。

从行政效率看，这个选择完全合理——被扣留者位置固定、身份可核、程序可控。但从保护标准看，这恰恰是最需要警惕的起点。

原因有三。其一，被关押者是举证能力最弱的一群：证件在偷渡途中遗失、语言不通、没有律师、与外界联系被切断，要他们证明自己"具备难民身分"，先天不利。其二，长期羁押本身构成压力，会让"自愿返回"的意思表示失去可靠性——一个人在扣留营待够久，签任何字都可能是为了离开那个地方，而不是为了回到那个国家。其三，马来西亚移民扣留营的健康与死亡问题多年来反复被提起，制度性透明度不足，外部监督有限。

所以真正要问的不是"要不要筛查"，而是三个技术问题：筛查标准是否公开？有没有复核与申诉通道？难民署是否被允许介入个案？这三条如果都缺席，"身份筛查"与"遣返前置程序"之间就没有实质区别。

## "别只说罗兴亚人"：一次正确但危险的正名

赛夫丁在同一场合还做了一次术语纠偏。他强调，马来西亚境内的缅甸难民涵盖多达19个族群，绝不只有罗兴亚人，还有钦族、阿拉干族等，呼吁民众与媒体改用"缅甸难民"的统称，避免以偏概全；他同时提醒，马来西亚也收容来自阿富汗、伊拉克、约旦等国的难民。

作为人口描述，这话完全站得住。难民署的分类里，缅甸籍登记者中罗兴亚人约占六成五，其余三成五确实来自钦邦、若开邦及其他冲突地区，长期被公共讨论忽略。在马来西亚国内，"罗兴亚"三个字已经高度政治化，与治安、就业、福利争议捆绑在一起；把叙事拉回"缅甸难民"这个中性框架，客观上有降温作用。

但这次正名有一个不能回避的副作用：它把最难的那个案子，溶解进了相对容易的案子里。

其他缅甸族群在法律上是缅甸公民，"遣返原籍国"至少在概念上成立。罗兴亚人不是。依据缅甸1982年公民法，罗兴亚人被排除在承认的民族名单之外，绝大多数不具备缅甸公民身份，是国际上规模最大的无国籍群体之一。对一个原籍国拒绝承认其为国民的人来说，"遣返原籍国"在法律上不是一个可以直接执行的动作。用统称抹平族群差异，行政上省事，法律上却把最硬的那块骨头藏了起来。

## 缅甸愿意接收五千人，然后呢

报道提到，缅甸方面早前承诺愿意接收约5000名滞留马来西亚的缅籍人员，这正是马方启动大规模登记筛查的直接触发点。

问题在于，一纸承诺离一次合规的遣返还有很远。国际公认的返回标准是三条：自愿、安全、有尊严。承诺只回应了第四条不存在的标准——接收方同意。它没有回答：返回后住在哪里、有没有身份文件、会不会被征兵或再次流离；也没有回答，谁来核实这5000人确实回到了各自的家乡，而不是另一处集中安置点。

更根本的是接收方本身的处境。缅甸自2021年以来陷入持续内战，实控范围分裂，人道准入受限。同一时期，缅甸产生的难民与寻求庇护者遍布周边：据难民署数据，截至2026年6月底，孟加拉国约120万、马来西亚约20.08万、泰国约13.66万、印度约8.73万、印尼约2600人。把人送回一个仍在制造难民的国家，这个逻辑本身就需要极强的证据支撑。

难民署另有一项统计不该被忽略：2025年是罗兴亚人海上迁移有记录以来死亡人数最多的一年。这说明推力仍在增强。在推力不减的情况下强化遣返，很可能不是减少了流动，而是把流动推向更危险的路径。

## 法律真空里的行政创新

马来西亚不是1951年《关于难民地位的公约》及其1967年议定书的缔约国，国内也没有难民法。长期以来，难民身份甄别（RSD）由难民署单方进行，政府不参与、不承认其法律效力，难民在马来西亚没有合法工作权，孩子进不了公立学校，医疗按外国人计费——这是一套"平行系统"：国际机构做认定，主权国家不背书。

现在马来西亚政府要自己做筛查，这在法律上是个新东西，而且是双刃的。

往好处看，它可能是国家庇护制度的胚胎。难民署本身也观察到，马来西亚政府关于建立国家庇护框架的讨论正在升温，存在从"难民署主导的平行系统"向"国家主导"过渡的机会窗口。国家愿意亲自甄别身份，逻辑上就得给出标准、程序和救济渠道，这本身是制度化的第一步。

往坏处看，它也可能只是给驱逐加装一道程序外壳。判断的关键在于筛查结果的用途：如果通过认定的人获得的是合法居留与工作权，那是庇护制度；如果通过认定的人被优先排入遣返序列——就像报道中"一旦确认难民身分，这批人士将成为首批被安排遣返原籍国的群体"这句话所暗示的——那么身份认定的功能就被倒置了：确认你是难民，成了送你走的理由，而不是留你下的理由。

需要指出，即便不是《难民公约》缔约国，不驱回原则（non-refoulement）在国际法上被广泛视为习惯法规则，对非缔约国同样具有约束力。这是这次行动最需要接受检验的一条底线。

## 一个克制的判断

把这件事只当成马来西亚一国的政策转向，会低估它的含义。

过去十余年，马来西亚一直是东盟内部在罗兴亚议题上声音最响的国家之一，多次在区域场合施压缅甸。从道义倡议转向组织遣返，落差不小。但这个落差主要不是由吉隆坡制造的：当富裕国家的安置配额被大幅削减、区域内又始终没有任何责任分担机制、东盟"五点共识"停留在纸面，最终承压的必然是过境国，而过境国的国内政治承受不了长期无解的存量。

所以真正的问题不在马来西亚，而在整个保护体系退潮之后，谁来补位。答案目前是：没有人。

接下来值得盯住三个节点：今年第四季度的初步登记数据是否公布、筛查标准与申诉机制是否成文、以及难民署能否参与个案复核。如果这三项都落空，那么无论用什么措辞包装，"筛查"这个词都会失去它应有的含义。

## 延伸阅读

**不驱回原则（non-refoulement）**：禁止将任何人遣返至其生命或自由可能受威胁的地区。该原则被广泛认为已构成国际习惯法，对未加入1951年《难民地位公约》的国家同样具有约束力。

**难民身份甄别（Refugee Status Determination, RSD）**：判定申请人是否符合难民定义的法律与行政程序。在马来西亚，这项工作长期由联合国难民署单方执行，政府不予法律承认。

**缅甸1982年公民法（Myanmar Citizenship Law 1982）**：将公民身份与政府认可的"土著民族"名单挂钩的法律，罗兴亚人被排除在外，由此形成全球规模最大的无国籍群体之一。

# When the Third-Country Door Closes: Malaysia Starts "Screening for Return" on 4,000 Myanmar Nationals

On 9 August 2026, after flagging off an Immigration Department convoy, Malaysian Home Minister Saifuddin Nasution Ismail told reporters something unusually blunt: as the global geopolitical landscape shifts, the chances of refugees stranded in Malaysia being resettled by UNHCR to third countries such as the United States have become "very slim." According to Lianhe Zaobao, citing China Press, this is why the government is now actively pushing refugee status screening to lay the groundwork for return to countries of origin. The first target group is the more than 4,000 Myanmar nationals currently held in immigration detention depots.

It would be easy to read this as a routine enforcement briefing. It is better read as a signal: with the international protection system contracting, transit states are swapping a model of waiting for resettlement for a model of organising return.

## The arithmetic behind "very slim"

Saifuddin's assessment is not diplomatic phrasing. It is arithmetic.

UNHCR figures show that as of end-February 2026, around 215,600 refugees and asylum-seekers were registered in Malaysia, of whom some 193,824 were from Myanmar — including roughly 126,144 Rohingya, 15,774 Chin, and 33,002 from other conflict-affected communities. The remaining 21,776 came from more than 50 countries. The Malaysian government's own parliamentary written reply in July put the number slightly higher: 219,472 registered as of April, comprising 152,979 refugees and 66,493 asylum-seekers — while stressing that the data is verified solely by UNHCR and may not reflect actual conditions.

The decisive comparison sits in another document. UNHCR Malaysia's progress reporting shows that 2,585 refugees were resettled or repatriated during the whole of 2025. Set against a stock above 200,000, that clears about 1.2 per cent a year. At that pace, emptying the current caseload would take roughly eighty years — before counting new arrivals.

Then there is money. UNHCR's annual funding requirement for its Malaysia operation is about US$21.8 million. Spread across more than 200,000 people, that is roughly US$100 per person per year. The figure alone tells you that "international protection" in Malaysia has never been a fiscally serious proposition.

With traditional resettlement countries, the United States foremost among them, sharply tightening quotas in recent years, an already narrow pipe has narrowed further. When the exit closes, pressure flows back to the country of first arrival. That is what "very slim" describes.

## What it means to start screening inside the depots

Note where the screening begins. The joint Immigration–police operation targets first the 4,000-plus Myanmar nationals held in detention depots; only later will it extend to those outside, with the government building an outreach mechanism urging people to come forward and register, and preliminary data expected in the fourth quarter of this year.

Administratively, this is entirely rational — detainees are stationary, identifiable, and procedurally manageable. Judged against protection standards, it is precisely the starting point that warrants caution.

Three reasons. First, detainees are the least able to produce evidence: documents lost en route, no shared language, no lawyer, contact with the outside severed. Asking them to prove they hold refugee status stacks the deck against them. Second, prolonged detention is itself coercive, and it corrodes the reliability of any expression of "voluntary" return — held long enough, a person may sign anything to leave that place, not to reach that country. Third, health and mortality conditions in Malaysian immigration depots have been raised repeatedly over the years, with limited transparency and thin external oversight.

The question worth asking is therefore not whether to screen, but three technical ones. Are the criteria published? Is there review and appeal? Is UNHCR permitted to intervene in individual cases? If all three are absent, there is no substantive difference between "status screening" and a pre-deportation procedure.

## "Don't just say Rohingya": a correction that is right and risky

At the same event, Saifuddin issued a terminological correction. Myanmar refugees in Malaysia span as many as 19 ethnic groups, he stressed, not only Rohingya but also Chin, Rakhine and others; he urged the public and media to use the umbrella term "Myanmar refugees" to avoid mistaking a part for the whole, and noted that Malaysia also hosts refugees from Afghanistan, Iraq and Jordan.

As demography, this holds up. In UNHCR's breakdown, Rohingya account for roughly 65 per cent of registered Myanmar nationals; the remaining 35 per cent come from Chin State, Rakhine State and other conflict areas, and have long been absent from public debate. Inside Malaysia the word "Rohingya" has become heavily politicised, bundled with disputes over crime, jobs and welfare. Pulling the narrative back to the neutral frame of "Myanmar refugees" does have a cooling effect.

But the correction carries a side effect that cannot be waved away: it dissolves the hardest case into the easier ones.

Other Myanmar ethnic groups are, legally, Myanmar citizens; "return to country of origin" is at least conceptually coherent for them. For the Rohingya it is not. Under Myanmar's 1982 Citizenship Law, the Rohingya are excluded from the list of recognised national races, and the overwhelming majority hold no Myanmar citizenship — making them one of the largest stateless populations in the world. For someone whose country of origin refuses to acknowledge them as nationals, "repatriation to the country of origin" is not a legally executable act. Flattening ethnic distinctions under an umbrella term saves administrative trouble while hiding the hardest bone in the dish.

## Myanmar will take 5,000 — and then?

The reporting notes that Myanmar earlier pledged to accept around 5,000 of its nationals stranded in Malaysia, and that this pledge is the direct trigger for the large-scale registration drive.

The distance between a pledge and a lawful return remains long. The accepted standard for return has three limbs: voluntary, safe, dignified. A pledge answers only a fourth criterion that does not exist — that the receiving side agrees. It says nothing about where returnees will live, whether they will hold identity documents, whether they face conscription or renewed displacement; nor about who verifies that the 5,000 actually reach their home areas rather than another holding site.

More fundamentally, there is the condition of the receiving state. Myanmar has been in sustained civil war since 2021, with fragmented territorial control and constrained humanitarian access. Over the same period, Myanmar-origin refugees and asylum-seekers have spread across the region: as of end-June 2026, UNHCR counted roughly 1.2 million in Bangladesh, 200,800 in Malaysia, 136,600 in Thailand, 87,300 in India and 2,600 in Indonesia. Returning people to a country still producing refugees is a proposition that demands an unusually heavy burden of proof.

One further UNHCR statistic should not be skipped: 2025 was the deadliest year on record for Rohingya maritime movements. The push factors are strengthening, not easing. Intensifying return while push factors hold is less likely to reduce movement than to divert it onto more dangerous routes.

## Administrative invention inside a legal vacuum

Malaysia is not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention or its 1967 Protocol, and has no domestic asylum law. Refugee status determination (RSD) has long been carried out unilaterally by UNHCR, with the government neither participating in nor recognising its legal effect. Refugees have no right to work, their children cannot enrol in public schools, and healthcare is billed at foreigner rates. It is a parallel system: an international agency does the recognising, the sovereign state declines to endorse it.

That the Malaysian state now intends to screen on its own account is legally novel — and cuts both ways.

Read charitably, it could be the embryo of a national asylum regime. UNHCR itself observes that government discussion of a national asylum framework has gained momentum, opening a window to transition from a UNHCR-led parallel system to a nationally led one. A state willing to determine status must, logically, produce criteria, procedure and remedies. That is the first step of institutionalisation.

Read sceptically, it may simply be deportation with a procedural shell. Everything turns on what the screening result is used for. If those recognised obtain lawful stay and the right to work, this is an asylum system. If those recognised are moved to the front of the removal queue — as the reported line "once refugee status is confirmed, this group will be the first arranged for return to their country of origin" suggests — then the function of status determination has been inverted: being recognised as a refugee becomes the reason to send you away rather than the reason to let you stay.

It bears noting that even for a non-signatory, the principle of non-refoulement is widely regarded as a rule of customary international law and binds non-parties as well. That is the floor against which this operation should be measured.

## A restrained judgment

Treating this as a Malaysian policy turn alone understates what it means.

For more than a decade Malaysia has been among the loudest ASEAN voices on Rohingya rights, repeatedly pressing Myanmar in regional forums. The gap between that advocacy and organised repatriation is wide. But the gap was not principally manufactured in Kuala Lumpur. When wealthy states cut resettlement quotas sharply, when the region has never built any burden-sharing mechanism, and when ASEAN's Five-Point Consensus remains a piece of paper, the pressure necessarily lands on transit states — and transit-state domestic politics cannot absorb an unresolved caseload indefinitely.

The real question, then, is not about Malaysia but about who fills the gap once the protection system recedes. So far the answer is: nobody.

Three checkpoints are worth watching. Whether the fourth-quarter preliminary registration data is published. Whether screening criteria and appeal mechanisms are written down. And whether UNHCR is allowed into case review. If all three fail, then whatever wrapping is used, the word "screening" will have lost its meaning.

## Further reading

**Non-refoulement** — The prohibition on returning any person to a place where their life or freedom would be at risk. Widely regarded as a rule of customary international law, it binds states that have not acceded to the 1951 Refugee Convention.

**Refugee Status Determination (RSD)** — The legal and administrative process of deciding whether an applicant meets the refugee definition. In Malaysia this has long been performed unilaterally by UNHCR without domestic legal recognition.

**Myanmar Citizenship Law 1982** — Legislation tying citizenship to a state-recognised list of "national races," from which the Rohingya are excluded, producing one of the world's largest stateless populations.
