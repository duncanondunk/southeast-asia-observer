# 烟霾未散又断气：印尼与新加坡的这场外交摩擦，揭开了东盟能源互赖的软肋

今年东南亚（Southeast Asia）的旱季格外漫长。据多方报道，印尼（Indonesia）苏门答腊与加里曼丹的林火是十一年来最猛的一季，强厄尔尼诺叠加干旱，泥炭地阴燃难灭，烟霾一路飘过马六甲，把新加坡、马来西亚（Malaysia）乃至东帝汶的天空染成褐黄。这原本是年年上演的老剧本，但这一次，剧本的走向变了——火还没灭，气先断了。

## 一场林火，烧出了两国的旧伤

印尼的林火从来不只是环境灾害。监测平台「努山塔拉（Nusantara）地图集」的数据显示，2026年前9个月，印尼被烧毁的森林与土地超过190万公顷。烟霾跨境，首当其冲的却是印尼自己人：呼吸道疾病在多地激增，学校停课，民众户外活动受限。

但邻国同样忍不了。新加坡地方不大，自己没有烧山，却要吸别人家的烟。这种「我烧我的、你吸你的」的错位，正是跨境烟霾治理几十年无解的根源——火在印尼境内，利润与责任却跨境流动。

## 烟霾跨境，谁该为明火负责

据《日经亚洲》（Nikkei Asia）等媒体报道，新加坡自10月16日起，先后向印尼境内7家、19日再增3家与林火相关的企业发出防火措施通知；23日更进一步，要求10家在新加坡注册、却在印尼拥有种植园或林业业务的企业提交材料。新加坡的逻辑很清楚：火虽然烧在印尼，但点燃它的企业，有不少注册在新加坡、股东来自四面八方。

这触及了东南亚治理最敏感的一根神经——域外追责。印尼方面显然不舒服：火灾发生在自己家里，新加坡却动用本国法律跨海查企业，甚至可能开罚。普拉博沃（Prabowo Subianto）总统此前公开抨击一些「喜欢侮辱印尼」的国家，虽未点名，话里话外指向分明。讽刺的是，印尼民众才是烟霾的第一批受害者，可外交话语却先把矛头对准了「外人」。

## 从环境纠纷到能源博弈

真正让局势升级的，是能源。10月22日，印尼能源与矿产资源部长巴赫利尔放话，将审视那些「不尊重印尼」国家的合同；24日，印尼上游油气监管机构宣布，即日起每天削减对新加坡的管道天然气供应量约3100万标准立方英尺，约占输新天然气总量的8%，理由是为优先保障国内电厂与下游产业需求。

更耐人寻味的是选择性：印尼方面强调，这次减供「只针对新加坡，其他国家不受影响」。一句话，把环境摩擦与经济制裁悄悄绑在了一起。气虽不多，信号却很足——这是一次精准的、点到为止的警告。

## 8%的减供，为何让新加坡紧张

为什么区区8%的减供，会让以淡定著称的新加坡如此紧张？答案藏在它的能源结构里。新加坡约95%的电力来自天然气发电，其中近一半（有说43%）通过海底管道从马来西亚和印尼输入，其余靠全球采购的液化天然气。联合国商品贸易数据库显示，2025年新加坡从印尼进口管道天然气约330万吨，占其管道天然气进口总量的约65%。

换句话说，印尼是新加坡能源版图上绕不开的邻居。8%的减供实际只占新加坡每日天然气总需求的不到2%，短期内无虞；但把它放在「合同2028年底到期」这个时间窗口下看，意味就完全不同了。

## 合同神圣，还是资源民族主义的试探

双方的隔空交锋，很快变成了「谁更守合同」的口水仗。新加坡总理黄循财10月25日表态，天然气供应受商业合同约束，期望各方「履行承诺」；新加坡进口商将采取必要行动维护权益。印尼能源部长巴赫利尔随即回呛：「我国是一个守合同、守规则的国家，所以别来教印尼怎么尊重合同。」

表面看是气量的多寡，内里是资源民族主义的升温。东盟（ASEAN）秘书长高金洪在新加坡国际能源周上点破了一层窗户纸：如果印尼频繁把能源合同与外交摩擦挂钩，会削弱国际投资者对东盟跨境合约稳定性的信心。摩根士丹利也判断，从马来西亚或现货液化天然气补缺口可以，但会推高2027年前的用气成本。

## 黄循财的「早有准备」说了什么

黄循财那句「我们早有准备」，不是场面话。新加坡早已成立中央燃气机构，投建液化天然气接收站，随时可从全球其他来源补位；他还顺势抛出生物甲烷试验与谨慎评估核能的长期选项。这恰恰暴露了小国生存的哲学：把命脉系在单一邻国的管道上，终归是睡不安稳的。

但「准备」也有限度。伍国潘迪（Rystad Energy亚太油气主管）就指出，真正的考验在2028年——现有合同到期后，印尼是否还会以今天的条款续约，谁也不敢打包票。能源自主不是一句口号，对新加坡这样的城市国家尤其如此。

## 东盟秘书长的担忧不是多余的

高金洪的忧虑，指向一个更宏大的命题：东盟电网。这个筹划多年的区域能源互联计划，本想把成员国的水电、气电、可再生能源并网，让电力像商品一样跨境流动。可一旦能源被当成外交筹码，互联互通的故事就讲不下去了。

更现实的是投资信心。印尼是东盟最大经济体，若它动辄把商业合约与政治情绪挂钩，外资会怎么想？淡马锡与新加坡政府投资公司（GIC）近期就不得不出面，驳斥印尼前官员「施压明晟（MSCI）下调印尼股市评级」的指控——能源、金融、舆论，几条战线同时起火。

## 一场「茶杯里的风暴」？

有分析把这场摩擦形容为「茶杯里的风暴」。毕竟，两国外长10月27日已在雅加达（Jakarta）闭门会谈，同意设立联合工作组，共享情报、追查涉林火企业的资金流向；双方还开通部长级热线。新加坡是印尼最大的外资来源国，印尼则依赖经新加坡的燃料进口与全球互联网光缆。经贸与防务的基本盘深厚，全面对抗谁都承受不起。

但「茶杯」之说也轻巧了些。普拉博沃政府上台以来资源民族主义抬头，国内又因林火、免费午餐计划的食物中毒与腐败指控而承压，民调支持率从去年底的81.2%跌至本月的44.5%。对外示强，往往是向内转移焦点的老办法。

## 烟霾散去之前，能源互赖的考题先来了

说到底，这场风波是一堂公开课：在东南亚，环境灾害与能源安全、外交博弈从来不是平行线。一次林火，能让两个关系稳固的邻国在几天内从合作走到对峙；8%的减供，撬动的是整个区域对「互赖是否可靠」的重新计算。

烟霾终会随雨季散去，但能源互赖的考题不会。对新加坡，是继续把鸡蛋放在邻国的管道里，还是加速多元化、把赌注押在液化天然气、核能甚至区域电网；对印尼，是守住在资源民族主义与合约信用之间的平衡。至于东盟，它的「中心性」与「团结」，正一次次被最朴素的烟雾与气流检验着成色。

## 延伸阅读

**跨境烟霾（Transboundary Haze）**：指一国境内焚烧产生的烟霾飘散至邻国、造成跨境空气污染的现象；东南亚多见于印尼苏门答腊、加里曼丹的烧荒与泥炭地火，治理受制于东盟「不干涉内政」原则与缺乏域外追责机制。

**资源民族主义（Resource Nationalism）**：指一国将自然资源视为国家主权象征、倾向于以政策手段优先保障国内供应或收回资源控制权的倾向；在能源与矿产领域常表现为出口限制、合同重审与国有化压力。

**东盟电网（ASEAN Power Grid）**：东盟自2000年代推动的区域能源互联倡议，旨在通过跨国输电线路实现成员国电力互济，被视为提升能源安全与消纳可再生能源的关键基础设施，但其推进一直受主权与商业顾虑掣肘。

# Haze Unsettled, Gas Cut: How the Indonesia–Singapore Spat Exposes ASEAN's Fragile Energy Interdependence

This year's dry season across Southeast Asia has been unusually long. According to multiple reports, the forest and land fires in Indonesia's Sumatra and Kalimantan are the most intense in 11 years. A strong El Niño, compounded by drought, and smouldering peatlands that are hard to extinguish sent haze drifting across the Malacca Strait, staining the skies of Singapore, Malaysia and even Timor-Leste a brownish yellow. This is an old script replayed year after year — but this time the plot turned: before the fires were out, the gas was cut.

## The fires that reopened old wounds

Indonesia's forest fires have never been merely an environmental disaster. Data from the Nusantara Atlas monitoring platform shows that in the first nine months of 2026, more than 1.9 million hectares of Indonesian forest and land were consumed by fire. The haze crosses borders, but its first victims are Indonesians themselves: respiratory illnesses spiked across many regions, schools closed, and outdoor activity was restricted.

Yet neighbours could not stomach it either. Singapore is small; it did not set the forests alight, yet it must breathe the smoke of others. This mismatch — "I burn mine, you inhale yours" — is precisely why transboundary haze governance has remained unsolved for decades: the fire is on Indonesian soil, but profit and responsibility flow across borders.

## Who is to blame for the cross-border smoke

According to Nikkei Asia and other media, starting on 16 October Singapore issued fire-prevention notices to seven companies in Indonesia linked to the fires, adding three more on 19 October; on 23 October it went further, demanding that ten Singapore-registered companies with plantation or forestry operations in Indonesia submit information. Singapore's logic is clear: the fire may burn in Indonesia, but many of the firms that ignited it are registered in Singapore, with shareholders from everywhere.

This touches the most sensitive nerve of Southeast Asian governance — extraterritorial accountability. Indonesia was visibly uncomfortable: the fire is in its own house, yet Singapore invoked its own law to investigate companies across the sea, perhaps even imposing penalties. President Prabowo Subianto had earlier publicly lashed out at countries that "like to insult" Indonesia — unnamed, but the target was hardly ambiguous. The irony is that Indonesians are the haze's first victims, yet the diplomatic narrative pointed the finger outward first.

## From an environmental row to an energy standoff

What truly escalated the situation was energy. On 22 October, Indonesian Energy and Mineral Resources Minister Bahlil Lahadalia warned that contracts with countries that "do not respect Indonesia" would be reviewed. On 24 October, Indonesia's upstream oil and gas regulator announced it would cut pipelined gas supplies to Singapore by about 31 million standard cubic feet per day — roughly 8% of total piped gas exports to the city-state — citing the need to prioritise domestic power plants and downstream industries.

What is more telling is the selectivity: Indonesian officials stressed that this reduction "affects only Singapore, not other countries." In one sentence, environmental friction and economic leverage were quietly tied together. The volume is small, but the signal is loud — a precise, measured warning.

## Why an 8% cut rattled Singapore

Why did a mere 8% reduction unsettle the usually unflappable Singapore? The answer lies in its energy structure. About 95% of Singapore's electricity comes from natural gas, nearly half of it (some say 43%) piped from Malaysia and Indonesia via subsea pipelines, the rest imported as liquefied natural gas (LNG). UN Comtrade data shows that in 2025 Singapore imported about 3.3 million tonnes of piped gas from Indonesia, around 65% of its total piped-gas imports.

In other words, Indonesia is a neighbour Singapore's energy map cannot do without. The 8% cut actually accounts for less than 2% of Singapore's daily gas demand and poses no short-term risk; but placed against the timeline — the existing contracts expire at the end of 2028 — the meaning changes entirely.

## Sacred contracts, or a probe of resource nationalism

The exchange quickly became a war of words over who honours contracts better. On 25 October, Singapore's Prime Minister Lawrence Wong said gas supply is governed by commercial contracts and he expects parties to "honour their commitments"; Singapore's importers will take necessary action to protect their rights. Indonesia's energy minister Bahlil shot back: "Our nation is a nation that honours contracts and rules. So don't lecture Indonesia on how to honour contracts."

On the surface it is about volume; underneath, it is the rise of resource nationalism. At the Singapore International Energy Week, ASEAN Secretary-General Kao Kim Hourn broke a layer of window paper: if Indonesia frequently ties energy contracts to diplomatic friction, it will erode international investors' confidence in the stability of ASEAN cross-border agreements. Morgan Stanley also judged that making up the gap from Malaysia or spot LNG is possible, but would push up gas costs before 2027.

## What Lawrence Wong's "we were prepared" really means

Wong's line that "we were prepared" is not mere pleasantry. Singapore long ago established a central gas entity and built LNG receiving terminals, ready to source from anywhere in the world; he also floated longer-term options of biomethane trials and a cautious assessment of nuclear power. This exposes, in miniature, the survival philosophy of a small state: tying one's lifeblood to a single neighbour's pipeline is, in the end, a restless sleep.

But "preparedness" has its limits. Prateek Pandey, Rystad Energy's head of Asia-Pacific oil and gas, pointed out that the real test comes in 2028 — when existing contracts expire, no one can guarantee Indonesia will renew on today's terms. Energy autonomy is not a slogan, especially not for a city-state.

## The ASEAN secretary-general's worry is not unfounded

Kao's concern points to a grander proposition: the ASEAN Power Grid. This long-planned regional energy interconnection scheme was meant to link member states' hydro, gas and renewable power so electricity could flow across borders like a commodity. But once energy is wielded as a diplomatic chip, the interoperability story falls apart.

More immediately, there is investment confidence. Indonesia is ASEAN's largest economy; if it casually links commercial contracts to political mood, what will foreign capital think? Temasek and the sovereign wealth fund GIC recently had to step forward to reject allegations by a former Indonesian official that they pressured index provider MSCI to downgrade Indonesia's equities — energy, finance and public opinion, several fronts aflame at once.

## A storm in a teacup?

Some analysts call this friction a "storm in a teacup." After all, the two foreign ministers met behind closed doors in Jakarta on 27 October and agreed to set up a joint task force to share intelligence and trace the money trail of companies linked to the fires; they also opened a ministerial hotline. Singapore is Indonesia's largest source of foreign direct investment at US$17.4 billion last year, while Indonesia relies on fuel imports and global internet cables through Singapore. The economic, trade and defence fundamentals run deep; no one can afford an all-out confrontation.

But the "teacup" reading is also a little too light. Since Prabowo took office, resource nationalism has risen; at home the government is pressed by fires, a food-poisoning scandal in its signature free-lunch programme, and corruption allegations, with his approval rating sliding from 81.2% late last year to 44.5% this month. Projecting strength outward is an old method of diverting attention inward.

## Before the haze clears, the test of interdependence arrives

In the final analysis, this episode is a public lesson: in Southeast Asia, environmental disaster, energy security and diplomatic games have never been parallel lines. A single forest fire can push two stable neighbours from cooperation to confrontation in days; an 8% cut pries open a region-wide recalculation of whether "interdependence" can be trusted.

The haze will eventually disperse with the monsoon, but the test of energy interdependence will not. For Singapore, the question is whether to keep its eggs in a neighbour's pipeline or accelerate diversification — betting on LNG, nuclear power and even the regional grid. For Indonesia, it is holding the balance between resource nationalism and contractual credibility. As for ASEAN, its "centrality" and "solidarity" are, again and again, tested for their true colour by the plainest smoke and air currents.

## Further reading

**Transboundary Haze**: Smoke from fires within one country that drifts across borders and causes transboundary air pollution; in Southeast Asia it is most common from slash-and-burn and peatland fires in Sumatra and Kalimantan, and its governance is constrained by ASEAN's non-interference principle and the lack of an extraterritorial accountability mechanism.

**Resource Nationalism**: The tendency of a state to treat natural resources as symbols of sovereignty and to use policy to prioritise domestic supply or reclaim control; in energy and minerals it often appears as export restrictions, contract reviews and nationalisation pressure.

**ASEAN Power Grid**: A regional energy-interconnection initiative promoted by ASEAN since the 2000s, intended to realise cross-border electricity sharing through transnational transmission lines; seen as key infrastructure for energy security and renewable absorption, yet long held back by sovereignty and commercial concerns.
