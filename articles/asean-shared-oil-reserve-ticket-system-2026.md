# 票券替代油罐？东盟共享石油储备的日本剧本

## 从概念到票券：东盟第一次把储备摆上签字桌

11月12日，据日经亚洲报道，东盟成员国将最早于2027年建立石油产品在紧急情况下的共享机制。储存设施落在印度尼西亚和泰国，主打柴油与汽油；应急时，菲律宾可以从中采购。11月14日，在第49届东盟峰会（11月15日至18日在马尼拉举行）开幕前的商务与投资峰会上，菲律宾国家石油公司旗下企业将与印尼油罐运营商、泰国储油企业签署意向书。

值得点评的是，这不是又一份原则性宣言。它给出了时间、地点、买方和工具——2027年、印尼与泰国、菲律宾、票券制。东盟的区域能源合作向来擅长开会、短于落地，这次至少把「谁签、在哪存、怎么用」摆到了台面上。从概念到合同，这一步比喊口号实在。

## 数字：15天与14天的家底

支撑这套机制的是一份不好看的账单。据东亚—东盟经济研究中心（ERIA）调查，菲律宾的石油储备仅相当于15至30天需求量，印度尼西亚更少，只有14至23天。另一组口径（日本资源能源厅按原油及油品计算）给出泰国61天、菲律宾53天、越南30天——统计方法不同，数字差了一大截，但方向一致：东南亚的缓冲垫薄得可怜。

更要紧的是外部依赖。据日经亚洲与区域媒体转引，途经霍尔木兹海峡的原油和石油产品约80%流向亚洲，菲律宾进口原油的90%以上来自中东。今年2月中东冲突爆发后，缺乏储备的东盟国家立刻感受到了供应不确定性。储备不足不是假设题，而是刚刚考过的现实题。

## 票券制：把应急供油变成一份预付的权利

这套机制的核心不是新建油罐，而是引入票券制：参与国或企业预先支付费用，取得在紧急情况下优先购买事先约定数量的柴油、汽油的权利。平时，储油罐运营商照常卖油；一旦出现供应中断，持票方可以按事先约定的数量优先提货。欧盟和其他地区已有类似做法，但在东盟是头一遭。

说穿了，这是把安全需求金融化：不必在每个国家都堆满油罐，而是给现有的商业库存装上一层「优先通行权」。思路聪明，成本也聪明——它把建罐的资本开支变成了买保险的费用。但保险单能否兑现，取决于条款：什么叫「紧急情况」？谁有权宣布启动？票价怎么定？目前这些细节一概未定。权利写在纸上，义务落在谁头上，还是未知数。

## 菲律宾为何第一个签字

菲律宾几乎是全套条件里最急的那一个。这个国家几乎所有能源需求都依赖进口，自身储备仅够15至30天，过去数年多次公开表示要建石油储备。这次它既是2026年东盟轮值主席国、峰会东道主，又是第一个买家，政治时机与实际需求刚好重叠。

但签字不等于拿到油。票券只保证「优先」，不保证「有货」。印尼自己的储备也只有14至23天，一旦区域性的供应冲击真的发生，储油罐里到底有多少油可供优先提取，是个绕不开的问题。更现实的是，优先权的兑现需要商业运营商配合，而商业运营商在危机中同样有自己的客户与利润表。菲律宾买到的，究竟是实物保障，还是一张需要再谈判的凭证，2027年见分晓。

## 日本的算盘：从供应链倒推经济安全

东京对这件事的热心，不能只用「援助」解释。据日经亚洲报道，日本在供应链和制造业上高度依赖东南亚生产的石油衍生产品；若东盟建立储备体系，日本自身的经济安全也随之增强。逻辑很清楚：东南亚的油罐稳，日本的工厂才稳。

把时间轴拉长，这一步与10月马尼拉「亚洲零排放共同体」部长级会议提出的扩大亚洲石油储备计划是一脉相承的。当时日方抛出总规模约100亿美元（约720亿元人民币）的能源支援构想，框架大、口号响；11月的动作则是把框架拧成一颗螺丝——三国、一套票券、一个日期。与其说是新战略，不如说是把战略落成了合同。对东京而言，出口「储备治理」经验，比出口油罐更划算。

## 东亚—东盟经济研究中心搭台：智库写规则，谁买单？

牵头推动的并非某国政府部门，而是总部设在印尼、由日本经济产业省主导的国际智库东亚—东盟经济研究中心。它把票券制写进了2026年11月的策略建议，主张把国家储备、票券机制和共享仓储组合起来。老挝与东帝汶据称也在考虑加入，未来的目标是把机制扩至东盟整体，并把储备品种从成品油延伸到原油。

这里有一个常被忽略的权力问题：区域规则的起点往往是技术建议。谁定义票券、谁设计触发条件、谁决定定价公式，谁就在事实上写下了未来的地区标准。智库搭台、企业签字、政府背书，这套流程效率很高，但东盟的「中心地位」究竟体现在哪里，仍要看规则制定过程中的话语权分配，而不只是看会议在哪国开。

## 三个未解之问

第一是价格。票券要预先付费，条件与价格留待后续敲定。费用过高，最需要保障的国家反而是最买不起的国家；费用过低，商业运营商没有动力参与。第二是启动权。「紧急情况」由谁认定、多长时间内必须放油、违约如何追责，目前都没有答案。没有争议解决机制的优先权，危机时刻就是一纸空文。

第三是物理约束。印尼的储备天数不足一个月，泰国虽有较厚底子，但三国的商业库存并非专为区域应急而设。票券制可以绕开新建油罐的漫长周期，却绕不开「罐里有没有油」这个朴素问题。制度设计再精巧，也不能凭空变出燃料。

## 华商视角：账单会落到谁头上

对东南亚的华人商户而言，这不是与己无关的技术新闻。区域内的燃油分销、船运、仓储与贸易环节，华人经营者参与很深；票券制一旦落地，意味着多一层合规、多一套资格、多一份费用。谁能成为持票方，谁只能继续排队，可能重新划分生意的边界。

另一重影响在投资端。中国与区域资本在印尼、泰国的炼化与储运项目中有不少布局，而这套规则由日方支持的智库牵头设计、以东盟名义推进，初期未必充分吸纳所有相关方。若未来的票券定价与优先顺序把部分投资者排除在外，「区域共享」就可能变成少数人的俱乐部。华侨华人商界需要关注的，不只是油价，还有规则的门槛。

## 结语：保险单不等于加油站

平心而论，票券制比没有机制强。它用低成本把闲置的商业库存变成区域应急资源，也为长期亏损的储备建设提供了一条新路径。但保险单不等于加油站：它不能替代炼化能力，不能替代足够的库存，也不能替代透明、可执行的规则。

11月14日的签字只是开头，真正的考试在2027年，在第一场需要启动票券的危机里。届时人们会知道，东盟买到的到底是抗风险的制度，还是一份纸面安心。东南亚要的不是一个更漂亮的储备概念，而是一套在危机时刻真的能打开油罐的规则。

## 延伸阅读

**票券制度（Ticket system）**：预先付费购买紧急情况下优先提取特定数量油品权利的安排，欧盟已有实践，东盟尚属首次；优点是盘活商业库存，难点在定价与启动认定。

**东亚—东盟经济研究中心（ERIA）**：总部位于印尼雅加达的国际智库，由日本经济产业省推动设立，长期为东盟区域能源与产业合作提供政策研究与规则草案。

**霍尔木兹海峡（Strait of Hormuz）**：波斯湾通往外部市场的关键航道，全球约五分之一海运原油经此运输；约80%经该海峡的油品流向亚洲，其安全直接决定亚洲国家的供油成本。

# Tickets instead of tanks? Japan's script for ASEAN's shared oil reserve

## From concept to contracts: ASEAN puts reserves on the signing table

On 12 November, Nikkei Asia reported that ASEAN members will create a system for sharing petroleum product stockpiles in emergencies as early as 2027. Storage facilities are planned in Indonesia and Thailand, focused on diesel and gasoline; in a crisis, the Philippines would be able to buy from them. On 14 November, ahead of the 49th ASEAN Summit in Manila on 15-18 November, a subsidiary of the Philippine National Oil Company was due to sign letters of intent with Indonesian tank operators and a Thai storage company at the ASEAN Business and Investment Summit.

Worth noting: this is not another declaratory statement. It supplies a date, a place, a buyer and an instrument — 2027, Indonesia and Thailand, the Philippines, and a ticket scheme. Regional energy cooperation in Southeast Asia has long been rich in meetings and poor in delivery. This time, at least the basic questions of who signs, where fuel is stored and how access works have been put on the table. From concept to contract is a step more real than slogans.

## The numbers: 15 days and 14 days of cover

The case for the scheme rests on an unflattering balance sheet. According to the Economic Research Institute for ASEAN and East Asia (ERIA), Philippine oil reserves cover just 15 to 30 days of demand; Indonesia's cover is thinner still, at 14 to 23 days. A different methodology — Japan's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy counts crude plus products — puts Thailand at 61 days, the Philippines at 53 and Vietnam at 30. The numbers differ widely because the definitions differ, but they point the same way: Southeast Asia's buffer is perilously thin.

External dependence makes it worse. Roughly 80% of crude and refined products passing through the Strait of Hormuz are destined for Asia, and more than 90% of Philippine crude imports come from the Middle East, according to Nikkei Asia and regional media citing the same figures. When conflict broke out in the Middle East in February 2026, countries without adequate stocks felt the supply uncertainty immediately. Thin reserves are not a hypothetical risk; they were tested earlier this year.

## The ticket system: turning emergency supply into a prepaid right

The core innovation is not new tanks but a ticket mechanism: participating countries or companies pay an upfront fee for the right to purchase a pre-agreed quantity of diesel and gasoline first when an emergency hits. In normal times, tank operators sell fuel commercially. When supply is disrupted, ticket holders get priority access to their agreed volume. Similar arrangements exist in the European Union and elsewhere, but this would be the first in ASEAN.

In plain terms, this financialises security. Instead of filling every country with public reserves, it layers a priority claim over existing commercial inventories. That is clever — and cost-efficient, turning capital expenditure on tanks into the premium on an insurance policy. But a policy pays out only if the terms are clear. What counts as an "emergency"? Who is authorised to trigger the scheme? How are tickets priced? None of these details has been settled. A right written on paper still leaves open whose obligation it becomes.

## Why the Philippines signed up first

Of all the parties, the Philippines has the most urgent reason. Almost all of its energy needs are imported, its own reserves last only 15 to 30 days, and Manila has repeatedly said it wants to build a strategic stockpile. It is also the 2026 ASEAN chair and the summit host, so political timing and practical need happen to coincide.

Signing, however, is not the same as getting fuel. A ticket guarantees priority, not availability. Indonesia holds only 14 to 23 days of cover itself; if a regional supply shock actually arrives, how much fuel will really sit in those tanks for priority withdrawal? Priority rights also depend on commercial operators, which have their own customers and profit sheets in a crisis. Whether Manila ends up with physical assurance or merely a voucher requiring another round of bargaining will become clear in 2027.

## Japan's calculus: working backwards from supply chains

Tokyo's enthusiasm cannot be explained by altruism alone. Nikkei Asia notes that Japanese supply chains and manufacturing depend heavily on petroleum-derived products made in Southeast Asia; a regional reserve system would therefore strengthen Japan's own economic security. The logic is simple: full tanks in Southeast Asia mean steadier factories in Japan.

Seen against a longer timeline, this step follows from the plan to expand Asian oil reserves floated at the Japan-led AZEC ministerial meeting in Manila in October. Tokyo then unveiled an energy-support vision of roughly US$10 billion, grand in scope and rich in slogan. The November move turns that frame into a single bolt: three countries, one ticket scheme, one date. It is less a new strategy than a strategy rendered as a contract. For Tokyo, exporting reserve governance is cheaper than exporting storage tanks.

## ERIA sets the stage: who writes the rules?

The effort has been spearheaded not by a government ministry but by ERIA, a Jakarta-based institute backed by Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. Its November 2026 strategy paper recommends combining national reserves, a ticket system and shared storage. Laos and Timor-Leste are reportedly considering joining, with the longer-term goal of extending the mechanism to all of ASEAN and to crude oil as well as products.

There is a quiet question of power here. Regional rules often begin as technical advice. Whoever defines the ticket, designs the trigger and sets the pricing formula effectively writes tomorrow's regional standard. Think tank, corporate signatures, governmental endorsement — the sequence is efficient. Yet ASEAN's much-vaunted centrality will be judged by who has a voice in drafting those rules, not by which city hosts the meeting.

## Three unanswered questions

First, price. Tickets require an upfront payment, with terms and prices left for later. Set too high, and the countries most in need of cover are those least able to buy it; set too low, and commercial operators have no incentive to participate. Second, the trigger. Who declares an emergency, how quickly must fuel be released, and what happens when a party defaults? Without a dispute-settlement mechanism, a priority right is wishful thinking in a crisis.

Third, physical constraints. Indonesia's cover is under a month, and while Thailand's is thicker, the commercial inventories of all three countries were not built for regional emergencies. A ticket scheme can bypass the long lead time for new tanks, but it cannot bypass the elementary question of whether the tanks contain fuel. No amount of institutional ingenuity conjures up barrels.

## The Chinese-business angle: who pays the bill?

For ethnic Chinese businesses across Southeast Asia, this is not a technical story with no bearing on their work. Chinese traders and families are deeply involved in fuel distribution, shipping, storage and regional commerce; a ticket system would add a layer of compliance, qualification and cost. Who qualifies as a ticket holder and who remains in the ordinary queue may redraw the boundaries of the trade.

There is a second-order effect for investors. Chinese and regional capital has built positions in refining and storage in Indonesia and Thailand, yet the rules are being designed under the guidance of a Japan-backed think tank and advanced in ASEAN's name, without all affected parties necessarily in the room. If future ticket pricing and priority rankings shut some investors out, "regional sharing" could become a club for a few. Overseas Chinese business communities should watch not only the price of oil, but the threshold for entry into the scheme.

## Conclusion: an insurance policy is not a gas station

To be fair, a ticket scheme is better than no scheme. It converts idle commercial inventory into regional emergency capacity at low cost and offers an alternative to the long, expensive business of building public stockpiles. But an insurance policy is not a gas station: it cannot substitute for refining capacity, adequate stocks, or transparent and enforceable rules.

The signing on 14 November is only the opening. The real examination comes in 2027, in the first crisis that requires tickets to be activated. Only then will we learn whether ASEAN bought a genuine institution for managing risk or a paper reassurance. What Southeast Asia needs is not a prettier concept of reserves, but a set of rules that will actually open the tanks when the lights go out.

## Further reading

**Ticket system**: an arrangement in which countries or firms pay in advance for the right to withdraw a specified quantity of fuel first during an emergency. Already used in the EU, it is new to ASEAN; its promise lies in activating commercial stocks, its difficulty in pricing and trigger rules.

**Economic Research Institute for ASEAN and East Asia (ERIA)**: a Jakarta-based think tank established with backing from Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, which has long supplied policy research and draft rules for regional energy and industrial cooperation.

**Strait of Hormuz**: the narrow passage from the Gulf to world markets, through which roughly a fifth of globally traded seaborne crude passes. About 80% of oil moving through it is bound for Asia, making its security a direct determinant of Asian supply costs.
