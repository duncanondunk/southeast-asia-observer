# 美报告点名九成东盟：“大转口骗局”下的产业链与关税博弈

白宫一份题为《大转口骗局：兴起、范围与代价》的报告，把东南亚再次推到中美贸易博弈的聚光灯下。据南华早报报道，这份发布于二〇二六年八月的报告指控中国企业借四十余国转运，以规避美国关税；在十个东盟国家中，九个被点名，仅文莱幸免。这既是一纸贸易调查的警告，也是“中国+1（China+1）”红利背后一道难以回避的裂缝。

## 一份报告，九成东盟上名单

报告将四十余个被点名的法域按与中国的融合深度分为三层。越南、泰国、马来西亚与印度尼西亚被列为第二层“与中国深度融合的规模型经济体”，意指它们既是电子、机械、塑料、鞋服等含中国成分商品的制造平台，也具备足以大量输美的港口与物流纵深。柬埔寨、老挝、缅甸、菲律宾与新加坡则落入第三层“机会型目标”，凭借低成本劳力、保税仓、自由区或宽松执法等“薄弱环节”被利用。换言之，东盟几乎整片被卷进同一张网：既是承接产能的赢家，也成了转运疑云的焦点。

## 手法：换标、重组、重开发票

报告列举的规避手法并不新鲜——有限的组装、换标、重新包装、重开发票，以及虚假的原产地申报。真正的关键，在于一笔货物是否发生了“实质性改变”：若只是在第三国贴牌、分装，原产地并未真正转移，关税差额便成了套利空间。这也解释了为何太阳能组件等领域屡屡成为反规避调查的重灾区——中国原产的光伏零件，在柬埔寨、马来西亚、泰国与越南完成最后工序后，常以当地身份进入美国市场。

## 数字之争：从四百亿到三千亿美元

规模的估算差异巨大。高盛（Goldman Sachs）给出最窄的四百亿美元（约两千八百八十亿元人民币）下限，而数据公司奥尔特纳（Altana）的上限高达三千零三十亿美元（约二万一千八百亿元人民币）；白宫经济顾问委员会估在三十四亿至八百九十亿美元（约六千四百亿元人民币）之间，咨询机构埃克西杰（Exiger）以七百五十亿美元（约五千四百亿元人民币）为中值。按七百五十亿美元的年转运量推算，美方称每年流失关税收入约一百九十亿至二百六十亿美元（约一千四百亿至一千九百亿元人民币），并模型化地估计牵连四十五万个美国岗位。越南与墨西哥、印度被列为全球三大枢纽，二〇二五年转运额约六百七十亿美元（约四千八百亿元人民币），对应关税流失约二百八十亿美元（约二千亿元人民币）。

## 东盟的两难：既受益，又被盯

转运并非零和噩梦——本地企业赚到组装费、仓储费与港口费，政府收获税收与贸易增量。但美国已不再只当旁观者：白宫贸易顾问纳瓦罗称正与美国海关与边境保护局共建一套人工智能“侦探边境”，用航运数据与路由历史识别可疑转运。泰国方面，开泰研究中心提醒政策制定者正视风险，业界则以四成本地采购规则自证清白。对东盟而言，真正的考题是如何在继续吸引直接投资的同时，建立可追溯的原产地与制造活动证明，避免被当作中国避税通道——否则，这张网迟早会收紧成谈判桌上的筹码。

## 不结盟的韧性，还是被夹的中间？

“中国+1”把订单与工厂送来东南亚，也顺手打开了转运的方便之门。东盟长期标榜不在中美间选边，但一份关税报告揭示：当中美都在用规则重塑供应链，不结盟的红利正变得越来越贵。报告更可能成为美国与各国贸易谈判中的警告弹——它不必然立刻施压选边，却会逼东南亚在国家层面厘清自家的供应链底稿。对越南、泰国、马来西亚与印度尼西亚这些深度融入中国网络的经济体来说，增长与合规，已从两可变成必须同时作答的一题。

## 延伸阅读

**原产地规则（Rules of Origin）**：判定货物“国籍”的准则，决定其适用何种关税；是否发生实质性改变，是甄别转运避税的核心。

**反倾销与反补贴税（AD/CVD, Anti-Dumping & Countervailing Duties）**：针对低价倾销与受补贴进口加征的关税；太阳能等品类常因中国原产零件在第三国完工而触发反规避调查。

**转口贸易（Transshipment）**：货物经第三国中转或再加工后输往最终市场；正当中转与借道避税之间，界限常取决于原产地是否真实转移。

# Washington Names Nine of Ten ASEAN: The "Great Transshipment Scam" and the Tariff Fight

A White House report titled "The Great Transshipment Scam: Rise, Scope, and Costs" has again pushed Southeast Asia into the spotlight of the US–China trade contest. According to the South China Morning Post, the August 2026 report accuses Chinese firms of routing goods through more than 40 countries to dodge US tariffs; of the ten ASEAN members, nine were named, with only Brunei spared. It is both a trade-investigation warning and a hard-to-ignore crack in the dividends of "China+1".

## A report that names nine of ten ASEAN

The report tiers the 40-odd named jurisdictions by how deeply they are fused with China. Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia and Indonesia are placed in Tier 2 — "scale leaders with significant economic integration with China" — meaning they are manufacturing platforms for electronics, machinery, plastics, footwear and apparel carrying Chinese inputs, with the port and logistics depth to ship large volumes to the US. Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, the Philippines and Singapore fall into Tier 3 — "opportunistic targets" exploited via cheap labour, bonded warehouses, free zones or lax enforcement. In short, nearly the whole bloc is caught in one web: winner of relocated capacity, and focal point of transshipment suspicion at once.

## The methods: relabel, rework, re-invoice

The evasion tactics are old: limited assembly, relabelling, repackaging, re-invoicing, and false country-of-origin declarations. The real question is whether a good underwent "substantial transformation" — if it was merely relabelled or repacked abroad, origin never truly shifted, and the tariff gap becomes arbitrage. That is why solar modules keep landing in anti-circumvention probes: Chinese-origin photovoltaic parts finished in Cambodia, Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam often enter the US market under a local identity.

## The numbers war: from US$40bn to US$303bn

The scale estimates vary wildly. Goldman Sachs puts the floor at US$40 billion; data firm Altana's ceiling reaches US$303 billion; the White House Council of Economic Advisers estimates US$34–90 billion, while consultant Exiger centres on US$75 billion. On a US$75 billion annual flow, Washington claims US$19–26 billion in lost tariff revenue a year and models some 450,000 displaced US jobs. Vietnam, with Mexico and India, is named a top-three global hub — about US$67 billion transshipped in 2025, implying roughly US$28 billion in lost duties.

## ASEAN's dilemma: beneficiary and target at once

Transshipment is not a zero-sum nightmare — local firms earn assembly, warehousing and port fees, and governments collect tax and trade growth. But the US is no longer a bystander: trade adviser Peter Navarro says Washington is building an AI-enabled "detective border" with Customs and Border Protection, using shipment data and routing histories to flag suspect flows. Thailand's Kasikorn Research warns policymakers to face the risk; industry cites 40% local-sourcing rules to prove clean hands. For ASEAN the real test is building traceable proof of origin and manufacturing while still courting direct investment — or the web tightens into a bargaining chip at the negotiation table.

## Resilience of non-alignment, or squeezed in the middle?

China+1 sent orders and factories to Southeast Asia, and conveniently opened the door to transshipment. ASEAN long boasts it will not pick sides between the US and China, but a tariff report shows the dividends of non-alignment are getting costlier. The document is more likely a warning shot in trade talks — it need not force an immediate choice, but it pushes Southeast Asia to clarify its own supply-chain picture at the national level. For Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia and Indonesia — economies deeply woven into Chinese networks — growth and compliance have shifted from optional to a single question that must be answered together.

## Further reading

**Rules of Origin**: The criteria that determine a good's "nationality" and thus its tariff; whether substantial transformation occurred is the core test for spotting transshipment evasion.

**Anti-Dumping & Countervailing Duties (AD/CVD)**: Extra tariffs on dumped or subsidised imports; solar and other categories often trigger anti-circumvention probes when Chinese-origin parts are finished in a third country.

**Transshipment**: Goods routed through or reprocessed in a third country before reaching the final market; the line between legitimate transit and duty evasion hinges on whether origin truly shifted.
