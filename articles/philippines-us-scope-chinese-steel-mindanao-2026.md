# 从废钢厂到前沿阵地：棉兰老岛一座中资钢厂如何成为美菲中三方博弈的焦点

8月19日，菲美两军人员试图走进棉兰老岛北部一座已停产的钢铁厂，却没能进门。据菲律宾武装部队通报，这支由美军士兵与 PHIVIDEC 综合工业园（菲律宾政府经营的重工业片区）管理局人员组成的联合小组，目的是对三佳钢铁公司的厂区进行"前期勘察"，评估其能否作为未来海军设施及明年"肩并肩"联合军演的候选场地。他们因缺乏书面授权而离开。美国大使馆特别强调，没有任何人强行进入。可是据南华早报报道，这绝非一次普通的行政小插曲——它是一扇小而锋利的窗口，让人看清在菲律宾的中资，正如何被卷入大国竞争的机器。

## 事件：一次未能进入的勘测

这次走访在形式上一本正经，在实质上却火药味十足。三佳钢铁租用位于北棉兰老岛东米萨米斯省塔戈洛安镇的政府运营 PHIVIDEC 综合工业园内的 22.6 公顷土地。这片厂区如今紧贴马尼拉视作北棉兰老岛军事扩张关键的地域。联合小组想评估厂房能否承载双边军演。当地媒体报道援引的警方记录证实，他们在被告知没有许可后随即折返。大使馆措辞谨慎——"自始至终没有强行要求进入"——显示双方都不愿再制造新的挑衅。然而，为一座中资工厂勘址、准备改作美式基地，这件事本身就足以惹恼北京。

## 一座早已蒙上阴影的工厂

三佳钢铁并非一块中立的地产。今年5月，菲律宾当局突袭该厂，拘押约70名中国劳工，指控其涉嫌违反移民与劳工法规。6月，厂区因进口废金属中检出放射性物质而被勒令停产。北京反应强硬：中国驻菲使馆指称执法带有歧视性与选择性；一名高级外交官警告可能招致报复。马尼拉否认任何偏颇，称行动完全依法依规。厂方则否认生产劣质钢材或威胁国家安全，坚称中国员工均合法受雇。这场纠纷自此成为本就紧张的双边关系里一处长期刺点。

## 从杜特尔特的靠拢到马科斯的重塑

讽刺在于地理，也在于政治。三佳钢铁建于2018年，正值杜特尔特总统靠拢北京的高峰，当时中资被当作"黄金时代"关系落地的证明而备受欢迎。如今，棉兰老岛同一隅却被觊觎为《加强防务合作协议》（Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement，简称 EDCA）下的海军基地选址。该协议允许美军在菲方约定的基地轮换驻扎；马科斯任内，适用地点已从5处增至9处。拟在 PHIVIDEC 区内新建的基地，正落在马尼拉修订后的"综合群岛防御概念"（Comprehensive Archipelagic Defense Concept，简称 CADC）之下——这一构想把优先方向转向对外领土防御。工厂没有动，战略气候却变了。

## 这块土地为何重要

北棉兰老岛紧邻在南海与台湾附近水域任何角逐中都举足轻重的航道与通道。谁控制土地，谁就控制周边兵力与舰船的调度。对美国而言，棉兰老岛一处可靠场地，扩大了在《加强防务合作协议》下轮换驻军与前置部署的选项。对马尼拉而言，它推进了以拒止外部力量进入群岛为核心的防御方案。对北京而言，这意味着一处中资商业落脚点正被美式军事存在合围——而它曾以为那片土地是敞开营商的。角逐围绕土地用途、租赁权利，以及当一座战略园区被重新规划时，谁有资格坐上谈判桌。

## "中资"身份的代价

这一事件给在菲中企上了一堂冷峻的课：政治转向可以在一夜之间把一项生产性资产变成负累。杜特尔特时期处理废钢的轧厂，到马科斯时期成了一道国家安全考题、一个外交爆点。北京的措辞异常尖锐：使馆指责防长吉尔伯托·特奥多罗企图"破坏与损害"双边关系；官媒《环球时报》更给他贴上"五角大楼提线木偶"的标签。马尼拉对这些指控不以为然。无论如何，教训会传开：在一个割裂的区域里，"中资"这一标签如今承载战略分量，而这是资产负债表从未记录过的。

## 东南亚的更普遍图景

棉兰老岛不是孤例。整个东南亚，随着美国与情愿的伙伴深化安全纽带，中资在港口、电信、电网与重工业的投资正面临日益严格的审视。几年前看似纯商业的资产，如今被排查双重用途风险、主权杠杆与数据暴露。商业与安全的灰色地带正在收窄，而具有中资背景的企业——无论是否国资背景——都承受这份审视。对东道国政府而言，两难真实存在：它们想要中资促增长，却又担忧由此带来的依赖，尤其当华盛顿还是条约盟友。

## 接下来要看什么

三个信号将表明这只是一次孤立勘察，还是更大动作的开端。其一，PHIVIDEC 是否启动正式场地研究、租约重谈或发放新许可。其二，马尼拉的公开定性——是把基地说成例行现代化，还是一次刻意释放的信号。其三，北京的回应：抗议、对菲出口报复，还是对投资方悄然施压。眼下钢铁已冷、厂房已关，但它脚下的土地已成争夺对象。在东南亚展开的这场竞争中，哪怕一座废钢厂，也可能被画上地图。

# From scrap mill to front line: how a Chinese-linked steel plant in Mindanao became a US–Philippines–China flashpoint

On 19 August, Filipino and American military personnel tried to walk into a shuttered steel plant in northern Mindanao. They did not get in. According to the Armed Forces of the Philippines, the team — which included US soldiers and staff from the PHIVIDEC Industrial Authority — had come to "scope" the Sanjia Steel Corporation site as a possible location for a future naval facility and for next year's Balikatan exercises. They left after being told they lacked written authorisation. The US embassy was quick to stress that no one forced entry. Yet the episode, reported by the South China Morning Post, is far more than a bureaucratic hiccup. It is a small but sharp window into how Chinese capital in the Philippines is being pulled into the machinery of great-power competition.

## The incident: a survey that never entered

The visit was routine in form and charged in substance. Sanjia Steel leases 22.6 hectares inside the government-run PHIVIDEC Industrial Estate in Tagoloan, Misamis Oriental province. That land now sits beside territory Manila treats as strategically vital for its military expansion in northern Mindanao. The joint team wanted to assess whether the premises could host bilateral drills. A police blotter cited by local reports confirms they turned back once told they had no permit. The embassy's careful wording — "at no point was entry insisted upon" — signals both sides' desire to avoid manufacturing a fresh provocation. But the very act of surveying a Chinese-invested plant for a US-aligned base could not help but inflame Beijing.

## A plant already under a cloud

Sanjia was not a neutral piece of real estate. In May, Philippine authorities raided the facility and detained roughly 70 Chinese workers, alleging violations of immigration and labour rules. In June, the plant was ordered shut after radioactive material was found in imported scrap metal. Beijing pushed back hard. The Chinese embassy called the enforcement discriminatory and selective; a senior diplomat warned of possible retaliation. Manila denied any bias and said the action was strictly by the book. The plant's operator rejected suggestions that it produced substandard steel or threatened national security, insisting its Chinese staff were lawfully employed. The dispute has since become a standing irritant in an already strained relationship.

## From Duterte's embrace to Marcos's realignment

The irony is geographical and political. Sanjia was established in 2018, at the height of President Rodrigo Duterte's courtship of Beijing, when Chinese investment was welcomed as proof of a "golden age" of ties. Today the same corner of Mindanao is coveted for a naval base under the Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement (EDCA), the pact that lets US forces rotate through agreed Philippine sites. Under President Ferdinand Marcos Jr., the number of EDCA locations has climbed from five to nine. A new base inside the PHIVIDEC zone would sit squarely within Manila's revised Comprehensive Archipelagic Defense Concept (CADC), which shifts priorities toward external territorial defence. The plant has not moved. The strategic weather has.

## Why this plot of land matters

Northern Mindanao sits close to the sea lanes and approaches that matter in any contest over the South China Sea and the waters near Taiwan. Whoever controls the land controls who stages forces and ships nearby. For the United States, a reliable site in Mindanao widens the options for rotating troops and pre-positioning under EDCA. For Manila, it advances a defence plan built around denying external access to its archipelago. For Beijing, it means a Chinese-linked commercial footprint is being ringed by an American-aligned military presence — on soil it once assumed was open for business. The contest is over land use, leasing rights, and who gets a seat at the table when a strategic estate is redrawn.

## The cost of being 'Chinese-linked'

The episode lays bare a brutal lesson for Chinese investors in the Philippines. Political realignment can turn a productive asset into a liability almost overnight. A mill that processed scrap under Duterte is, under Marcos, a national-security question and a diplomatic flashpoint. Beijing's rhetoric has been unusually pointed: the Chinese embassy accused Defence Secretary Gilberto Teodoro of seeking to "destabilise and sabotage" relations, and the state-run Global Times branded him a "puppet on the Pentagon's strings." Manila dismisses the charges. Either way, the lesson travels: in a fragmented region, the label "Chinese-linked" now carries strategic weight that balance sheets never captured.

## A wider pattern across Southeast Asia

Mindanao is not an isolated case. Across Southeast Asia, Chinese investment in ports, telecommunications, power grids and heavy industry faces intensifying scrutiny as the United States deepens security ties with willing partners. Assets that looked purely commercial a few years ago are now screened for dual-use risk, sovereign leverage and data exposure. The grey zone between commerce and security is narrowing, and firms of Chinese origin — whether state-linked or not — bear the scrutiny. For host governments, the dilemma is real: they want Chinese capital for growth yet fear the dependency it can bring when Washington is also a treaty ally.

## What to watch next

Three signals will show whether this was a one-off survey or the start of something larger. First, any formal site study, lease renegotiation or new permit from PHIVIDEC. Second, Manila's public framing — does it present the base as routine modernisation or as a pointed message? Third, Beijing's response: protest, retaliation against Philippine exports, or quiet pressure on the investor. For now, the steel is cold and the plant is shut. But the ground beneath it has become contested. In the competition unfolding across Southeast Asia, even a scrap-metal mill can end up on the map.
