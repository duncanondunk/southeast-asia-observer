# 泰国中华图书馆在曼谷揭牌：当中文书漂流到湄南河畔

10月中旬，曼谷多了一处特别的文化空间。由南通赤子情华侨图书馆（下称「华侨图书馆」）与泰国丝路国际教育文化交流中心合作创办的「泰国中华图书馆」（华侨图书馆泰国图书漂流总站）日前在曼谷揭牌。中国驻泰国大使馆参赞倪洋、泰国教育部基教委秘书长助理阿鲁妮·吉拉玛哈萨、民教委顾问黄淑环，以及华侨图书馆发起人镇翔等百余位嘉宾出席。仪式上，十所泰国学校获授「图书漂流站」牌匾，部分在泰华裔学生在世界华人学生作文大赛与中华诗词诵读大会泰国赛区的获奖者亦获颁奖状。

一所图书馆的开张，乍看是文化小事。但把它放进泰国华文教育升温、中泰民间交流加密的背景里，这件事的分量远不止「多了个借书的地方」。

## 一场「图书漂流」的总站落地

所谓「图书漂流」，是华侨图书馆在全球推行的阅读共享机制：一本本中文图书在海外校园、社区、侨团之间流转，读到的人再传给下一个人，不靠正式图书馆的借还系统，靠的是「读完就传下去」的公约。泰国中华图书馆的定位，正是这一项目的泰国总站——它不仅自己藏书，还要统筹、管理泰国境内已有的图书漂流站，把这套轻量网络做实。

据揭牌现场信息，泰国中华图书馆由南通赤子情华侨图书馆与泰国丝路国际教育文化交流中心合办。前者是中国民间自发、志愿者运营的华侨主题图书馆，发起人为镇翔；后者是立足泰国的教育文化交流机构。两者结合，等于把中国民间的中文藏书资源，与在地的华文教育渠道直接接上了轨。

## 轻量化的文化出海

值得玩味的是这套模式的「轻」。它不盖大楼、不配编制、不追求政绩式的馆舍规模，而是把中文书一本本地「漂」进泰国校园。对接收方来说，门槛极低：一所学校只要愿意让学生读完传下去，就能挂上一块漂流站牌匾。对输出方来说，成本也低：书是现成的，志愿者是现成的，机制是可复制的。

这恰恰是民间文化软实力的聪明之处。比起砸钱建中心、派专人常驻，图书漂流更像「播撒」而非「摆设」——它把中华文化稀释进日常阅读，让泰国学生在课堂之外，通过中文故事、中华诗词自己去感受文字之美。泰国教育部民教委顾问黄淑环就直言，华文教育在泰国学校中「越来越受欢迎」，而这座图书馆正好补上了阅读资源的缺口。

## 为何是泰国：华文教育的需求底色

为什么是泰国，而不是别处？答案写在需求里。近年来泰国的中文学习热度持续走高，从王室到民间、从国际学校到寺庙学校，中文课的开课面越来越广。泰国教育部基教委秘书长助理阿鲁妮在致辞中特别提到，希望借中文阅读「认识中文故事，感受中华文字与诗词之美，助力中文教学提质增效」——这番话出自泰国教育官员之口，本身就说明中文教育已被纳入泰国主流教育体系的提质增效议程，而非边缘化的族裔兴趣班。

对泰国的华裔家庭而言，这座图书馆还有另一层意义。泰国华人约占全国人口一成多，是东南亚规模可观、融入度高的华社之一。过去，华人子弟的母语传承多靠家庭与华校；如今，一座面向全社会的中华图书馆落地，意味着中文阅读资源从「族裔内部」走向「校园公共空间」，这对离散社群的身份延续是一种温和而务实的支撑。

## 使馆、教育部与侨社的三方合力

这场揭牌的来宾结构，也值得咀嚼。中国驻泰使馆参赞、泰国教育部两位官员、华侨社团与华文教育机构负责人同台，百余位嘉宾涵盖了官方、学界与民间三层。倪洋参赞把图书漂流概括为「在流转共享中传承中华文化，传递中泰友谊」，这句话点出了项目的双重属性：它既是文化工程，也是民间外交工程。

这种「三方合力」的格局，恰恰是中国文化走出去里少见却有效的组合：使馆提供背书与渠道，泰国教育部提供制度入口，侨社与华教机构提供落地网络。比起单靠某一方唱独角戏，把官方桥梁、教育系统和离散社群拧成一股绳，渗透力反而更强。

## 与孔子学院不同的路径

有人会问：全球不是已经有五百一十所孔子学院了吗，再多一个中文图书馆，意义何在？问得有理，但两者路径并不重合。孔子学院多由高校承办、依托所在大学、偏重于语言教学与文化交流活动；而泰国中华图书馆走的是社区化、漂流式的民间路线，不绑定固定课堂，不依赖大学建制，服务的是更广泛的社会读者——包括那些不在孔子学院覆盖范围内的普通学校与学生。

换句话说，这是文化软实力的「毛细血管」，而非「主干道」。主干道负责规模和声势，毛细血管负责触达和黏性。对一个华文教育需求旺盛、又高度重视民间友好的国家而言，多一些这样的毛细血管，显然是好事而非重复建设。

说到底，一本书从南通「漂」到曼谷，成本不高，意义却不小。它让中文不再只是课堂上的生字表，而成了可以在校园里流传、被学生自己翻开的真实读物。当更多泰国孩子通过一本本漂流书认识中华文字与诗词之美，中泰之间的民间纽带，也就在一次次「读完传下去」里，被悄悄系紧了。

## 延伸阅读

- **华侨图书馆（Overseas Chinese Library）**：总部位于江苏南通、由志愿者运营的民间华侨主题图书馆，发起人为镇翔，以「全球图书漂流」项目在海外设立漂流站、输送中文读物。
- **图书漂流（Book Drifting）**：一种非正式的阅读共享机制，书籍在读者间依次流转、读完即传给下一位，不依赖正式借还系统，常用于海外华文教育与文化推广。
- **华文教育（Chinese-Medium Education）**：面向华侨华人及外国学习者的中文教学体系；在泰国等地近年需求上升，被纳入主流教育提质增效议程。

# A Chinese Library Opens in Bangkok: When Chinese Books Drift to the Chao Phraya

In mid-October, Bangkok gained an unusual cultural space. The Thailand Chinese Library — also the Thailand hub of the "Overseas Chinese Library Book Drifting Project" — was inaugurated in the city, co-founded by the Nantong Chiziqing Overseas Chinese Library and the Thailand Silk Road International Education and Cultural Exchange Center. More than a hundred guests attended, including Ni Yang, counselor of the Chinese Embassy in Thailand; Arunee Jiramahasa, assistant secretary to the secretary-general of Thailand's Basic Education Commission; Huang Shuhuan, advisor to the Private Education Commission; and Zhen Xiang, founder of the Overseas Chinese Library. At the ceremony, ten Thai schools were awarded plaques as book-drifting stations, and winners of the 26th World Chinese Student Composition Competition and the 2026 Chinese Poetry Recitation Conference (Thailand division) received awards.

The opening of a library might look like a minor cultural footnote. Placed against the backdrop of surging Chinese-language education in Thailand and deepening people-to-people exchange between China and Thailand, however, its significance reaches well beyond "one more place to borrow books."

## A hub for "book drifting" lands in Bangkok

"Book drifting" is a reading-share mechanism promoted by the Overseas Chinese Library worldwide: Chinese books circulate among overseas campuses, communities, and diaspora associations; each reader passes the book on after finishing it, without a formal lending system, relying instead on an informal pact of "read and pass along." The Thailand Chinese Library is designed as the project's national hub — it will not only hold its own collection but also coordinate and manage existing drifting stations across Thailand, turning a light network into something operational.

According to the ceremony, the library is jointly run by the Nantong Chiziqing Overseas Chinese Library and the Thailand Silk Road International Education and Cultural Exchange Center. The former is a volunteer-run, civil-society library in China whose founder is Zhen Xiang; the latter is a Thailand-based education and cultural exchange body. Their pairing connects China's civil-society stock of Chinese books directly to on-the-ground Chinese-education channels in Thailand.

## A lightweight form of cultural outreach

What is worth noting is how "light" the model is. It builds no grand building, creates no standing bureaucracy, and chases no prestige-driven floor space. Instead, it lets Chinese books "drift" into Thai campuses one volume at a time. For recipients, the threshold is minimal: a school only needs to promise that students will read and pass books on to earn a drifting-station plaque. For the sender, the cost is also low: the books exist, the volunteers exist, and the mechanism is replicable.

This is precisely the clever part of civil-society cultural soft power. Rather than spending heavily on centers or resident staff, book drifting is closer to "sowing" than to "displaying" — it dilutes Chinese culture into everyday reading, letting Thai students encounter the beauty of Chinese characters and poetry on their own, outside the classroom. Huang Shuhuan, advisor to Thailand's Private Education Commission, put it plainly: Chinese-language education is "increasingly popular" in Thai schools, and the library fills a gap in reading resources.

## Why Thailand: the demand behind Chinese-language education

Why Thailand, and not somewhere else? The answer lies in demand. Chinese-language learning has kept rising in Thailand in recent years — from the palace to the public, from international schools to temple schools, Chinese classes keep spreading. Arunee Jiramahasa, assistant secretary to Thailand's Basic Education Commission, noted in her remarks that she hoped Chinese reading would help students "know Chinese stories, feel the beauty of Chinese characters and poetry, and improve the quality and efficiency of Chinese teaching." That a Thai education official would say this shows Chinese-language education has entered the quality-and-efficiency agenda of Thailand's mainstream system, not a marginal ethnic-interest track.

For Thai Chinese families, the library carries another meaning. Ethnic Chinese make up well over ten percent of Thailand's population — one of Southeast Asia's largest and most integrated communities. In the past, mother-tongue transmission relied mainly on families and Chinese schools. Now, a Chinese library open to society at large moves Chinese reading resources from "inside the ethnic group" into "public campus space," a gentle yet practical buttress to the diaspora community's identity continuity.

## Embassy, ministry, and community: a three-way push

The guest list at the inauguration also repays attention. A Chinese embassy counselor, two Thai education-ministry officials, and leaders of overseas-Chinese associations and Chinese-education institutions shared the stage; more than a hundred guests spanned official, academic, and civil-society layers. Counselor Ni Yang described book drifting as a way to "pass on Chinese culture and China–Thailand friendship through circulation and sharing" — a phrase that names the project's dual nature: it is both a cultural undertaking and a people-to-people diplomacy undertaking.

This "three-way push" is a rare but effective combination in China's cultural outreach: the embassy provides endorsement and channels, the Thai ministry provides an institutional gateway, and the diaspora community and Chinese-education bodies provide the delivery network. Rather than any single side performing solo, braiding the official bridge, the education system, and the diaspora community together yields stronger penetration.

## A different path from Confucius Institutes

Some will ask: with some 510 Confucius Institutes already established worldwide, what is the point of one more Chinese library? A fair question — but the paths do not overlap. Confucius Institutes are mostly run by universities, attached to host institutions, and oriented toward language teaching and cultural events. The Thailand Chinese Library takes a community-based, drifting, civil-society route: no fixed classroom, no dependence on university structures, serving a broader social readership — including ordinary schools and students outside the Confucius Institute footprint.

In other words, this is the "capillary" of cultural soft power, not its "main artery." The artery handles scale and visibility; the capillaries handle reach and stickiness. For a country with strong Chinese-education demand and a high premium on people-to-people friendship, more such capillaries are clearly a plus, not redundant construction.

In the end, a book drifting from Nantong to Bangkok costs little but means a good deal. It turns Chinese from a vocabulary list in class into a real book a student can open on their own. As more Thai children meet the beauty of Chinese characters and poetry through drifting volumes, the people-to-people bond between China and Thailand is quietly tightened, one "read and pass along" at a time.

## Further reading

- **Overseas Chinese Library**: a volunteer-run, civil-society library based in Nantong, Jiangsu, founded by Zhen Xiang, which sends Chinese reading materials overseas through its global "book drifting" project and station network.
- **Book Drifting**: an informal reading-share mechanism in which books pass from reader to reader, each passing the volume on after finishing, without a formal lending system; commonly used in overseas Chinese-language education and cultural promotion.
- **Chinese-Medium Education**: the system of Chinese teaching for overseas Chinese and foreign learners; demand has risen in Thailand and elsewhere, entering the quality-and-efficiency agenda of mainstream education.
