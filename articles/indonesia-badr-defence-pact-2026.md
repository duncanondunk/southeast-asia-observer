# 从「满月」到自主：印尼牵头的四国「巴德尔」防务协议，能甩掉西方军售的「政治弦」吗？

2026年10月21日，在卡塔尔首都多哈，四个以穆斯林为主体的国家——土耳其、卡塔尔、巴基斯坦与印度尼西亚——签署了一份谅解备忘录，并将其命名为「巴德尔」（Badr），阿拉伯语意为「满月」。这份文件不是共同防御条约，而是一份产业契约：四国承诺把国防生产、技术培训、研发与供应链拧成一股绳。对其中体量最小却人口最多的签约方印度尼西亚而言，真正的问题是：这轮「满月」能否真正照亮国防自主之路，还是一如部分分析人士私下嘀咕的那样，只是一只纸老虎。

## 现场：多哈升起一轮「满月」

签字仪式的象征意味很浓。土耳其国防部长亚沙尔·居莱尔、国防工业局局长哈鲁克·戈尔根、卡塔尔副首相兼国防事务国务大臣沙特亲王、巴基斯坦国防部长赫瓦贾·阿西夫，以及印尼国防部副部长优素福·乔哈里，在土耳其国防部的旗帜下共同落笔。措辞很克制：讲的是合作与融合，而非对抗。雅加达很快把这份协议框定为它早已分别与各伙伴建立的双边关系的自然延伸。正如印尼外交部发言人瓦赫德·纳比尔所言，该协议契合印尼拓宽防务伙伴、提升本土产能的整体布局。

这个定位很关键。印尼加入的不是针对任何人的安全集团，而是一个生产俱乐部。

## 数字：四国各自端出什么家底

「巴德尔」协议真正有意思的地方，在于它的算术。四个签约方各拿一样家底。土耳其拿出的是成熟的军工体系——系统集成、无人机与战斗机的研发经验（know-how），以及四国中最发达的军火产业，并预计承接大部分联合生产线。卡塔尔拿出的是资本与需求：其国防部下属的投资臂膀巴尔赞控股（Barzan Holdings）已于今年1月与印尼企业共和集团（Republikorp）签下23亿美元（约166亿元人民币）的合资协议，用于升级印尼的陆上与海上系统。巴基斯坦与印尼拿出的则是制造能力、熟练劳动力和可观的国内市场，以及在研制与维护复杂平台方面积累的经验。

印尼并非从零起步。它早已与土耳其一家防务公司联合生产「卡普兰」中型坦克（Kaplan MT）——2019年敲定的协议，18辆坦克已于2024年交付完毕；去年它又同意采购48架土耳其「可汗」第五代战斗机（Kaan），协议金额超过100亿美元（约720亿元人民币）。如今它还在探讨从巴基斯坦采购至多40架「雷电」战斗机（JF-17）的可能性，不过仍停留在初步阶段。用印尼经济学家穆罕默德·祖尔菲卡尔·拉赫马特的话说，「巴德尔」带来三重实利：技术与工艺主要来自土耳其，资金来自卡塔尔，而更广阔的市场则留给印尼本土的平达德（Pindad）、帕尔（PAL）、印尼宇航（Dirgantara Indonesia）等军工企业。

## 动机：甩掉西方的「政治弦」

剥开产业语言的外壳，真正的动力是政治。曾担任卡塔尔武装部队英国国防部顾问的安德烈亚斯·克里格说得很直白：「巴德尔」本质上是为了「恰恰逃离这种脆弱性」——即依赖西方供应商的那种脆弱性，因为西方的出口管制与政治条件，决定了这些武器系统「何时、以何种方式才能被使用」。克里格认为，伊朗战争暴露了依赖外部供应商的地区安全体系的短板。「巴德尔」紧跟着今年8月初由土耳其、沙特、巴基斯坦签署的「麦加防务联盟」（Mecca Defence Alliance）：麦加给的是威慑的政治承诺，而巴德尔未来可能提供支撑这种威慑的工业手段。

对这四国而言，逻辑并非抛弃与西方的长期盟友关系，而是要在自身安全上拿到更大的主动权。正如一位分析人士所说，更紧密的关系能让更多国家「在彼此的安全中拥有利害关系（stakes）」，这比一纸友好宣言管用得多。

## 印尼的算盘：战略自主优先

印尼的算盘和伙伴们并不一样。巴基斯坦活在与印度核武对抗的阴影之下，卡塔尔眼前的麻烦是伊朗，土耳其则正重新定位（repositioning）为军火出口国。印尼则不同：它面临迫在眉睫的威胁最小——远离中东，也不是南海争议的当事方。观察人士强调，它的首要目标是战略自主——有能力自行研制并维持装备，而不是向大国租借。

这在雅加达是一项由来已久的抱负，却一次次被对外国原始设备制造商的依赖所挫败。「巴德尔」协议为印尼提供了筹码，使其得以在中东撬动武器与新防务交易的准入，阿伯里斯特威斯大学的国际关系学者艾哈迈德·里兹基如此分析。对一个自视为中等强国的国家来说，一个游离于西方帐篷之外的「买而造」网络（buy-and-build），吸引力显而易见。

## 隐忧一：技术天花板

最严肃的保留意见是，「巴德尔」恐怕并不能真正让成员摆脱西方。先进推进系统、航电、传感器与部分电子元器件，仍离不开西方技术。土耳其的军工体系再成熟，底层子系统依旧依赖西方。而把新平台整合进印尼既有装备序列，也绝非易事。由巴基斯坦与中国联合生产的「雷电」战斗机，就抛出一个尴尬的问题：一架带着中国血缘的战机，如何与印尼的法国「阵风」战斗机（Rafale）和土耳其「可汗」同框列装？正如穆罕默德所言，「问题在于雷电是和中国一起造的」。一个许诺自主的协议，到头来可能只是换了一副依赖的枷锁。

## 隐忧二：印度的变数

外部反应同样不容小觑。前美国五角大楼高官戴夫·德斯罗什将「巴德尔」备忘录称作一个具有政治意义的「意愿声明」——四国有意获取先进军事能力。但他点出了一个印度变数：新德里的印度教民族主义政府，可能把「巴德尔」解读为印尼这个世界人口最多的穆斯林国家，已被「拉入伊斯兰堡的军事盟友或伙伴」之列。巴基斯坦今年5月刚与印度打了一场短暂的空中冲突，任何此类观感都极易被点燃。即便协议纯属产业性质，雅加达与伊斯兰堡防务拥抱的「观感」，在德里眼中也会被读作战略信号，而非商业往来。

## 区域镜鉴：给东南亚的一堂课

对东南亚而言，「巴德尔」是一记信号，而非威胁。它展示了一个地区中等强国，试图像工业化消费品那样工业化自己的国防——靠的是组网，而不是选边。这堂课有两面。一方面，它表明战略自主正越来越多地通过供应链建设、而非集团站队来追求，而这正是东盟国家公开奉行的姿态。另一方面，它也暴露了这种自主有多难：最深层的技术仍被西方的出口管制与中国的联合生产所把持。印尼的「满月」或许照亮了目标，但通往真正国防自主的道路依旧漫长，且遍是它本想逃离的那些依赖。

## 延伸阅读

**麦加防务联盟（Mecca Defence Alliance）：** 2026年8月由土耳其、沙特、巴基斯坦签署的政治性共同防御谅解，与「巴德尔」的产业取向形成互补。

# From "full moon" to autonomy: can Indonesia's four-nation "Badr" defence pact loosen the West's political strings on arms sales?

On October 21, 2026, in Doha, four Muslim-majority nations — Turkey, Qatar, Pakistan and Indonesia — signed a memorandum of understanding they named "Badr," the Arabic word for "full moon." The agreement is not a mutual-defence treaty. It is an industrial compact: the four pledge to pool defence production, technical training, research and development, and supply chains. For Indonesia, the junior but most populous signatory, the question is whether this "full moon" can actually light a path toward defence autonomy, or whether it is, as some analysts already whisper, a paper tiger.

## The scene: a "full moon" rises in Doha

The signing ceremony was heavy with symbolism. Turkey's defence minister Yaşar Güler, the head of its defence industries agency Haluk Görgün, Qatar's deputy prime minister Sheikh Saud Abdulrahman, Pakistan's defence minister Khawaja Muhammad Asif, and Indonesia's deputy defence minister Yusuf Jauhari put pen to paper under the Turkish Defence Ministry's banner. The language was careful: cooperation and integration, not confrontation. Jakarta was quick to frame the pact as a natural extension of ties it already had with each partner separately. As Foreign Ministry spokesperson Vahd Nabyl Achmad Mulachela put it, the deal aligns with Indonesia's broader push to diversify its defence partnerships and lift domestic production capacity.

That framing matters. Indonesia is not joining a security bloc aimed at anyone. It is joining a production club.

## The numbers: what each partner brings

The arithmetic of the Badr pact is what makes it interesting. Each signatory contributes a different asset. Turkey brings the mature defence industry — systems integration, drone and combat-aircraft know-how, and the most prolific arms sector among the four. It is also expected to host most of the joint production facilities. Qatar brings capital and demand: its Ministry of Defence investment arm, Barzan Holdings, already signed a US$2.3 billion (about 16.6 billion yuan) joint venture with Indonesian firm Republikorp in January to modernise Jakarta's land and maritime systems. Pakistan and Indonesia bring manufacturing capacity, skilled workforces and sizeable domestic markets, plus hard-won experience in producing and maintaining complex platforms.

Indonesia is not starting from zero. It already co-produces the Kaplan MT medium tank with Turkey's FNSS — a 2019 deal that delivered 18 vehicles completed in 2024 — and last year agreed to buy 48 Turkish-made Kaan fifth-generation fighters in a deal estimated at over US$10 billion (about 72 billion yuan). It is now exploring a potential purchase of up to 40 JF-17 Thunder jets from Pakistan, though that remains exploratory. The Badr framework, in the words of researcher Muhammad Zulfikar Rahmat, delivers three concrete dividends: technology and know-how mostly from Turkey, funding from Qatar, and a wider market for Indonesian defence firms such as Pindad, PAL and Dirgantara Indonesia.

## The motive: shedding the West's political strings

Strip away the industrial language and the real driver is political. The Badr pact is, in the blunt phrasing of Andreas Krieg, a former British defence-ministry adviser to Qatar's armed forces, an attempt "to escape precisely this vulnerability" — the vulnerability of relying on Western suppliers whose export controls and political conditions determine "how and when those systems can be used." The Iran war, Krieg argues, exposed the limits of a regional security system built on external suppliers. Badr follows the Mecca Defence Alliance of Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, formed in early August 2026: Mecca offers the political promise of deterrence, while Badr could eventually supply the industrial means to sustain it.

For the four, the logic is not to abandon long-standing Western alliances but to gain greater control over their own security. As one analyst noted, closer relationships "give more countries a stake in each other's security" than declarations of friendship ever could.

## Indonesia's calculus: strategic autonomy first

Indonesia's motives differ from its partners'. Pakistan lives under the shadow of a nuclear-armed rivalry with India. Qatar's immediate concern is Iran. Turkey is repositioning itself as a defence exporter. Indonesia, by contrast, faces no imminent threat: it is far from the Middle East and is not a party to the South China Sea dispute. Its prime objective, observers stress, is strategic autonomy — the ability to build and sustain its own kit rather than rent it from great powers.

That is a long-held ambition in Jakarta, but one repeatedly frustrated by dependence on foreign original-equipment manufacturers. The Badr pact offers leverage to secure access to weapons and new defence deals across the Middle East, as Aberystwyth University's Ahmad Rizky Mardhatillah Umar notes. For a country that wants to be treated as a middle power, the appeal of a "buy-and-build" network outside the Western tent is obvious.

## Risk one: the technology ceiling

The most serious caveat is that Badr may not actually free its members from the West. Western technology remains indispensable for advanced propulsion, avionics, sensors and certain electronic components. Turkey's defence industry, for all its maturity, still depends on Western subsystems. And integrating new platforms into Indonesia's existing inventory is no small feat. The JF-17, co-produced by Pakistan with China, raises an awkward question: how does a Chinese-linked fighter fit alongside Indonesia's French Rafale jets and Turkish Kaan aircraft? As Muhammad put it, "the problem is that the JF-17 is made together with China." A pact that promises autonomy could instead deepen a different dependency.

## Risk two: India's wild card

Then there is the external reaction. Dave Des Roches, a former senior Pentagon official, calls the Badr MOU a politically significant "statement of desire" to acquire advanced capability. But he flags the India wildcard: New Delhi's Hindu-nationalist government could read Badr as evidence that Indonesia — the world's most populous Muslim-majority country — has been "enlisted as a military ally or partner" of Islamabad. Pakistan's recent air clash with India in May 2025 makes any such perception combustible. Even if the pact is purely industrial, the optics of an Indonesia–Pakistan defence embrace will be read in strategic, not commercial, terms in Delhi.

## A regional lesson for Southeast Asia

For Southeast Asia, Badr is a signal, not a threat. It shows a regional middle power trying to industrialise its defence the way it has industrialised its consumer economy — by networking, not by choosing a side. The lesson is double-edged. On one hand, it demonstrates that strategic autonomy is increasingly pursued through supply-chain building rather than bloc alignment, a posture ASEAN states formally embrace. On the other, it shows how hard that autonomy remains when the deepest technologies still sit behind Western export controls and Chinese co-production. Indonesia's "full moon" may illuminate the goal, but the road to genuine defence autonomy is still long, and littered with the same dependencies it set out to escape.

## Further reading

**Strategic autonomy (战略自主):** The principle that a state should control its own security and defence industrial base rather than rely on external powers; a recurring theme in Indonesian defence white papers.

**Mecca Defence Alliance (麦加防务联盟):** A political mutual-defence understanding signed in August 2026 by Turkey, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, complementing the industrial focus of the Badr pact.

**Kaan fighter jet (Kaan 战斗机):** Turkey's indigenously developed fifth-generation stealth aircraft; Indonesia agreed in 2025 to purchase 48 units in a deal worth over US$10 billion.
