# 火山灰与泥炭火：印尼"火环带宿命"下的治理考题

九月初，印度尼西亚同时被两场灾难夹击。苏门答腊与爪哇之间的巽他海峡，喀拉喀托之子火山（Anak Krakatau）自九月四日深夜起持续喷发整整二十五小时，火山灰柱一度冲高至一万五千米，雅加达苏加诺—哈达国际机场等七座机场被迫关闭；与此同时，受强厄尔尼诺与干旱高峰期叠加影响，西加里曼丹省的泥炭地与森林大火已鏖战数周，一线消防队连找水都成难题。一边是天降的火山灰，一边是地底阴燃的泥炭火，印尼这个"火环带"国家又一次被推到灾害治理的考场上。

## 双重灾情：火山灰封机场，泥炭火难扑灭

喀拉喀托之子火山的喷发并不算突然，但其猛烈程度仍令人咋舌。印尼地质局称，火山的长时连续喷发已告一段落，转为斯特龙博利式的中等强度间歇喷发，但未来一段时间再次发生大喷发的概率依然很高。真正麻烦的是火山灰的飘散路径正好压在人口最稠密、航线最密集的巽他海峡上空，雅加达、万丹省、西爪哇省万隆市以及苏门答腊南部楠榜省的机场相继关闭，仅南苏门答腊一座机场获准重开。

与"看得见"的火山灰相比，西加里曼丹的林火更像一场慢性消耗战。泥炭地火灾在土壤底下阴燃蔓延，极少引发地表明火，因此极难扑灭。据法新社报道，新当县消防局局长萨姆佩直言，团队正面临装备与水源的双重短缺：全县仅有两辆六千公升容量的消防车，消防软管也不够用，周边河流水位骤降后，队员不得不到数公里外的水沟找水。他说，大家在极高强度的扑救中还得先分头找水，"有时要一直灭火到天亮，甚至彻夜未眠"。这不是个别现象——西加里曼丹省目前有近一万四千五百名消防人员在现场，另有九架飞机空中洒水、三架巡逻、一架执行人工增雨，仍难阻火势。

## 数字：七座机场、三十四万旅客、上万亿盾预算

灾难的规模，最终都会落到数字上。印尼基础设施与区域发展统筹部长阿古斯公布，已有两千九百六十一趟航班延误、改道或取消，受影响旅客约三十四万一千人。新加坡航空公司取消了九月六日晚至九月八日凌晨往返新加坡与雅加达的二十趟航班，旗下廉航酷航也取消了十趟；为疏导需求，新航临时增开四趟往返东爪哇泗水的航班，滞留旅客改乘火车与接驳巴士转往其他机场。

更具深意的数字在预算端。国家减灾署的预算从去年的两万亿印尼盾（约一亿六千七百万新元，约合九亿元人民币），大幅削减到今年的四千九百一十亿印尼盾（约四千万新元，约合两亿两千万元人民币）。一个在六个受灾省份部署五十五架直升机与飞机、却只有九架属于军方与林业部的国家，救灾资金却被砍掉四分之三——这本身就是一道治理难题。

## 外援入场：日本直升机与意大利退役航母

当本国装备与水力双双吃紧，外援随之入场，也让这场救灾带上了地缘政治的色彩。据《雅加达环球报》报道，日本政府应印尼请求，已派遣海上自卫队国东号运输舰，运载三架CH-47"契努克"直升机及约六百名人员，预计九月九日或十日抵达西加里曼丹省，直接参与泥炭地与森林大火的投水作业。

更具象征意味的是那艘意大利退役航母。普拉博沃（Prabowo Subianto）指示国防部和军方，将刚获赠的加里波第号（印尼更名加查马达号）改装为专用的直升机与无人机移动基地，可容纳十架中型直升机，每架平均一次携带四至五吨水进行空中洒水。该舰目前仍在赴印尼途中，预计九月十九日抵达。把一艘原用于远洋作战的航母改为救火平台，既是应急之举，也折射出印尼自身空中灭火能力的缺口。

## 预算悖论：砍掉减灾款，再求外援救火

最值得玩味的是其中的预算悖论。就在减灾署预算被砍四分之三的同一年，印尼却不得不向日本求援直升机、把意大利退役航母改作救火基地。普拉博沃在九月七日的国家灾害管理会议上要求"大幅提高"减灾署预算，并直言印尼需要更多直升机和水泵。这话没错，但逻辑上绕了一个弯：先砍掉常态化的减灾投入，等灾难压顶再临时外借重装备，成本往往更高、反应也更慢。

更现实的短板是装备自给率。印尼在六省部署的五十五架直升机与飞机中，仅九架为军方与林业部自有，其余数十架全靠高价租赁。租赁体系在平日尚能运转，一旦多省同时受灾、全球租赁市场吃紧，缺口立刻暴露。外援能解燃眉之急，却填不满结构性亏空。

## 火山灰之外：印尼灾害治理的结构性短板

普拉博沃把这一切归于"宿命"——"我们地处太平洋火环带，这是我们的宿命。"这句话道出了地理现实，却也容易成为回避长期投入的借口。印尼坐落的火环带，决定了火山、地震、海啸与泥炭火将是周期性常态，而非偶发意外。真正的考题不在于一次救灾能否成功，而在于能否把"超前预防"从口号落到预算与机制里。

强厄尔尼诺正把林火季推向高峰，泥炭火燃起的烟霾历来有跨境传播的风险，邻国对此记忆犹新。当火山灰与烟霾可能在同一个窗口期叠加，单靠临时调机、外借航母显然不够。对印尼而言，提升减灾署的常态预算、补齐自有空中灭火力量、建立跨境烟霾联防，才是跳出"宿命论"的实在一步。否则，下一次火山灰封机场、泥炭火困住消防队的故事，恐怕还会重演。

## 延伸阅读

**喀拉喀托之子火山（Anak Krakatau）**：位于巽他海峡的活火山，1927年自1883年喀拉喀托大喷发后的残骸中重生，"Anak"在印尼语中意为"之子"，是印尼火环带最活跃、也最难预测的火山之一。

**泥炭地火灾（Peatland fire）**：泥炭层在地下阴燃、可绵延数月的特殊林火，扑灭极度困难，是东南亚旱季跨境烟霾的主要源头，与厄尔尼诺干旱高度相关。

**国家减灾署（BNPB）**：印度尼西亚负责灾害管理的国家机构，统筹预警、应急救援与重建，其预算规模直接决定全国救灾能力的上限。

# Ash and Peat Fire: Indonesia's Governance Test on the "Ring of Fire"

In early September, Indonesia found itself squeezed between two disasters at once. In the Sunda Strait between Sumatra and Java, Anak Krakatau erupted through the night of 4 September for a continuous twenty-five hours, its ash column once reaching 15,000 metres and forcing the closure of seven airports including Jakarta's Soekarno–Hatta International. At the same time, fuelled by the peak of a strong El Niño and drought, peatland and forest fires in West Kalimantan had been burning for weeks, with frontline crews struggling even to find water. One disaster fell from the sky as ash; the other smouldered underground as peat fire. Once again, this "Ring of Fire" nation was pushed into the examination hall of disaster governance.

## A double disaster: ash shuts airports, peat fires resist extinction

Anak Krakatau's eruption was not exactly a surprise, but its violence was still staggering. Indonesia's geological agency says the prolonged continuous eruption has paused and shifted to intermittent Strombolian activity of moderate intensity, yet the probability of another major eruption remains high. The real trouble is the drift path of the ash, which sits squarely over the Sunda Strait — the country's densest population belt and busiest air corridor. Airports in Jakarta, Banten, Bandung in West Java, and Lampung in southern Sumatra were shut in turn, with only one airport in South Sumatra allowed to reopen.

Compared with the visible ash, the West Kalimantan fires are more like a war of attrition. Peatland fires smoulder and spread beneath the soil, rarely producing surface flames, which makes them extraordinarily hard to extinguish. According to Agence France-Presse, the fire chief of Sintang district, Sampet, said plainly that his team faced a double shortage of equipment and water: the district had only two 6,000-litre fire trucks and not enough hoses, and as river levels dropped, crews had to fetch water from ditches several kilometres away. "While fighting at very high intensity, we still have to split up to look for water," he said, adding that they sometimes fought the fire until dawn or through the night. This is not an isolated case — across West Kalimantan nearly 14,500 firefighters are on the ground, supported by nine water-bombing aircraft, three patrol planes, and one cloud-seeding aircraft, yet the flames keep spreading.

## The numbers: seven airports, 341,000 passengers, trillions of rupiah

Disasters, in the end, always land on the numbers. Coordinating Minister for Infrastructure and Regional Development Agus reported 2,961 flights delayed, rerouted, or cancelled, affecting about 341,000 passengers. Singapore Airlines cancelled twenty Singapore–Jakarta flights from the evening of 6 September to the early hours of 8 September, and its low-cost arm Scoot cancelled ten more; to absorb demand, SIA added four Singapore–Surabaya flights, while stranded travellers switched to trains and shuttle buses to reach other airports.

The more telling figures sit on the budget side. The National Disaster Management Agency's budget was slashed from 2 trillion rupiah (about S$167 million, ~US$120 million) last year to 491 billion rupiah (about S$40 million, ~US$30 million) this year. A country that has deployed 55 helicopters and aircraft across six disaster-stricken provinces — but owns only nine of them through the military and forestry ministry — has cut its disaster funding by three-quarters. That, by itself, is a governance problem.

## Foreign assistance enters: Japanese helicopters and an Italian carrier

As domestic equipment and water ran short, foreign help arrived, and with it a geopolitical tint. According to the Jakarta Globe, at Indonesia's request the Japanese government has dispatched the Maritime Self-Defense Force transport ship Kunisaki, carrying three CH-47 Chinook helicopters and around 600 personnel, expected to reach West Kalimantan on 9 or 10 September to join water-bombing of the peat and forest fires.

More symbolic is the Italian retired aircraft carrier. President Prabowo has instructed the defence ministry and military to convert the recently donated Giuseppe Garibaldi — renamed Gajah Mada by Indonesia — into a dedicated mobile base for helicopters and drones, capable of hosting ten medium helicopters, each carrying four to five tonnes of water per sortie. The ship is still en route and is expected to arrive on 19 September. Turning a carrier built for blue-water combat into a firefighting platform is both an emergency measure and a quiet admission of Indonesia's gap in organic aerial firepower.

## The budget paradox: cutting disaster funds, then asking for help

The most revealing thread is the budget paradox. In the very year the disaster agency's budget was cut by three-quarters, Indonesia had to ask Japan for helicopters and convert an Italian retired carrier into a firefighting base. At the 7 September national disaster-management meeting, Prabowo demanded an immediate and "substantial" increase to the agency's budget, noting plainly that Indonesia needs more helicopters and water pumps. He is right — but the logic bends in a circle: slash routine disaster-prevention spending first, then, when catastrophe lands, borrow heavy equipment from abroad at higher cost and slower speed.

The more practical shortfall is self-sufficiency in hardware. Of the 55 helicopters and aircraft deployed across six provinces, only nine belong to the military and forestry ministry; the rest are expensive leases. The leasing system works in normal times, but the moment several provinces burn at once and the global lease market tightens, the gap shows immediately. Foreign aid eases the acute crisis but cannot fill a structural deficit.

## Beyond the ash: structural gaps in Indonesia's disaster governance

Prabowo frames all this as destiny — "We are on the Pacific Ring of Fire, this is our destiny." The line captures a geographic reality, yet it also risks becoming an excuse to avoid long-term investment. Indonesia's position on the Ring of Fire means volcanoes, earthquakes, tsunamis, and peat fires are cyclical constants, not one-off accidents. The real test is not whether a single relief effort succeeds, but whether "forward prevention" moves from slogan into budget and mechanism.

A strong El Niño is pushing the fire season to its peak, and peat fires have long been the main source of transboundary haze that neighbouring countries remember all too well. When volcanic ash and haze can overlap in the same window, temporarily borrowing aircraft and a foreign carrier is plainly not enough. For Indonesia, the concrete steps out of "destiny" are raising the disaster agency's baseline budget, building organic aerial firefighting capacity, and establishing cross-border haze coordination. Otherwise, the next story of ash shutting airports and peat fires trapping firefighters will likely repeat.

## Further reading

**Anak Krakatau**: An active volcano in the Sunda Strait, reborn in 1927 from the ruins of the 1883 Krakatau cataclysm; "Anak" means "child" in Indonesian. It is among the most active and least predictable volcanoes on Indonesia's Ring of Fire.

**Peatland fire**: A distinctive kind of forest fire in which the peat layer smoulders underground for months, making it extremely hard to extinguish. It is the primary source of transboundary haze during the Southeast Asian dry season and is closely tied to El Niño droughts.

**BNPB (National Disaster Management Agency)**: Indonesia's state agency responsible for disaster management, coordinating early warning, emergency response, and reconstruction. Its budget scale directly sets the ceiling on the nation's relief capacity.
