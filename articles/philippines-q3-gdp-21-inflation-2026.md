# 增速跌至2.1%：菲律宾经济为何在东南亚掉队？

第三季度国内生产总值（GDP）同比仅增2.1%，创下逾五年来最慢增速——《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》11月9日援引菲律宾（Philippines）统计局（PSA）数据的报道，给这个长期被贴上「东南亚（Southeast Asia）新星」标签的经济体泼了一盆冷水。更刺眼的是，这是菲律宾连续第五个季度减速：从去年同期的4%，到今年二季度的2.4%，再到如今的三季度2.1%，下滑曲线清晰得令人不安。若剔除疫情造成的异常波动，2.1%甚至是2009年第四季度以来的最低水平。一个被寄望承接全球产业链转移、搭上人工智能（AI）顺风车的国家，为何在增长赛道上率先掉队？答案藏在结构、通胀与治理的三重夹缝里。

## 数字：2.1% 与五年最弱

菲律宾统计局11月9日公布的数据并不复杂：三季度国内生产总值（GDP）同比增长2.1%，低于市场预期的2.8%（据《菲律宾询问者报》对14位经济学家的调查中位数），也低于二季度的2.4%。经季节调整后，环比仅增0.1%，较二季度的0.6%进一步走弱。前三季度累计增长2.5%，而政府年初定下的全年目标是3.5%至4.5%——这意味着要在剩余一个季度把增速拉回5%以上，几乎是不可能完成的任务。国民总收入（GNI）同比增长2.7%，与国内生产总值（GDP）大体同步，说明问题不在统计口径，而在实体经济本身。

## 结构：谁在拖累，谁在支撑

拆开三季度的行业账，疲态一目了然。农业、林业和渔业同比下滑4.8%，而一年前这一板块还增长2.9%——台风与气候扰动之外，更深层的是农业部门长期投资不足。工业几乎原地踏步（微降0.003%），去年同期尚增0.7%。唯一还算稳健的服务业增长3.7%，却也远低于去年同期的5.5%。

支出端则暴露了更关键的矛盾：居民最终消费仅增2.4%，资本形成总额不增反降0.2%，说明企业与家庭都在收缩。真正撑起全场的是政府最终消费支出，增长6.8%——换句话说，这一季度的增长，主要靠财政花钱顶着。货物和服务出口增长8.6%、进口增长7.7%，外贸还算活跃，但净贡献有限。

## 通胀：被油价掐住的经济命脉

为什么家庭不敢花钱？答案在油价里。菲律宾约九成原油依赖进口，而自今年2月美以对伊朗发动袭击、引发全球能源震荡以来，国际油价反复冲高，直接推升了国内的燃油与交通成本。能源价格以美元计价，菲律宾比索的每一次贬值都让进口账单更沉重。通胀抬头，真实购买力被侵蚀，居民消费自然趋于保守。一个国家把经济命脉系在别人家的油井上，一旦中东（the Middle East）生变，首当其冲的就是马尼拉（Manila）街头的菜价与通勤费。这并非偶发冲击，而是结构性的软肋。

## 半导体的「唯一亮点」能撑多久

《日经亚洲》援引经济学家的判断，把人工智能（AI）驱动的半导体出口称为菲律宾经济「唯一的亮点」。这话不假，却也透着无奈——当其他引擎集体熄火，唯一还在转的轮子，反而显得格外单薄。电子与半导体出口长期占菲律宾总出口的四到五成，是美国人工智能（AI）算力扩张的直接受益者；但这类出口高度集中于少数出口加工区和跨国企业，对广大普通劳动者的就业与收入的带动相当有限。更值得警惕的是，它把菲律宾的景气与否，绑在了华盛顿的科技周期与对华科技管制的指挥棒上。

## 财政独木难支与反腐阴影

政府消费增长6.8%是三季度的定海神针，但财政独木难支。一方面，公共开支本就受限于财政空间；另一方面，今年以来围绕防洪工程等基建项目的腐败丑闻，不仅侵蚀了政府公信力，也拖慢了支出落地与基建投资节奏。当地媒体将放缓部分归因于腐败丑闻与中东冲突——前者伤信心，后者伤油价。当「花钱稳增长」既缺钱又缺信任，政策工具箱其实比账面数字更局促。

## 区域对照：掉队的菲律宾

把镜头拉远，菲律宾的掉队尤为显眼。同一时期，越南（Vietnam）三季度国内生产总值（GDP）同比增长近10%，印度尼西亚（Indonesia）三季度增长5.47%，马来西亚（Malaysia）上半年增速约6%——菲律宾2.1%的季度表现，在主要东盟（ASEAN）经济体中几乎垫底。较早前曾有机构预测菲律宾全年增速在区域内最慢，如今看来并非空穴来风。承接产业转移、友岸外包、吕宋经济走廊等叙事固然动听，但当全球需求转弱、能源价格高企，口号终要回到增长数字上接受检验。

## 全年目标：一道难解的算术题

政府已将2026年增长目标从年初的5%至6%下调至3.5%至4.5%，但前三季度仅2.5%的现实，让即便是下限也显得遥远。要在四季度把全年拉到3.5%，单季增速需逼近5.5%以上，而在当前动能下几乎不可想象。菲律宾央行（BSP）若出于通胀压力维持偏紧的货币政策，又会进一步压制内需。增长、通胀、汇率三难之间，决策层的回旋空间正被快速压缩。

## 侨汇减震器与结构性软肋

不过，正如历次下行所印证的，菲律宾经济仍有一道传统减震器——海外务工人员的侨汇。常年规模约400亿美元（约2880亿元人民币）的侨汇，是千百万家庭消费的重要支撑，也是比任何财政刺激都更稳定的现金流。这道由离散族群撑起的缓冲，让菲律宾在风雨中不至于失速。但减震器终归只是减震：它掩盖不了农业孱弱、工业停滞、投资萎缩与能源命门受制于人的结构性软肋。2.1%不是一次偶然的颠簸，而是一记提醒——光靠外部周期与侨汇输血，撑不起一个真正稳健的经济体。

## 延伸阅读

**国民总收入（GNI）**：衡量一国居民（含境外要素收入）创造的总收入，与国内生产总值（GDP）的差别在于是否计入来自国外的净要素收入；菲律宾三季度国民总收入（GNI）同比增长2.7%。

**友岸外包（Friend-shoring）**：将供应链集中于政治与安全盟友国家的策略；菲律宾借吕宋经济走廊等框架承接部分从中国大陆转出的制造与半导体产能。

**海外务工者（OFW）**：菲律宾赴海外就业的劳动力，其汇回的侨汇长期占经济总量近一成，是家庭消费与宏观稳定的关键变量。

# Philippines Grows Just 2.1% in Q3 2026 — Why Manila Is Losing the Regional Race

The Philippines' gross domestic product (GDP) expanded only 2.1% year on year in the third quarter of 2026, the weakest pace in more than five years, according to data from the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) reported by Nikkei Asia on November 9. The figure is more than a soft patch; it is the fifth consecutive quarter of deceleration. Growth has slid from 4% a year earlier, to 2.4% in the second quarter, to 2.1% now — a clean, worrying downward slope. Stripping out the distortions of the pandemic, 2.1% is the lowest since the fourth quarter of 2009. A country long branded a "rising star" of Southeast Asia, and expected to absorb supply-chain relocation and ride the artificial intelligence (AI) wave, is instead the first to stumble. The reasons lie in the triple squeeze of structure, inflation and governance.

## The numbers: 2.1% and the weakest in five years

The PSA release on November 9 was straightforward: third-quarter GDP rose 2.1% from a year earlier, below the 2.8% median forecast of 14 economists surveyed by the Inquirer, and below the 2.4% recorded in the second quarter. On a seasonally adjusted basis, output grew just 0.1% quarter on quarter, down from 0.6% in the prior period. Growth for the first nine months came in at 2.5%, while the government's full-year target — already cut from 5–6% to 3.5–4.5% — now looks unreachable without a near-impossible rebound above 5% in the final quarter. Gross national income (GNI) rose 2.7%, tracking GDP closely and confirming that the problem is the real economy, not a statistical artifact.

## The composition: what dragged, what held up

The sectoral breakdown exposes the malaise. Agriculture, forestry and fisheries contracted 4.8% year on year, against a 2.9% expansion a year earlier — a result of climate shocks and, more fundamentally, chronic underinvestment in the farm sector. Industry was essentially flat (down 0.003%), compared with 0.7% growth a year before. The only relatively resilient pillar, services, grew 3.7%, yet that too was far below the 5.5% of a year earlier.

The expenditure side reveals the deeper contradiction. Household final consumption rose a meagre 2.4%, and gross capital formation actually fell 0.2% — households and firms are both retrenching. The single engine carrying the quarter was government final consumption, up 6.8%. In other words, this quarter's growth was propped up largely by public spending. Exports of goods and services rose 8.6% and imports 7.7%, leaving only a limited net contribution.

## Inflation: an economy held hostage by oil prices

Why are households holding back? Look at the oil price. The Philippines imports around 90% of its crude oil, and since the U.S.–Israeli strikes on Iran in February 2026 triggered a global energy shock, international crude has swung higher repeatedly, pushing up domestic fuel and transport costs. Because energy is priced in dollars, every depreciation of the Philippine peso makes the import bill heavier. Inflation erodes real purchasing power, and consumption turns defensive. An economy that ties its lifeblood to someone else's wells is the first to feel the pinch when the Middle East convulses. This is not a one-off shock; it is a structural vulnerability.

## The semiconductor "only bright spot" — how durable?

Nikkei Asia quoted an economist who called AI-driven semiconductor exports the economy's "only bright spot." The label is accurate, but it also betrays a precarious reliance: when every other engine has stalled, the single wheel still turning looks exceptionally thin. Electronics and semiconductor shipments have long accounted for 40–50% of total Philippine exports and are direct beneficiaries of Washington's AI compute build-out. Yet this export base is concentrated in a handful of export-processing zones and multinational firms, with only limited spillover to broad-based employment and household income. More worrying, it tethers the Philippines' fortunes to the rhythm of U.S. technology cycles and Washington's export controls on China.

## Fiscal stimulus alone, and the corruption overhang

Government consumption growth of 6.8% was the anchor of the quarter, but a single pillar cannot bear the whole roof. Public spending is constrained by fiscal space, and this year's corruption scandals surrounding flood-control and infrastructure projects have not only damaged public trust but also slowed the execution of expenditure and investment. Local media attributed part of the slowdown to the corruption scandals and the Middle East conflict — the former undermining confidence, the latter lifting oil prices. When "spend-to-stabilise" is short on both money and trust, the policy toolkit is more constrained than the headline figures suggest.

## Regional contrast: the laggard of Southeast Asia

Zoom out, and the Philippines' underperformance stands out. Over the same period, Vietnam's third-quarter GDP grew close to 10%, Indonesia's rose 5.47%, and Malaysia expanded around 6% in the first half — the Philippines' 2.1% quarterly print sits near the bottom among major ASEAN economies. Earlier forecasts that pegged the Philippines as the slowest grower in the region no longer look like outliers. Narratives of supply-chain relocation, friend-shoring and the Luzon Economic Corridor sound compelling, but when global demand softens and energy prices stay elevated, slogans must ultimately answer to the growth number.

## The annual target: an arithmetic puzzle

The government has already trimmed its 2026 growth target from 5–6% to 3.5–4.5%, yet the 2.5% first-nine-months print makes even the lower bound distant. To reach 3.5% for the full year, the fourth quarter would need growth near or above 5.5% — almost unimaginable under current momentum. If the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) keeps monetary policy tight to fight inflation, it will further suppress domestic demand. Caught among growth, inflation and exchange-rate pressures, policymakers' room to manoeuvre is shrinking fast.

## The remittance cushion and structural soft spots

Yet, as in every past downturn, the Philippine economy retains a traditional shock absorber: the remittances of overseas Filipino workers. Running at roughly US$40 billion a year, these flows underpin the consumption of millions of families and constitute a steadier cash stream than any fiscal stimulus. This buffer, built by the diaspora, keeps the Philippines from veering off course in rough weather. But a shock absorber is only that: it cannot hide the structural soft spots — a weak farm sector, stagnant industry, shrinking investment and an energy lifeline exposed to foreign control. The 2.1% print is not an accidental bump; it is a reminder that an economy relying on external cycles and remittance transfusions alone cannot stand truly robust.

## Further reading

**Gross National Income (GNI):** the total income earned by a country's residents, including net factor income from abroad; it differs from GDP in whether cross-border factor income is counted. The Philippines' Q3 GNI rose 2.7% year on year.

**Friend-shoring:** the strategy of concentrating supply chains within politically and security-aligned countries; the Philippines, through frameworks such as the Luzon Economic Corridor, has absorbed some manufacturing and semiconductor capacity relocated from mainland China.

**Overseas Filipino Workers (OFW):** Filipino labour employed abroad, whose remittances have long accounted for nearly a tenth of the economy and are a key variable for household consumption and macroeconomic stability.
