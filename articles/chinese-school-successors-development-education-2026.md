# 华校二代接力改写华文教育：从留住根到发展型教育

据中新社报道，11月18日至19日，第六届世界华文教育大会在海南博鳌举行，来自70多个国家和地区的近600名代表围绕“新格局、新华教、新发展”展开讨论。新华社的通稿强调标准化、正规化、专业化、信息化，以及“讲好中国故事”。但这场会议上真正值得细读的，不是主旨发言，而是几位华校二代接班人讲出的具体变化：他们正在把中文学校从族群文化传承的封闭空间，改造成面向当地社会的技能平台与流动通道。这不是修辞上的调整，而是海外华文教育的一次功能性转向。

## 从留住根到知文化信文化：办学目标换了内核

西班牙哆来咪文化艺术学校校长翁佳艺2015年从母亲手中接过学校。她为学生设计了“语言+文化+艺术”课程，又搭建网络平台，鼓励学生自己撰稿、拍摄短视频，用中文讲述身边故事。她对中新社说，老一辈创办人建校，大多以守住文化、留住根脉为目标；新一代青年校长在守住根脉的基础上，更侧重让中华文化变得鲜活、有吸引力，让孩子们“知文化、信文化”。

这番话点破了一个长期被回避的事实：华裔二代、三代早已不满足于枯燥的识字与背诵。以“留住根”为目标的教育，预设了一个前提——学生认同感的缺口可以用记忆填补；但当一个孩子出生在马德里、日常语言是西班牙语，让他记住几个汉字并不能自动产生认同。转向“知文化、信文化”，是把教育目标从“保存”改为“说服”：文化不能靠血统自动继承，必须靠可体验、可表达的内容重新赢得年轻一代的认可。这个转变看似温和，实则把华校从家族事务变成了需要持续投入内容生产的教育机构。

## 缅甸样本：非华裔生源倒逼三语教学

缅甸东方学校董事长李丽清同样是被母亲感召的华校第二代。她的母亲曾在缅甸边远地区支教，让华裔后代有书可读。如今生源结构变了，越来越多非华裔学生走进华校，李丽清随即调整办学思路：华裔周末班重点教拼音、国学与书法，夯实母语基础；全日制私立中学的学生大多不是华裔，则推行缅甸语、中文、英语三语教学，课程对接当地主流教学框架，照顾本土学生的升学诉求。

值得留意的是，学校教师今年自主编写了《缅甸华校高中本土化语文实验教材》，内容涵盖中国古典文学、中国现当代文学与缅甸华文文学。教材编写权从北京（Beijing）下移到一线教师，这不是简单的教学技巧问题，而是课程主权的重新分配。当华校要同时服务华裔与非华裔学生，统一教材既无法回应本地升学考试，也无法解释“缅甸华文文学”为何属于中文课堂。学校的应对是把课程本土化——把学生熟悉的生活经验写进文本，让中文学习与当地教育体系发生真实关系。

## 标准化与本土化：总部话语与一线实践的距离

第六届世界华文教育大会提出推进华文教育标准化、正规化、专业化、信息化建设。方向本身没有错，问题在于“标准化”到底意味着什么。如果标准化是教材、师资认证与教学评估的最低门槛，它为散落各国的华校提供了公共品；如果标准化被理解为统一内容、统一口径，它恰恰会削弱华校在当地的生存能力。

缅甸东方学校的做法给出了一个务实的答案：核心能力可以标准化，课程叙事必须本土化。拼音、汉字、文学常识是共同底座，但选用哪些文本、怎样对接本土升学、如何解释华文文学在缅甸的存在，只能由熟悉当地学情的教师决定。总部与一线之间的距离，不必视为对立——关键在于把标准当作地板而不是天花板。假如大会的倡议最终只落成一套统一的考核指标，而忽视了华校在不同国家面对的完全不同的制度环境，那么“标准化”很可能会加速而非减缓华校的边缘化。

## 泰国（Thailand）管道：把中文接进就业链

泰国华文教师公会推动华校与职业学校、企业合作办学，形成了一套清晰的贯通路径：学生在华校完成初中教育后，分别在泰国本地职业学校和中国职业学校各学习一年，随后进入中资企业实习；在岗期间还可以继续攻读本科。公会理事会主席罗铁英对中新社表示，父辈投身华文教育，首要目标是生存，是在重重困境中守护中文火种；新一代华教工作者立足本土，推动华文教育从语言文化传承转向面向全社会的发展型教育。据中新社报道，泰国教育部也鼓励华校增加中文课时、开设“中文+专业”课程。

这条管道的意义在于，它让中文第一次脱离了“祖辈的语言”这一身份标签，成为可兑换的就业技能。对于泰国学生而言，学中文不再是为了向父母交代，而是为了进入中资企业的招聘名单。但风险同样清楚：当中文教育的吸引力高度依赖中资企业的用工需求，其可持续性就与投资周期绑定。一旦用工收缩，课程价值会迅速缩水。所谓“在岗攻读本科”，也意味着学校正在把教育环节部分嵌入企业的人力资源流程。这不是坏事，却要求教育者守住一条底线——华校不能沦为企业的岗前培训班，否则“发展型教育”就退化成了订单式培训。

## 接班人的算术：从族群传承到社会流动

把西班牙、缅甸、泰国的案例放在一起看，华校二代接班人的共同逻辑很清楚：他们接手的不是一份祖产，而是一个需要在当地教育市场中重新证明自身价值的机构。父辈的算术是“守住火种”，只要中文还在课堂上被读出声，使命就算完成；新一代的算术是“扩大入口”，只有当非华裔学生愿意来、家长认为有用、企业愿意录用，华文教育才真正站得住。

这也重新定义了海外华文教育的评价尺度。过去常见的问题是“海外华人还懂不懂中文”，今后更该问的是“中文在学校体系、劳动力市场与公共生活中占什么位置”。据新华社报道，大会号召让各国人民通过华文教育了解中国；但从缅甸的本土教材到泰国的实习管道可以看出，真正有效的传播恰恰发生在具体而微的地方——一门能帮学生升学的课，一份能拿到的实习合同，远比抽象的叙事更有说服力。华文教育的下一次增长，很可能不来自侨社的血缘动员，而来自它在当地制度中扎下的那些细小而真实的连接。

## 延伸阅读

**发展型教育（Development-oriented Education）**：指华文教育从语言文化传承转向服务当地学生的升学、就业与社会流动，评价重心由“是否守住根脉”转为“是否提升现实机会”。

**课程本土化（Curriculum Localisation）**：一线教师依据所在国学情、考试制度与文化环境自主编写及调整教材，与总部推动的标准化形成分层而非对立的关系。

**中文+职业技能（Chinese-plus-Vocational Skills）**：把中文教学与职业能力培训结合的办学模式，常见于东南亚（Southeast Asia）华校与职业学校、企业的合作项目，其可持续性受当地用工周期影响。

# Second-Generation School Leaders Are Rewriting Chinese-Language Education in Southeast Asia

Chinese-language education abroad is usually discussed as a question of heritage: can a diaspora transmit its language to children raised somewhere else? The Sixth World Conference on Chinese Language Education, held in Bo'ao, Hainan, on 18–19 November, suggested a different framing. According to China News Service, nearly 600 delegates from more than 70 countries and regions gathered under the theme of "new landscape, new education, new development". Xinhua's readout emphasised standardisation, professionalisation and "telling China's story well". Yet the most consequential material came not from the keynote speeches but from second-generation school operators in Myanmar and Thailand, who described how they are turning Chinese-language schools from enclosed spaces of ethnic memory into skills platforms and channels of social mobility. This is a functional redefinition, not a change of slogan.

## From roots to belief: a change of purpose

Weng Jiayi, principal of Spain's Duolaimi Culture and Arts School, took over the institution from her mother in 2015. She designed a "language plus culture plus arts" curriculum and built an online platform where students write their own pieces and shoot short videos, telling stories from their own lives in Chinese. She told China News Service that the founding generation built schools mainly to preserve culture and keep roots alive, whereas the new generation of young principals, while holding on to those roots, places more weight on making Chinese culture vivid and attractive, so that children "know the culture and believe in it".

That distinction exposes a long-avoided fact: second- and third-generation descendants are no longer satisfied with rote memorisation. An education aimed at "keeping roots" rests on an assumption — that a deficit of identity can be filled by memory. But for a child born in Madrid whose daily language is Spanish, remembering a few characters does not automatically produce identification. Shifting the goal to "knowing and believing" changes the task from preservation to persuasion. Culture cannot be inherited automatically through blood; it has to win the assent of younger people through experiences and forms of expression they can actually use. The change sounds modest. In practice it turns a Chinese school from a family affair into an educational institution that must keep producing content worth paying attention to.

## Myanmar's classroom: when non-Chinese students are the majority

Li Liqing, chair of Myanmar's Oriental School, is another second-generation successor, drawn into Chinese-language education by her mother, who once taught in remote parts of the country so that descendants of Chinese migrants would have somewhere to study. The student body has since changed. More and more non-Chinese pupils are enrolling, and Li adjusted the school's model accordingly. Weekend classes for children of Chinese descent focus on pinyin, classical studies and calligraphy to build a mother-tongue foundation. The full-time private secondary school, where most students are not Chinese, uses a trilingual curriculum — Burmese, Chinese and English — aligned with the mainstream national framework so that local pupils can progress to higher education.

One detail deserves more attention than it received. The school's teachers compiled their own textbook this year, an experimental locally adapted literature reader for Chinese classes at upper-secondary level, covering classical and modern Chinese writing alongside Chinese-language literature from Myanmar. The authority to write textbooks has moved from the centre to the classroom. That is not a matter of teaching technique; it is a redistribution of curricular sovereignty. A school serving both Chinese and non-Chinese students cannot rely on a uniform imported text to prepare pupils for local examinations, nor can it explain why Burmese Chinese-language writing belongs in a Chinese lesson. The response has been to localise the curriculum — to put students' own experience into the texts and make Chinese learning part of a real relationship with the local education system.

## Standards and localisation: the distance between headquarters and the classroom

The Bo'ao conference called for standardised, regularised, professionalised and digitally enabled Chinese-language education. The direction is sound; the question is what "standardisation" is taken to mean. If it means minimum thresholds for textbooks, teacher credentials and assessment, it supplies a public good to schools scattered across very different systems. If it means uniform content and a single line of interpretation, it will erode precisely the local usefulness on which those schools depend.

Myanmar's Oriental School offers a practical answer: core competencies can be standardised, while curricular narratives must be localised. Pinyin, characters and literary fundamentals form a common base; which texts to read, how to prepare students for local examinations, and how to account for Chinese-language writing in Myanmar are decisions that only teachers familiar with local conditions can make. The gap between headquarters and the classroom need not be read as conflict. The trick is to treat standards as a floor rather than a ceiling. If the conference's proposal ultimately hardens into one uniform set of indicators, ignoring the institutional environments that differ sharply from one country to the next, standardisation could accelerate the marginalisation of Chinese-language schools rather than prevent it.

## Thailand's pipeline: wiring Chinese into the labour market

Thailand's Chinese Teachers Association has promoted cooperation between Chinese-language schools, vocational schools and companies, producing a legible pathway. After finishing junior secondary at a Chinese school, a student spends one year at a Thai vocational school and one year at a Chinese vocational school, then enters an internship with a Chinese-invested company; while working, the student can continue towards an undergraduate degree. Luo Tieying, chair of the association's council, told China News Service that the founding generation entered Chinese-language education to survive, guarding the flame of the language under pressure, whereas the new generation works from within local society and is moving the field from cultural transmission towards development-oriented education for the whole of society. China News Service also reported that Thailand's Ministry of Education encourages Chinese schools to increase class hours and open "Chinese plus specialised subject" courses.

The significance of this pipeline is that it detaches Chinese from the label of "the language of grandparents" and turns it into a skill with convertible value. For a Thai student, learning Chinese is no longer a favour to one's parents; it is a route onto the recruitment lists of Chinese firms. The risk is equally clear. Once the appeal of Chinese-language education depends heavily on hiring by Chinese-invested companies, its sustainability becomes tied to investment cycles. When hiring contracts, the value of the course shrinks quickly. The provision to study for a degree while on the job also means that schools are embedding part of their educational function inside corporate human-resources processes. That is not objectionable in itself, but it sets a boundary worth defending: a Chinese school must not become a corporate induction programme, or "development-oriented education" degrades into contract training.

## The successors' calculus: from ethnic continuity to social mobility

Set the Spanish, Myanmar and Thai cases side by side and a common logic emerges. Second-generation successors are not inheriting a family asset; they are taking charge of an institution that must justify its value again in a local education market. The founding generation's arithmetic was "keep the flame alive" — as long as Chinese was still read aloud in class, the mission was fulfilled. The new generation's arithmetic is "widen the entrance": Chinese-language education stands only when non-Chinese students are willing to enrol, parents see a return, and employers are prepared to hire.

That reframes how the field should be evaluated. The familiar question — do overseas Chinese still understand Chinese? — matters less than a different one: what position does Chinese occupy in school systems, labour markets and public life? Xinhua reported the conference's call for people in other countries to understand China through Chinese-language education. Yet the Myanmar textbook and the Thai internship route suggest that effective communication happens at a much smaller scale: a course that helps a pupil advance, a contract a student can actually sign. Those are more persuasive than abstract narrative. The next phase of growth in Chinese-language education is unlikely to come from kinship-based mobilisation inside the diaspora. It will come from the small, real connections the schools have built into local institutions.

## Further reading

**Development-oriented education**: A shift in Chinese-language schooling from cultural transmission towards serving local students' progression, employment and social mobility; the measure of success becomes expanded opportunity rather than preserved continuity.

**Curriculum localisation**: The practice of teachers adapting or writing textbooks in response to the host country's examinations, culture and classroom conditions, forming a layered relationship with headquarters' standardisation rather than opposing it.

**Chinese-plus-vocational skills**: Models combining language teaching with job training, common in Southeast Asian Chinese schools working with vocational institutions and firms; their sustainability is exposed to local hiring cycles.
