# 老挝债务绷紧：铁路梦如何成了资产负债表噩梦

2026年3月，国际货币基金组织（IMF）给老挝下了一个冷冰冰的判定：债务「不可持续」。彼时，这个被群山锁住的内陆国刚把中老铁路跑成区域互联互通的样板，却也把自己跑进了一个更深的财务窟窿。标普（S&P）随后给出 CCC+ 的垃圾级主权评级，通胀高悬、基普（老挝货币）一路贬值、外债偿付压得财政喘不过气。一个问题挥之不去：当一条铁路既是一国的命脉，又成为债权方的筹码，内陆国的发展叙事，究竟是谁在书写？

## 数字：站在悬崖边的资产负债表

老挝的经济体量小得惊人。2025年其国内生产总值约183亿美元（约1318亿元人民币），全国人口不到800万。可就是这样一块「小蛋糕」，却背上了与其体量极不相称的债务。政府债务在2022年曾冲到国内生产总值的115.7%，虽随后回落，世界银行2026年6月仍提示其债务负担沉重；标普的 CCC+ 评级意味着违约风险已高到市场几乎不愿碰触。更刺眼的是利息成本：为压制9.6% 的通胀，老挝央行把政策利率顶到22.61%，实际利率高达约13个百分点——借钱续命的成本，本身就成了压垮财政的另一块石头。

## IMF 的定性：「不可持续」

「不可持续」这四个字，从国际货币基金组织（IMF）口中说出来分量极重。它意味着当前的借新还旧模式已难以为继，若不重组，违约只是时间问题。对老挝而言，麻烦在于债权人高度集中：中国既是最大双边债权方，也是最大贸易伙伴与买家。西方舆论乐于把它讲成「债务陷阱」——即北京以贷款换港口、换影响力的叙事。但真相要复杂得多：老挝的债务里既有中国政策性银行的优惠贷，也有其自身财政纪律松弛、税基薄弱、长期依靠外援与投资的老毛病。把一切推给「外部设局」，既简化了因果链条，也放过了老挝自身的治理短板。

## 一条铁路：既是命脉也是筹码

中老铁路是这场债务叙事里最矛盾的符号。自2021年底通车至2026年6月1日，这条线累计发送旅客逾7300万人次、货运逾8400万吨，其中跨境货物逾1900万吨——它确实把老挝从「陆锁国」推向「陆联国」，让农产品、矿产更快进入中国市场。但硬币的另一面是：铁路本身由巨额中方融资支撑，还本付息的压力，正是「不可持续」判定的重要注脚。铁路带来客流与货流，也带来账单。对万象而言，这是一笔不得不算的账：通了路，不等于脱了困。

## 重新审视「中国因素」

把老挝的困境简单归咎于中国，并不公允。北京确实是其最大债主，但也是其最大买家与基建伙伴；中国对老挝的投资与贷款，与其说是「陷阱」，不如说是一把双刃剑——它填补了西方资本不愿进入的空白，却也让老挝的财政与外部融资高度绑定单一对手方。真正的风险，是「一家独大」带来的议价失衡，而非贷款本身。老挝要做的，是在享受互联互通红利的同时，把债权结构多元化、把税基做实、把项目从「贷款驱动」转向「收入驱动」。

## 毕业悖论

2026年11月24日，老挝将正式脱离联合国（UN）「最不发达国家」名单。这本该是件值得庆祝的事，却透着荒诞：一个刚被国际货币基金组织（IMF）判为债务「不可持续」的国家，转眼要「毕业」了。毕业意味着优惠贸易待遇与援助的逐步退场，外部融资环境只会更紧。发展阶段的跃升，反而可能掐断最廉价的生命线。对老挝来说，这是一道残酷的算术题：名义上「升级」了，口袋里却更空了。

## 被围困的货币

基普的命运，是这场危机最直观的体温计。为对抗近10% 的通货膨胀，老挝央行把利率拉到22.61%，可高利率又扼住本就脆弱的投资与消费；货币持续贬值，进口更贵、外债更沉。普通老挝人感受到的，是物价一天比一天高、工资却追不上的日常。一个被债务与汇率双重夹击的经济，最难的不是某一项指标，而是所有指标同时恶化、互为因果。

## 对区域意味着什么

老挝不是孤例。从中老铁路到中泰铁路，从皎漂港到各类产业园区，中国—东盟的互联互通正以贷款与基建为骨架铺开。老挝的债务困境，给这一宏大叙事敲了警钟：当「硬联通」跑在「软治理」前面，项目越多，风险敞口越大。对东盟整体而言，一个深陷债务的内陆成员国，既是区域一体化的短板，也可能成为外部地缘博弈的支点。怎样让连通真正带来收入而非只是账单，是整个中南半岛都要回答的题。

## 前路

老挝的解法，不在关掉铁路，而在把账算清。债务重组、债权人协调、把优惠贷转化为能产生现金流的项目、在「毕业」前夯实财政——这些都不性感，却决定生死。外部世界与其争论「陷阱」与否，不如帮老挝把造血能力建起来。毕竟，一条再漂亮的铁路，若终点站是违约，那它载不动一个国家的明天。

## 延伸阅读

**主权信用评级（Sovereign Credit Rating）**：标普、穆迪、惠誉对一国政府偿债能力的评级，CCC+ 属「垃圾级」，意味违约风险高。

**最不发达国家（Least Developed Countries）**：联合国认定的发展最薄弱经济体群体，享优惠贸易与援助；老挝定于2026年11月24日退出该名单。

**中老铁路（China–Laos Railway）**：连接中国昆明与老挝万象的准轨铁路，2021年底通车，是泛亚铁路中线关键一段。

# Laos's Debt Squeeze: How a Railway Dream Became a Balance-Sheet Nightmare

In March 2026 the IMF delivered a blunt verdict on Laos: its debt is "unsustainable." The landlocked, mountain-ringed state had just turned the China–Laos Railway into a showcase of regional connectivity — and run itself into a far deeper financial hole. S&P followed with a CCC+ junk rating; inflation is stuck near double digits, the kip keeps sliding, and debt service is suffocating the budget. One question lingers: when a single railway is both a country's lifeline and its creditor's leverage, who exactly is writing the development story?

## The numbers: a balance sheet on the edge

Laos is tiny by any measure. Its 2025 GDP was about US$18.3 billion (roughly 132 billion yuan), spread across fewer than 8 million people. Yet this small cake carries debt wildly out of proportion to its size. Government debt peaked at 115.7% of GDP in 2022; it has since eased, but the World Bank warned in June 2026 that the burden remains heavy. S&P's CCC+ rating signals the market barely trusts Laos to repay. The cruelest line item is interest: to fight 9.6% inflation, the central bank has pushed its policy rate to 22.61%, leaving a real rate near 13 percentage points. Borrowing to stay alive now costs more than the economy can comfortably bear.

## The IMF's verdict: 'unsustainable'

"Unsustainable" is a loaded word from the IMF. It means the borrow-to-roll-over model no longer works and, absent restructuring, default is only a matter of time. Laos's problem is a concentrated creditor base: China is simultaneously its largest bilateral lender, its biggest trade partner, and its top buyer. Western commentary loves the "debt trap" frame — Beijing using loans to extract ports and influence. The truth is messier. Laos's debt blends Chinese policy-bank concessional loans with its own chronic weaknesses: loose fiscal discipline, a thin tax base, and a long habit of living off aid and external investment. Blaming it all on an outside "setup" simplifies a tangled chain of cause and effect and lets Vientiane off the hook for its own governance gaps.

## A railway as lifeline and leverage

The China–Laos Railway is the most contradictory symbol in this story. From its late-2021 opening through 1 June 2026, it carried over 73 million passengers and more than 84 million tonnes of freight, including over 19 million tonnes crossing borders. It has genuinely moved Laos from "land-locked" toward "land-linked," speeding farm goods and minerals into Chinese markets. But the flip side: the line itself rests on huge Chinese financing, and the debt service it obliges is a major footnote to the "unsustainable" verdict. The railway brings passengers and freight — and a bill. For Vientiane the arithmetic is unavoidable: building the road is not the same as escaping the hole.

## The China question, reframed

Reducing Laos's predicament to China is unfair. Beijing is indeed its biggest creditor, but also its biggest buyer and infrastructure partner. Chinese loans and investment in Laos are less a "trap" than a double-edged gift — they fill the gap Western capital will not touch, yet they bind Vientiane's finances and external financing to a single counterparty. The real risk is the bargaining imbalance that "all eggs in one basket" creates, not the loans per se. What Laos must do is diversify its creditor base, broaden its tax base, and shift projects from "loan-driven" to "revenue-driven" while still harvesting the connectivity dividend.

## The graduation paradox

On 24 November 2026, Laos is scheduled to graduate from the UN's Least Developed Countries list. This should be a cause for celebration — yet it rings hollow. A state the IMF just called debt-"unsustainable" is about to "graduate." Graduation means preferential trade terms and aid gradually withdraw, tightening external financing further. The leap in development stage may simply cut off the cheapest lifeline. For Laos the arithmetic is brutal: it is "upgraded" on paper while emptier in the pocket.

## A currency under siege

The kip is the most visible thermometer of the crisis. To fight near-10% inflation, the central bank has lifted rates to 22.61%, yet high rates choke already-fragile investment and consumption; the currency keeps depreciating, making imports dearer and external debt heavier. Ordinary Lao people feel it as prices rising faster than wages, day after day. An economy squeezed by both debt and exchange rate is hardest hit not by any single indicator but by all of them worsening at once and feeding on each other.

## What it means for the region

Laos is not alone. From the China–Laos Railway to the China–Thailand line, from Kyaukpyu port to industrial parks, China–ASEAN connectivity is being built on a skeleton of loans and infrastructure. Laos's debt distress sounds a warning for that grand narrative: when "hard connectivity" runs ahead of "soft governance," more projects mean more risk exposure. For ASEAN as a whole, a debt-bound inland member is both a weak link in integration and a potential pivot for external geopolitical play. How to make connectivity yield income rather than just invoices is a question the whole Indochinese peninsula must answer.

## The road ahead

Laos's fix does not lie in closing the railway but in balancing the books. Debt restructuring, creditor coordination, converting concessional loans into cash-generating projects, and shoring up the budget before graduation — none of it is glamorous, yet all of it is existential. Rather than debating whether there is a "trap," the outside world should help Laos build its own capacity to generate income. After all, no matter how elegant the railway, if its terminus is default, it cannot carry a nation's tomorrow.

## Further reading

**Least Developed Countries (LDC)**: The UN group of weakest economies, entitled to preferential trade and aid; Laos is set to exit the list on 24 November 2026.

**China–Laos Railway**: A standard-gauge line linking Kunming and Vientiane, opened in late 2021 and a key segment of the pan-Asian rail network.
