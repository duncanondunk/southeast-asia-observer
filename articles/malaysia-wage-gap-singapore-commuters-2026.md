# 工资追不上长堤：为何每天10万马来西亚人越堤赴新加坡打工

马来西亚投资、贸易及工业部长佐哈里本周在国会作出一番少见的直白批评。他指出，部分马来西亚制造商一年盈利数以亿计令吉，却仍以1700令吉（约534新元，约合2870元人民币）的最低工资雇佣本地人，甚至想让技职毕业生与外籍劳工拿同样的薪水。结果可想而知：每天约有10万名马来西亚人搭乘巴士与列车跨过长堤前往新加坡，因为对岸「有经济价值」。据《联合早报》与《星报》11月2日报道，这番话撕开了马来西亚一道仅靠调高最低工资无法修补的结构性短板。

## 数字：一道早已撑不住的底薪

佐哈里援引的数字相当刺眼。马来西亚法定最低工资为每月1700令吉（约534新元，约合2870元人民币）。他点名一些年赚数以亿计令吉的企业，却连这道底线都不愿突破，还想让受过技职训练、手持文凭的毕业生与低薪外劳同酬。政府称已通过技术与职业教育（TVET）培养了约40万名专业人才，许多雇主却仍在大量引进廉价外籍劳工，而非雇用他们。

对岸的吸引力是可以量化的。佐哈里说，每天约有10万名马来西亚人跨境到新加坡工作，其中不止劳工，也有持文凭、学士甚至硕士学位的人，做的活儿与本国工厂相差无几。为什么宁可跨海去做相似的工作？「因为那里有经济价值。」说白了：新加坡给得更多。

## 新加坡为何有磁力

工资差就是全部答案。新加坡底层新人的薪资仍是马来西亚同侪的数倍，而令吉相对新元的疲软更拉大了这道鸿沟。对一名马来西亚人而言，在兀兰或裕廊谋一份普通差事，折算汇率后的到手收入，便能远超本地工厂的薪水。长堤——这条殖民时代画在地图上的界线——早已变成一台每日运作的工资套利机器。佐哈里的意思并非归咎新加坡，而是马来西亚雇主已习惯了低薪模式，当半小时车程外就有出价更高者时，这种模式留不住人才。

## 政策回应：抬高底线，但留了口子

吉隆坡的解法是2027年「昌明预算案（Madani Budget）」。自2027年6月起，全国最低工资由每月1700令吉上调至2000令吉（约3380元人民币），预计惠及超过400万名劳工；年销售额低于5000万令吉（约8470万元人民币）的微型及中小企业暂时豁免，以腾出调整空间。此外，政府将半熟练岗位与大学毕业生的每月最低起薪定为2500令吉（约4230元人民币），并敦促官联企业把生活工资基准从3100令吉提高到3400令吉（约5240元至5760元人民币）。

这些都是实招。但正如预算案演词自己承认的，最低工资「无法解决薪酬未能充分体现劳动者技能与资历这一更深层问题」。2000令吉只是地板，不是阶梯。

## 更深的病灶：对廉价外劳的依赖

佐哈里也戳破了一个流行的迷思——说马来西亚人「懒惰」、不肯进工厂。他的反驳是：若真懒惰，为何每天10万人甘愿跨海去新加坡做相似的活？更硬的真相是结构性的。马来西亚目前合法登记的外籍客工约260万名，其上还有庞大的非法移工群体。数十年来依赖低成本的廉价外籍劳工，让企业推迟了生产率升级，也削弱了向本地人支付溢价的动力。当一名技职毕业生与一名客工可以同酬，传递给年轻马来西亚人的信号再清楚不过：你的学历在家乡换不到溢价。

## 越过地板：要的是生产率，不只是薪水

预算案把病根写得明白——马来西亚劳工报酬占国内生产总值比重仅33.9%，处于偏低水平，工资多年落后于生产率。抬高地板能帮到最弱势者，但若没有与生产率挂钩的薪酬，企业的反应必是老三样：自动化、外移，或更依赖外劳。吉兰丹州苏丹1日从另一个讲台讲了同一番道理：他勉励毕业生不要再被动「找工作」，而要成为「机会的开拓者」，并警告人工智能与数码经济正在重塑职场。部长的「工资说教」与君主的「创业劝勉」，是同一条论证的两半：马来西亚不能继续拿廉价劳力自我剥削。

## 对区域的意味

马来西亚的工资困局并非孤例。整个东南亚在2000年代依靠充裕廉价劳力换来的轻松增长，正走到尽头；越南、泰国、印尼各自面对同一陷阱的变体。马来西亚案例的启示在于，它隔壁就摆着一个看得见的替代选项——半小时车程外的高薪城邦，正实时抽走它的人才。2027年的工资改革只是首付，不是解药。只要马来西亚企业仍靠「谁付最低令吉」而非「谁拼技能与生产率」来竞争，长堤上的通勤就不会停，而国家恰恰在最需要人才的时候，把最好的人持续输出境外。

# Malaysia's Wage Floor Can't Compete with the Causeway: Why 100,000 Malaysians Cross to Singapore Daily

Malaysia's Minister of Investment, Trade and Industry, Zahari, delivered an unusually blunt rebuke in Parliament this week. Some Malaysian manufacturers, he said, earn hundreds of millions of ringgit a year yet still hire locals at the 1,700-ringgit minimum wage — and would even pay vocational graduates the same as foreign workers. The result, he argued, is predictable: every day roughly 100,000 Malaysians board buses and trains across the causeway to Singapore, because "there is economic value" on the other side. His comments, carried by Lianhe Zaobao and The Star on 2 November, lay bare a structural weakness that no minimum-wage hike alone can fix.

## The numbers: a floor that no longer holds

The arithmetic Zahari cited is stark. Malaysia's statutory minimum wage stands at 1,700 ringgit (about S$534, ~US$400) a month. He singled out firms posting yearly profits in the hundreds of millions yet refusing to pay above that floor — and wanting to pay skilled technical graduates the same as low-wage migrant labour. The government says it has trained some 400,000 TVET (technical and vocational) professionals, yet many employers keep importing cheap foreign workers instead of hiring them.

The pull from across the strait is measurable. About 100,000 Malaysians commute to Singapore for work each day, Zahari said — and not only labourers. Among them are diploma, bachelor's and even master's holders doing jobs not unlike those in Malaysian factories. Why cross for comparable work? "Because there is economic value," he said. The blunt translation: Singapore simply pays more.

## Why Singapore pulls

The wage gap is the whole story. A fresh Singaporean worker at the bottom of the scale still earns several times a Malaysian counterpart, and the ringgit's weakness against the Singapore dollar widens the chasm. For a Malaysian, a modest job in Woodlands or Jurong can mean a take-home pay that dwarfs a local factory salary after the exchange rate. The causeway — once a colonial-era line on a map — has become a daily wage-arbitrage machine. Zahari's point is not that Singapore is to blame, but that Malaysian employers have grown comfortable with a low-wage model that cannot retain talent once a higher bidder is a 30-minute ride away.

## The policy answer: a higher floor, with carve-outs

Kuala Lumpur's response is the 2027 Madani Budget. From June 2027 the national minimum wage rises from 1,700 to 2,000 ringgit (~US$470) a month, covering more than 4 million workers. Micro, small and medium enterprises with annual sales below 50 million ringgit (~US$11.8 million) are temporarily exempted to ease the adjustment. Beyond the floor, the government will set a 2,500-ringgit (~US$590) minimum starting pay for semi-skilled roles and graduates, and urges government-linked companies to lift their living-wage benchmark from 3,100 to 3,400 ringgit (~US$730 to ~US$800) a month.

These are real steps. But as the budget speech itself concedes, a minimum wage "does not address the wider issue of wages that do not adequately reflect the skills and qualifications of our workforce." The 2,000-ringgit floor is a floor, not a ladder.

## A deeper malaise: dependence on cheap labour

Zahari also demolished a convenient myth — that Malaysians are "lazy" and simply will not take factory jobs. His rebuttal: if they were lazy, why would 100,000 of them cross daily to do similar work in Singapore? The harder truth is structural. Malaysia now hosts about 2.6 million legally registered foreign workers, with a large pool of irregular migrants on top. Decades of relying on low-cost foreign labour have let firms defer productivity upgrades and dull the incentive to pay locals a premium. When a skilled graduate and a guest worker can be paid the same, the signalling to young Malaysians is unmistakable: your credentials command no premium at home.

## Beyond the floor: productivity, not just pay

The budget documents the root cause plainly — Malaysia's labour compensation share of GDP sits at a relatively low 33.9 per cent. Wages have lagged productivity for years. Raising the floor helps the poorest, but without productivity-linked pay, firms will respond the way they always have: automate, offshore, or lean harder on foreign labour. The Sultan of Kelantan made the same point from a different podium, telling graduates on 1 November to stop hunting for jobs and start creating them, warning that AI and the digital economy are reshaping the workplace. The minister's wage sermon and the monarch's entrepreneurship plea are two halves of one argument: Malaysia cannot keep selling cheap labour to itself.

## What it means for the region

Malaysia's wage squeeze is not unique. Across Southeast Asia, the easy growth of the 2000s — built on abundant, cheap labour — is running out. Vietnam, Thailand and Indonesia face their own versions of the same trap. What makes Malaysia's case instructive is the visible alternative next door: a high-wage city-state a bridge away, draining talent in real time. The 2027 wage reforms are a down payment, not a cure. Until Malaysian firms compete on skills and productivity rather than on who can pay the lowest ringgit, the causeway commute will keep humming — and the country will keep exporting its best workers at the exact moment it needs them most.
