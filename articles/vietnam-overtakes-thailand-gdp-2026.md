# 越南要超泰国了：一场迟来的东南亚经济座次重排

据《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》报道，越南按名义国内生产总值计算，最快将于今年超过泰国，跻身东南亚第三大经济体。这不是一道简单的算术题，而是东南亚经济重心正在发生的一次结构性位移。数十年来，泰国稳坐东盟第二大经济体的位置，如今却被北方的邻国步步逼近直至反超——这背后，写满了两国在产业、人口与治理上的不同选择。

## 一场迟来的“座次”重排

为什么是现在？答案要从两国的增长曲线说起。越南2025年实际国内生产总值增速约8.02%，在主要经济体中相当亮眼；而泰国2026年的实际增速，经合组织（OECD）预测仅为1.5%，较前一年再降0.5个百分点。一边是冲击两位数的雄心，一边是勉力维持的低位增长——两国差距不是偶发波动，而是趋势性的分岔。

或许有人会说，名义口径受汇率与价格影响，并不能完全反映真实购买力。这话没错。但恰恰是在名义口径下，越南的赶超最具象征意义：它意味着投资者、企业和国际机构正在用真金白银为两国的未来投票。泰国《国家报》直言，这是东南亚经济版图的一次“代际更替”。

## 数字：5000亿美元的临界点

《日经亚洲》给出的关键临界点是5000亿美元。越南2025年现价国内生产总值已达约5140亿美元（约3.65万亿元人民币），人均约5026美元，首次跨入上中等收入国家门槛；若增长如期推进，其名义规模有望在2026年或2027年落入5000亿—6000亿美元区间，正式越过泰国。

支撑这一跃升的，是全国范围的基础设施冲刺。越南计划在2026至2030年间投入相当于国内生产总值约一成、即约1500万亿越南盾（约600亿美元，约合4260亿元人民币）的资金用于基建；仅2026年公共投资就拟增约26%。据越南国有投资与发展银行（BIDV）首席经济学家测算，仅此一项即可直接拉动增长1.6个百分点。胡志明市郊的隆城国际机场定于2026年启用，北部获中国支持的铁路项目也已开工——正是这些钢筋水泥，把“增长故事”从纸面落到地面。

## 越南的引擎：国家主导的投资与产业迁徙

越南的快跑，靠的不只是政府花钱。更深层的动力，是全球供应链的一次“向东迁徙”。中美贸易摩擦以来，电子、半导体、电动车等产业加速向越南转移；如今越南电子出口已突破1200亿美元，高技术产业占制造业产出的比重超过三成。越南不再只是低成本的组装车间，而正在成为区域性的制造与物流节点。

值得玩味的是，这一轮产业转移里，“中国因素”挥之不去。为规避美国关税，大量中国电池、半导体和光伏企业把东南亚视为替代性的生产与出口通道，越南恰好接住了这波外溢。换言之，越南的崛起，某种意义上也是中国产业链向外延伸的一个投影。

## 中国的影子：铁路、电动车与供应链重组

如果说产业转移是暗线，基建合作就是明线。越南北方在建的中国标准铁路，正把两国间的货运与人员往来拉得更紧；在电动车与零部件领域，中国品牌和产能的进入，正在改写东南亚的汽车版图。据多方统计，日系车在东南亚的市场份额从2019年到2025年下降了约22个百分点——这背后，是中国电动车攻势与泰国、印尼本土电动车渗透率快速抬升的合力。

但这层“中国影子”也自带隐忧：对中国经济依存度的上升，意味着越南在大国博弈中的回旋空间被同步压缩。增长可以借势，风险同样会共振。

## 泰国的困境：老龄化、家庭债务与汽车业退潮

把镜头转向泰国，画面则沉重得多。泰国人口中位数年龄已约40岁，而越南仅约30岁——人口结构的天平正在缓慢而不可逆地倾斜。更棘手的是高企的家庭债务，它持续压制国内消费，让这个长期依赖内需与旅游的经济体步履蹒跚。

最具象征意味的，是汽车业的退潮。2025年，铃木退出泰国四轮整车生产，本田也收缩了产能。日本车企曾是泰国制造业的骄傲，如今却在重新评估东南亚战略。这不只是几家公司的商业决策，更折射出泰国产业升级的迟滞——当新的增长极属于电动车而非燃油车时，起跑线已被改写。

## 边境之火：地缘风险如何拖累增长

雪上加霜的，是西部边境的火光。2025年以来升级的泰国—柬埔寨边境紧张，已波及双边贸易与旅游。波士顿学院访问学者康武（Kang Wu）警告，边境冲突会令外国投资者更趋谨慎，还可能把东南亚更深地拖入美中战略竞争的漩涡。对泰国而言，内生结构难题尚未破解，外生地缘风险又添一层阴影。

这也给整个东盟提了个醒：区域增长从来不是孤立的宏观经济指标，它与边境的安宁、大国关系的温度紧紧绑在一起。

## 重心东移：排名背后的区域变局

把视野拉高，越南超越泰国不只是一个排名的变化，而是东南亚经济重心的一次“东移”与“北抬”。印尼凭体量稳居第一，新加坡以人均财富与金融深度独树一帜，而越南的上升，代表“国家主导投资＋承接产业转移＋年轻人口”的组合拳正在胜出。

对一个长期以旅游业和汽车业自豪的经济体而言，被后来者反超的冲击，远大于数字本身。泰国的失落，恰恰是东南亚竞争逻辑变化的注脚：这个地区不再奖励躺在新优势上睡大觉的国家，只奖励能够持续升级的经济体。

## 对中国意味着什么：机遇与考题

对中国而言，这场重排既是机遇也是考题。机遇在于，一个更快增长的越南，意味着中国—东盟经济纽带更紧、区域市场更大；考题在于，如何让合作不止于产能外溢，而真正沉淀为互利共赢的结构。越南北方的钢轨、北部湾的航线、湄公河的水电，都是这张答卷上的具体题目。

观察东南亚，不能只盯着数字本身。数字背后，是每一个国家关于“往哪里走”的选择——而选择，永远比预测更值得敬畏。

## 延伸阅读

**名义国内生产总值（Nominal GDP）**：按当年市场价格计算的国内生产总值，未剔除通胀影响；常用于跨国经济规模比较，但会受汇率与价格波动干扰。

**产业转移（Industrial Relocation）**：企业将生产环节从一地迁往另一地的过程，常由成本、关税或地缘因素驱动；近年电子与电动车产业链向越南的迁移即典型一例。

**东盟（ASEAN）**：东南亚国家联盟，现有十个成员国，是亚太重要的区域合作机制与经济板块。

# Vietnam Is About to Pass Thailand: A Long-Overdue Reshuffle of Southeast Asia's Economic Order

According to Nikkei Asia, Vietnam is on track to surpass Thailand in economic size measured by nominal GDP as early as this year, becoming Southeast Asia's third-largest economy. This is no simple arithmetic exercise; it is a structural shift in the region's economic centre of gravity. For decades Thailand sat comfortably as ASEAN's second-largest economy. Now its northern neighbour is closing in and overtaking — and behind that crossover lies a story of very different choices on industry, demographics and governance.

## A long-overdue reshuffle of the pecking order

Why now? The answer lies in the two growth curves. Vietnam's real GDP expanded by roughly 8.02% in 2025, a standout among major economies, while the OECD projects Thailand's real growth at just 1.5% in 2026, down another 0.5 percentage points from the year before. On one side, an ambition to break into double digits; on the other, a struggle to hold low single-digit growth. The gap is not a cyclical blip but a trend-line fork.

One might object that nominal comparisons are distorted by exchange rates and prices and fail to capture true purchasing power. Fair enough. Yet it is precisely in nominal terms that Vietnam's overtaking carries the greatest symbolic weight: investors, companies and international institutions are voting on the two countries' futures with real money. Thailand's Nation newspaper called it, bluntly, a "generational change" in the region's economic map.

## The numbers: the US$500 billion tipping point

The critical threshold Nikkei Asia sets out is US$500 billion. Vietnam's GDP at current prices reached about US$514 billion in 2025, with per capita output around US$5,026, crossing into upper-middle-income territory for the first time. If growth proceeds as planned, its nominal size could land in the US$500–600 billion range in 2026 or 2027, formally edging past Thailand.

Underpinning the leap is a nationwide infrastructure sprint. Between 2026 and 2030, Vietnam plans to spend the equivalent of roughly 10% of GDP — about 1.5 quadrillion dong (about US$60 billion) — on public works; public investment alone is set to rise some 26% in 2026. By the estimate of the chief economist at the state-owned Bank for Investment and Development of Vietnam (BIDV), that alone would add 1.6 percentage points to growth. Long Thanh International Airport on the outskirts of Ho Chi Minh City is due to open in 2026, and the China-backed railway in the north has broken ground — the concrete and steel that turn a growth story from paper into terrain.

## Vietnam's engine: state-led investment and industrial relocation

Vietnam's sprint rests on more than government spending. The deeper driver is an eastward migration of global supply chains. Since the US–China trade frictions, electronics, semiconductors and electric vehicles have accelerated their move into Vietnam; the country's electronics exports have surpassed US$120 billion, and high-tech sectors now account for more than 30% of manufacturing output. Vietnam is no longer a low-cost assembly floor — it is becoming a regional manufacturing and logistics node.

What is telling is that a "China factor" runs through this relocation. To circumvent US tariffs, large numbers of Chinese battery, semiconductor and solar firms have treated Southeast Asia as an alternative production and export channel, and Vietnam has caught the spillover. Vietnam's rise, in other words, is in some sense also a projection of China's industrial chains extending outward.

## China's shadow: railways, EVs and supply-chain reconfiguration

If industrial relocation is the hidden thread, infrastructure cooperation is the visible one. The China-standard railway under construction in northern Vietnam is drawing freight and people ever closer; in electric vehicles and components, the entry of Chinese brands and capacity is redrawing Southeast Asia's automotive map. By multiple counts, Japanese carmakers' share of the Southeast Asian market fell by roughly 22 percentage points between 2019 and 2025 — the combined effect of China's EV push and the rapid rise of EV penetration in Thailand and Indonesia.

Yet this "China shadow" carries risks of its own: rising dependence on the Chinese economy compresses Vietnam's room to manoeuvre in great-power competition. Growth can ride the wave; risk resonates just as readily.

## Thailand's predicament: ageing, household debt and the auto retreat

Turn the lens to Thailand and the picture darkens. Thailand's median age is about 40, against Vietnam's roughly 30 — the demographic balance is tipping, slowly but irreversibly. More intractable is high household debt, which keeps suppressing consumption and leaves an economy long reliant on domestic demand and tourism trudging along.

Most symbolic is the retreat of the auto industry. In 2025 Suzuki exited four-wheel vehicle production in Thailand and Honda scaled back output. Japanese carmakers were once the pride of Thai manufacturing; now they are reassessing their Southeast Asian strategy. This is not merely the business decision of a few firms but a refraction of Thailand's sluggish industrial upgrading — when the new growth pole belongs to electric rather than combustion vehicles, the starting line has been redrawn.

## The fire at the border: how geopolitics weighs on growth

Adding injury to insult is the fire along the western border. Thailand–Cambodia border tensions, escalated since 2025, have disrupted bilateral trade and tourism. Kang Wu, a visiting scholar at Boston College, warns that the conflict will make foreign investors more cautious and could pull Southeast Asia deeper into the vortex of US–China strategic rivalry. For Thailand, endogenous structural problems remain unresolved even as exogenous geopolitical risk piles on another shadow.

It is a reminder for ASEAN as a whole: regional growth is never an isolated macroeconomic indicator; it is bound tightly to the peace of borders and the temperature of great-power relations.

## The centre of gravity drifts east

Zoom out, and Vietnam passing Thailand is not merely a change in ranking but an eastward and northward drift of Southeast Asia's economic centre. Indonesia holds first place by sheer scale; Singapore stands apart on per-capita wealth and financial depth; Vietnam's ascent shows that the combination of state-led investment, absorbed supply chains and a young population is winning out.

For an economy long proud of its tourism and its cars, being overtaken by a latecomer stings far beyond the numbers. Thailand's loss is a footnote to how the region's competitive logic has changed: Southeast Asia no longer rewards countries dozing on old advantages — only economies that keep upgrading.

## What it means for China: opportunity and an exam

For China, the reshuffle is both an opportunity and an exam. The opportunity: a faster-growing Vietnam means a tighter China–ASEAN economic bond and a bigger regional market. The exam: making sure the cooperation settles into a structure of mutual benefit rather than mere capacity spillover. The steel rails of the northern railway, the shipping lanes of the Gulf of Tonkin and the hydropower of the Mekong are all concrete questions on that answer sheet.

To read Southeast Asia, look past the numbers. Behind every figure lies a country's choice of where to go — and choices always deserve more respect than forecasts.

## Further reading

**Nominal GDP**: GDP measured at current market prices, without adjusting for inflation; commonly used to compare economic size across countries, though exposed to exchange-rate and price swings.

**Industrial relocation**: The movement of production from one place to another, typically driven by costs, tariffs or geopolitics; the recent shift of electronics and EV supply chains toward Vietnam is a textbook case.

**ASEAN**: The Association of Southeast Asian Nations, a ten-member regional bloc and a central mechanism for cooperation and economic integration in the Asia-Pacific.
