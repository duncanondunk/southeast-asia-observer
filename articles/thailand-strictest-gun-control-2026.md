# 泰国史上最严控枪令：货架清空了，黑市无恙

曼谷三约（Samyot）枪店的店主们，把货架上的枪支全部撤了下来。不是因为卖完了，而是因为不让卖了。今年8月，一名14岁少年先在家中、后在学校开枪，杀害8人后自尽，首相阿努廷随即宣布泰国史上最严控枪措施：暂停发放新购枪许可证、叫停公务人员优惠购枪计划、加强枪弹交易检查，并拟要求购枪申请者提交心理健康证明。纸面上，这是一次雷厉风行的回应；实际上，它瞄准的是泰国枪患中唯一看得见、也最不危险的那一部分。

## 一纸禁令，清空了枪店

措施来得很快。校园枪击案发生后的几周内，泰国又接连发生两起备受瞩目的枪击事件；一名负责监管枪支许可的政府官员对《日经亚洲》（Nikkei Asia）坦言，枪击案“毫无疑问引发了恐慌”，问题的严重程度“令人担忧”。政府的对策是冻结新证、严查交易、增设心理评估。于是三约——曼谷最主要的民用枪支销售街区——的枪店清空展柜，以示合规。在政府看来，这是行动；在店主看来，这是慢性窒息。“我既补不了货，也卖不出东西，”一位店主说，“但店还得开着，灯和冷气也一直开着。”

## 千万支枪，政府却数不清

问题的规模有多大？泰国官方常引的数据是：民间流通枪支约1000万支，超过东南亚其他国家总和，其中约400万支属无证非法枪支。而这可能还是低估。日内瓦小型武器调查组织（Small Arms Survey）的高级顾问直言：“泰国不擅长编制国家统计数据，我们没有可靠的枪支数据，甚至没有可靠的凶杀案数据。”

请停下来想一想这句话：一个正在冻结购枪许可的政府，说不清国内有多少枪、在谁手里、从哪来。许可制度记录的是守法者的名单；黑市（black market）则是名单之外的一切。当国家冻结新证时，它修改的是一份本来就不包含问题的名单。

## 纸面管不到的市场

如果说整顿的逻辑是“合法枪少了，枪击就少了”，黑市显然没有收到通知。一名化名里基的工厂工人对《日经亚洲》说，他15岁就通过黑市弄到了第一把枪——比20岁的法定购枪年龄整整早了5年。他的“供货人”是街坊里的一名掮客：发条消息，等上一周左右，几乎什么都能到手，甚至包括手榴弹。

这才是新规碰不到的市场。再严厉的许可冻结，也无法让一个靠熟人与口碑运转的街区网络里的枪支变难得多少。未登记的枪、走私的枪、改装的枪：已经在流通的400万支黑枪没有一支经过三约的柜台，接下来的400万支同样不会。

## 泰国人为什么还在买枪

别处的控枪辩论往往围绕大规模枪击与意识形态展开，泰国更关键的驱动却安静得多：恐惧。盖洛普（Gallup）2024年的调查显示，近30%的泰国受访者表示，夜间独自在住家附近行走会感到不安全。再加上射击运动的流行，以及把折扣枪支当作职业福利的公务人员购枪计划，需求端自己就解释清楚了：人们买枪，是因为不相信国家能保护自己；而国家对枪支暴力的回应，却是冻结那些“足够信任国家、走正规程序申请”的人的文书。

这里有一个禁令解不开的循环：不安全感催生持枪，持枪催生暴力，暴力又加深不安全感。打破循环靠的是没有镜头的苦活——让民众有安全感的治安、让走私变难的边境执法、让枪支可被统计的数据系统——而不是冻结一个本来就守规矩的市场。

## 挨罚的偏偏是守规矩的人

枪店业者的委屈不难共情。“许多暴力和犯罪案件都涉及地下流通的非法枪支，但管控措施针对的却是守法公民。”一位店主说。他大体上没错：整顿的可见目标——零售店、许可申请人、射击爱好者——在枪械暴力统计中恰恰是最不可能的源头；而不可见的目标——掮客、走私者、改装枪支——需要泰国尚未展现的侦查能力。

这不意味着政府该无所作为。一个14岁少年能弄到枪并杀害8人，是治理失灵，必须回应；惨案之后雷厉风行，无可厚非。但“快”和“有效”是两回事。这场冻结会赢得掌声、拍出照片，却对下一次枪击案几乎无关痛痒。

## 真正的控枪需要什么

认真的控枪应当从一次诚实的普查开始：登记大赦、弹道建档，以及直面“数不清”的行政意愿。应当有带特赦的回购——给400万支黑枪一个流向市场之外的去处。心理健康证明不应只是申请者面前的门槛，而应成为支持体系的起点。最重要的是直面催生需求的恐惧——夜里感到安全的人，不需要给自己配枪。

泰国办得起人口普查、收得了税、管得住东南亚屈指可数的大型经济体，它有能力把枪数清楚。问题只在于：它想不想。因为“数清楚”是“管得住”的第一步——这正是三约那些空荡荡的货架真正在问的问题。

## 延伸阅读
- **小型武器调查组织（Small Arms Survey）**：总部设于日内瓦的研究项目，对全球民间枪支持有量进行估算；其对泰国约1000万支、其中400万支无证的估计，构成多数泰国枪患报道的数据底座。
- **泰国枪支政治（Gun Politics in Thailand）**：民间持枪合法但须严格许可，黑市枪支泛滥叠加屡次重大枪击案，使控枪成为反复出现却悬而未决的政策难题。
- **阿努廷（Anutin Charnvirakul）**：泰国首相，疫情时期以卫生部长身份崭露头角，此番控枪整顿是其面临的首场重大国内安全考验。

# Thailand's Toughest Gun Rules Empty Legal Shops, Leave the Black Market Untouched

The gun shops of Samyot, Bangkok's main civilian firearms retail district, have pulled every weapon from their shelves. Not because they have sold out — because they have been told to stop. In August, a 14-year-old boy shot and killed eight people, first at home and then at his school, before turning the gun on himself. Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul promptly announced the strictest gun controls in Thailand's history: a freeze on new purchase permits, the end of a discounted-gun scheme for civil servants, tighter checks on ammunition and firearms transactions, and a plan to require mental-health certificates from applicants. On paper, this is a decisive response. In practice, it targets the one part of Thailand's gun problem that the state can actually see — and the least dangerous part.

## A freeze that emptied the shelves

The measures came fast. Within weeks of the school shooting, two more high-profile gun incidents made headlines. An official responsible for overseeing firearms licensing told Nikkei Asia that the case "undoubtedly triggered panic" and that the severity of the problem was "worrying". The government's answer: suspend new licences, scrutinise transactions, add psychological evaluation. And so in Samyot, shopkeepers cleared their display cases in a visible show of compliance. From the government's perspective, this is action. From the shopkeepers', it is slow suffocation. "I can neither restock nor sell," one owner said, "but the shop still has to stay open — the lights and the air conditioning stay on."

## Ten million guns, and a state that cannot count them

How big is the problem? Figures routinely cited by Thai authorities put civilian firearms at roughly ten million — more than all other Southeast Asian countries combined — of which about four million are unlicensed. And even that may be an undercount. As a senior adviser to the Geneva-based Small Arms Survey put it: "Thailand is not good at compiling national statistics, so we have no reliable data on guns. We do not even have reliable homicide statistics."

Pause on that sentence. A government freezing purchase permits cannot say how many guns are in the country, who holds them, or where they came from. The licensing system records a list of the law-abiding; the black market is everything not on it. When the state freezes new permits, it is editing a list that never contained the problem in the first place.

## The market the paperwork cannot reach

If the logic of the crackdown is that fewer legal guns mean fewer shootings, the black market has clearly not received the memo. A factory worker interviewed by Nikkei Asia, using the pseudonym Ricky, acquired his first gun at 15 — five years before the legal purchasing age of 20. His supplier is a neighbourhood fixer: send a message, wait about a week, and almost anything arrives, hand grenades included.

That is the market the new rules do not touch. No permit freeze, however strict, will make a gun much harder to obtain in a neighbourhood network that runs on trust and word of mouth rather than paperwork. Unregistered guns, smuggled guns, converted guns: the four million illicit firearms already in circulation never passed through a Samyot counter, and the next four million will not either.

## Why Thais keep arming themselves

Elsewhere, gun-control debates revolve around mass shootings and ideology. In Thailand, the more powerful driver is quieter: fear. A 2024 Gallup survey found that nearly 30 per cent of Thai respondents said they would feel unsafe walking alone at night in their own neighbourhood. Add the popularity of shooting sports, and a civil-service scheme that made discounted guns a job perk, and the demand side explains itself: people buy guns because they do not trust the state to protect them — and the state's answer to gun violence is to freeze the paperwork of those who trusted it enough to apply.

There is a circularity here that no permit freeze addresses: insecurity drives ownership, ownership feeds violence, violence deepens insecurity. Breaking the cycle requires the unglamorous work — policing that makes people feel safe, border enforcement that makes smuggling hard, data systems that make guns countable — not a freeze on the one market that was already following the rules.

## Punishing the compliant

The dealers' grievance is not hard to sympathise with. "Much of the violence and crime involves illegal guns circulating underground, yet the measures target law-abiding citizens," one shopkeeper said. He is mostly right. The crackdown's visible targets — retail shops, permit applicants, hobbyist shooters — are statistically the least likely sources of gun violence; its invisible targets — the fixers, the smugglers, the converted weapons — demand investigative capacity Thailand has yet to demonstrate.

None of this means the government should do nothing. A 14-year-old obtaining a gun and killing eight people is a governance failure that demands a response, and Mr Anutin cannot be blamed for moving fast after such an atrocity. But moving fast and moving effectively are different things. This freeze will earn applause, make photographs, and matter little to the next shooting.

## What real control would take

Serious gun control in Thailand would begin with an honest census: registration amnesties, ballistic records, and the administrative will to confront the uncountable. It would include a buyback with amnesty — giving the four million illicit guns somewhere to go other than the black market. The mental-health certificate should become not a barrier for applicants but the beginning of a support system. Above all, it would address the fear that drives demand, because people who feel safe at night do not need to arm themselves.

Thailand can run a national census, collect taxes and administer one of Southeast Asia's largest economies. It is perfectly capable of counting its guns. The only question is whether it wants to — because counting is the first step towards confronting. That is what the empty shelves of Samyot are really asking.

## Further reading
- **Small Arms Survey**: A Geneva-based research project that estimates global civilian firearm holdings; its figures for Thailand — roughly ten million guns, four million unlicensed — underpin most reporting on Thai gun prevalence.
- **Gun politics in Thailand**: Civilian gun ownership is legal but strictly licensed; pervasive black-market firearms and repeated mass shootings have made gun control a recurring yet unresolved policy issue.
- **Anutin Charnvirakul**: Thailand's prime minister, who rose to national prominence as health minister during the pandemic; the gun crackdown is the first major domestic-security test of his tenure.
