# 东南亚（Southeast Asia）的住房悖论：房子越盖越多，年轻人却买不起

南华早报（South China Morning Post）一篇报道，揭开了区域性的错配——空置的公寓与买不起房的年轻一代并存。

从河内、胡志明市、大马尼拉、吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）到曼谷，过去十年的天际线都在重复同一个画面：起重机不断竖起新的公寓楼。然而，本应入住的年轻上班族却拿不到房贷，而那些卖出去的单元，往往空置在投资客手中。南华早报在2026年10月27日的一篇报道中，将这一现象称为本地区的"住房悖论"：未售出的房子越堆越多，首次置业者却依旧找不到买得起的家。

本能的反应是归咎于供给不足。数据却指向相反的方向。问题不在于房子太少，而在于盖出来的，是错的房子、错的地点、错的买家——而本该弥合这道缺口的融资安排，反而把缺口拉得更大。

## 数字：越南（Vietnam）、菲律宾（Philippines）、马来西亚（Malaysia）的库存清单

库存的数字相当刺眼。据越南建设部数据，2026年第三季度全国房地产库存约为5.195万套公寓及地块（统计覆盖34个省市中的27个）。在菲律宾，高力国际（Colliers）统计大马尼拉都会区未售出公寓超过3.26万套，其中逾四成集中在360万至700万比索（约41万至79万元人民币）的区间。马来西亚在2026年上半年录得3.3094万套已完工却未售出的住宅——连本该面向本地人的低价房，也出现了过剩。

泰国（Thailand）的故事在更大尺度上重复。泰国《民族报》（Nation Thailand）援引市场数据称，曼谷未售出公寓超过6.53万套，估值约3700亿泰铢（约合780亿元人民币）。研究机构估计，首都2024年底的未售公寓约为23.5万套。这已不是局部困境，而是整个市场的形态。

## 越南：土地财政如何绑架了住房

为什么盖出没人买得起的房子？在越南，答案藏在政府如何为自己筹资之中。新加坡尤索夫伊萨研究所（ISEAS—Yusof Ishak Institute）访问学者阮克江（Nguyen Khac Giang）认为，越南日益依赖土地来为增长融资，使开发天平倒向房地产投资，而非新城市家庭的住房需求。2025年，与土地相关的财政收入估计达575.5万亿越南盾（约合1670亿元人民币），相当于国家预算总收入的21.7%。当地方政府从土地出让与增值中赚得如此之多，其激励便是审批能最大化土地价值的项目——也就是高端公寓，而非首套住房。

结果直接反映在价格结构上。2026年第三季度，每平方米7000万至1亿越南盾（约合2万至2.9万元人民币）的公寓占了新增供应的约48%，1亿至2亿越南盾的又占23%。在这个价位，一套普通公寓的价格是年轻工厂或写字楼雇员年收入的若干倍。如果房价本身远超工资所能负担，单纯放松信贷毫无用处。

## 菲律宾：一杯"星巴克月供"背后的杠杆

菲律宾把同样的错配，包装成了"慷慨"。高力国际菲律宾研究总监乔伊·罗伊·邦多克（Joey Roi Bondoc）指出，开发商如今推销的预缴方案，月供"相当于一杯星巴克咖啡"——一套价值700万比索（约79万元人民币）的公寓，一成首付可以摊到交屋前的60个月。陷阱在交屋那一刻：一旦收房，月摊还额可能变为原来的三到四倍，买家还须向银行贷出余款。高力估计，360万至700万比索这一档的买家，月薪至少需达7万比索（约7900元人民币），约为2024年大马尼拉平均月薪2.93万比索（约3300元人民币）的2.4倍。

投机性需求在2017至2019年见顶（当时开发商每年竣工近1.4万套公寓），如今已崩塌；2027至2029年的年竣工量预计降至约6000套。曾帮业主以租金养贷的缓冲——菲律宾离岸及网络博彩（POGO）从业者带来的租赁需求——也随着该行业反复整顿而消失。空置的塔楼，遇上了空空的钱包。

## 马来西亚与泰国：平价房也空置

马来西亚的过剩值得注意，因为它蔓延到了低价端。2026年上半年3.3094万套已完工却未售出的住宅中，包含本该由本地人接盘的平价单元——它们仍因收入疲弱、放贷收紧以及过往过剩的遗留问题而无人问津。泰国的情况因家庭债务逼近国内生产总值88%而更棘手，房贷拒贷率升至历史高位；即便是300万泰铢（约63万元人民币）以下、中产"甜区"的单元，拒贷率据报也高达七成。曾占外国公寓过户大头的中国买家，随着中国自身房地产危机与资本管制而退场。

## 中国因素：退潮之后谁在裸泳

中国买家的退潮值得细看，因为它暴露出本地区此轮繁荣有多少是一场"离岸故事"。多年来，东南亚房产被卖给中国内地买家，既作"避风港"，对某些人而言更是逃离中国金融体系的出口。柬埔寨的自由持所有权规则、泰国的精英签证、马来西亚的"第二家园"计划（My Second Home），都把公寓变成了资本流动的工具，而不只是居所。当中国的财富效应崩塌，买家不再到来——二级市场被甩卖房源淹没，进一步压低价格。曼谷的空置、清迈停滞的"北部生活方式"项目、老挝入住率偏低的中资楼，都是同一本书的章节：建起塔楼的资金走了，债务与空房却留了下来。

## 缺的不是房子，是买得起的房子

最舒服的叙述是：东南亚只是盖多了。更尖锐的解读是，本地区在错误的融资模式下，盖出了错误类型的住房——把投资需求错当成了真实需要。渴求土地收入的地方政府、追逐高端利润空间的开发商，以及推高价格的外国投机者，共同生产了本地人与终端用户最终都接不住的塔楼。若不纠正价格与区位，只放松信贷，不过是给同一堆库存再融一次资。

解法是朴素的：重整土地与审批激励，让价格回到工资可负担的范围；在人们真正工作与通勤的地方建房；并停止把住房当作资本外逃的载体。在此之前，悖论将持续——一个不缺屋顶、年轻人却仍难安身的区域。

## 延伸阅读
- **土地财政（Land finance）**：越南对土地相关收入的依赖，决定了市场盖什么——是高价值项目，而非可负担住房。
- **菲律宾离岸及网络博彩（POGO）**：这一行业的兴起与整顿，重塑了大马尼拉的租赁需求与公寓吸纳率。
- **马来西亚第二家园计划（Malaysia My Second Home）**：马来西亚的长期居留签证，把房产变成了外国买家的资本流动工具。

# Southeast Asia's housing paradox: too many towers, too few owners

A South China Morning Post report lays bare a regional mismatch — glutted condos next to a generation priced out of the market.

From Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Metro Manila, Kuala Lumpur to Bangkok, the skyline of the past decade has repeated the same picture: cranes keep raising new apartment blocks. Yet the young office workers who were meant to fill them cannot get a mortgage, and the units that do sell often sit empty in investors' hands. In a report published on 27 October 2026, the South China Morning Post called it the region's "housing paradox": unsold homes piling up while first-time buyers still cannot find an affordable place.

The instinctive response is to blame a shortage of supply. The data points the other way. The problem is not that too few homes exist; it is that the homes being built are the wrong homes, in the wrong places, for the wrong buyers — and the financing meant to bridge the gap has instead widened it.

## The numbers: Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia

The inventory figures are striking. According to Vietnam's Ministry of Construction, real estate inventory in the third quarter of 2026 reached roughly 51,950 apartments and land plots, based on data from 27 of the country's 34 localities. In the Philippines, Colliers counts more than 32,600 unsold apartments across Metro Manila, with over 40 per cent concentrated in the 3.6–7 million peso bracket (roughly US$56,000 to US$110,000). Malaysia ended the first half of 2026 with 33,094 completed but unsold homes — and the overhang is visible even in the low-cost segment meant for locals.

Thailand tells the same story at larger scale. Market data cited by Nation Thailand puts Bangkok's unsold condominium stock at more than 65,300 units, valued at around 370 billion baht (about US$11 billion). The research house KKP estimated the capital closed 2024 with roughly 235,000 unsold condos. These are not pockets of distress; they are the shape of the market.

## Vietnam: how land finance hijacked housing

Why build what nobody can buy? In Vietnam, the answer lies in how the state funds itself. Nguyen Khac Giang, a visiting fellow at Singapore's ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute, argues that Vietnam's growing reliance on land to finance growth has tilted development toward property investment rather than the housing needs of new urban families. Land-related revenue reached an estimated 575.5 trillion dong (about US$23 billion) in 2025, equal to 21.7 per cent of total state budget revenue. When local governments earn so much from leasing and appreciating land, the incentive is to approve projects that maximise land value — which means high-end apartments, not starter homes.

The result shows up in the price mix. In the third quarter of 2026, apartments priced at 70–100 million dong per square metre (about US$2,800–4,000) accounted for roughly 48 per cent of new supply, and those at 100–200 million dong a further 23 per cent. At those levels, a modest flat costs several times a young factory or office worker's annual income. Simply loosening credit will not help if the home's price sits far above what wages can service.

## The Philippines: a 'Starbucks-a-month' down payment

The Philippines dresses the same mismatch up as generosity. Joey Roi Bondoc, research director at Colliers Philippines, notes that developers now promote down-payment schemes priced at the equivalent of "a Starbucks coffee per month" — a 10 per cent down payment on a 7 million peso apartment (about US$109,560) can be stretched across 60 months before the unit is even handed over. The trap is at handover. Once buyers take possession, monthly amortisation can triple or quadruple, and they must then qualify for a bank loan to cover the balance. Colliers estimates a buyer in the 3.6–7 million peso segment needs a monthly income of at least 70,000 pesos (about US$1,095) — roughly 2.4 times the 29,310 peso (about US$460) average salary in Metro Manila in 2024.

Speculative demand, which peaked between 2017 and 2019 when developers completed nearly 14,000 apartments a year, has collapsed; completions are expected to fall to around 6,000 a year in 2027–2029. The rental cushion that once helped owners service loans — demand from Philippine Offshore Gaming Operators (POGOs) and their workers — has also evaporated after the sector's on-again, off-again crackdown. Empty towers, meet empty pockets.

## Malaysia and Thailand: even affordable stock sits empty

Malaysia's overhang is notable because it reaches the low end. The 33,094 completed-but-unsold homes in the first half of 2026 include affordable units that buyers still will not or cannot take up, weighed down by weak incomes, stricter lending and a hangover from past oversupply. Thailand's case is amplified by household debt nearing 88 per cent of GDP, pushing mortgage rejection rates to record highs; even units under 3 million baht (about US$90,000) — the middle-class "sweet spot" — face rejection rates reported as high as 70 per cent. Chinese buyers, who once accounted for a large share of foreign condo transfers, have retreated as China's own property crisis and capital controls bite.

## The China factor: who is exposed as the tide recedes

That Chinese retreat deserves a closer look, because it exposes how much of the region's boom was an offshore story. For years, Southeast Asian property was sold to mainland buyers as a "safe haven" and, for some, an exit hatch from China's financial system. Cambodia's freehold rules, Thailand's elite visas and Malaysia's My Second Home scheme turned condos into instruments of capital movement as much as homes. When China's wealth effect collapsed, the buyers stopped coming — and the secondary market flooded with fire-sale units that depressed prices further. Bangkok's vacancy, Chiang Mai's stalled "northern lifestyle" projects and Laos' under-occupied Chinese-built blocks are all chapters of the same book: the capital that built the towers left, but the debt and the empty rooms stayed.

## Not a shortage of homes, but of homes people can afford

The comfortable narrative is that Southeast Asia simply overbuilt. The sharper reading is that the region built the wrong kind of housing on a financing model that confused investment demand with genuine need. Land-hungry local governments, developers chasing margins on premium units, and foreign speculators bidding up prices together produced towers that neither locals nor end-users could ultimately absorb. Loosening credit without fixing prices and locations would only refinance the same glut.

The fix is unglamorous: restructure land and approval incentives so prices return within reach of wages, build where people actually work and commute, and stop treating housing as a vehicle for capital flight. Until then, the paradox will persist — a region with no shortage of roofs, and a generation that still cannot get under one of them.

- **Malaysia My Second Home (马来西亚第二家园计划)**: Malaysia's long-stay visa scheme turned property into a capital-movement instrument for foreign buyers.
