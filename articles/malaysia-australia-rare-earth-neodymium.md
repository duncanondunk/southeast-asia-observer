# 稀土"去中国化"的一小步：马澳合作背后的供应链算计

8月11日，马来西亚（Malaysia）天然资源及环境永续部长聂纳兹米（Nik Nazmi）在吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）国会大厦与澳洲驻马最高专员海内克会晤，双方同意加强在稀土元素等关键矿产领域的合作，并探讨在马国发展钕磁铁（neodymium magnet）的制造能力。钕磁铁是高性能永久磁铁，广泛用于电动车、风力发电机与先进电子设备。

这则看似技术官僚的合作声明，实为全球稀土供应链"去风险"叙事下的一枚落子：当中国在轻、重稀土加工环节占据主导，资源国与消费国正急于在"中国以外"搭建替代产能。马来西亚与澳大利亚的靠拢，正是这条叙事线的缩影。

## 从格宾到钕磁铁：一条"中国以外"的产线

马澳稀土合作并非从零开始。澳洲莱纳斯（Lynas）公司是马国规模最大的稀土伙伴，其加工厂2012年落户彭亨州关丹格宾。去年5月，莱纳斯在格宾成功生产首批重稀土元素产品，成为全球唯一能在中国以外同时供应轻稀土与重稀土的生产商。去年10月，公司又宣布追加投资5亿令吉（约1亿5400万新元，约合人民币7.9亿元），在格宾新建稀土分离设施，扩增重稀土产能。

此次部长级会晤，把合作从"加工"进一步推向"下游制造"——探讨在马国本土发展钕磁铁产能，意味着资源国不再满足于出口原料或初加工，而试图把价值链延伸到终端零部件。

## 1610万公吨储量与一道禁令

马来西亚并非缺资源。据估计，马国约有1610万公吨稀土储量，却长期缺乏开采与加工技术。为防资源流失，马国在发展下游产业的同时，已禁止出口稀土原料。

这道禁令点出了"资源国困境"的核心：坐拥矿藏却无技术，若直接卖原料，不仅附加值外流，还可能在环保与地缘上受制于人。正因如此，引入澳洲的技术专长、把产能留在国内，成为吉隆坡的理性选择。该部文告即称，马国"致力根据本国矿产政策，全面发展稀土价值链，从中游延伸至下游阶段"。

## 去风险叙事下的真实边界

把马澳合作放进更大图景，它契合西方近年力推的关键矿产"去风险"战略——减少对中国加工能力的单点依赖。然而现实边界清晰：莱纳斯虽在中国以外独树一帜，其体量仍远小于中国全产业链；钕磁铁制造所需的分离、提纯与磁材工艺，技术与产能高度集中在中国。

换言之，马澳此番合作是供应链"多元化"的有益尝试，却难在短期内动摇格局。对东南亚（Southeast Asia）观察者而言，它的意义在于揭示一个趋势：资源国正从被动供应者，转向主动谋求价值链升级，而外部资本与技术的竞合，将成为未来区域产业政治的主线之一。

# A Small Step in Rare-Earth "De-Risking": The Supply-Chain Calculus Behind Malaysia–Australia

On 11 August, Malaysia's Minister of Natural Resources and Environmental Sustainability, Nik Nazmi, met Australia's High Commissioner to Malaysia, Danielle Heinecke, at Parliament House in Kuala Lumpur. The two sides agreed to deepen cooperation in critical minerals such as rare earths and to explore building neodymium magnet manufacturing capacity in Malaysia. Neodymium magnets are high-performance permanent magnets used widely in electric vehicles, wind turbines, and advanced electronics.

The seemingly technocratic statement is in fact a move within the global "de-risking" narrative of rare-earth supply chains: as China dominates both light and heavy rare-earth processing, resource and consumer states are rushing to build alternative capacity "outside China." The Malaysia–Australia rapprochement is a microcosm of that story.

## From Gebeng to neodymium: a "non-China" production line

The Malaysia–Australia rare-earth partnership did not start from scratch. Australia's Lynas, Malaysia's largest rare-earth partner, opened its processing plant in Gebeng, Pahang, in 2012. Last May, Lynas produced its first batch of heavy rare-earth products at Gebeng, becoming the world's only producer outside China supplying both light and heavy rare earths. Last October, it pledged an additional RM500 million (about S$154 million, ~US$115 million) to build a new separation facility there, expanding heavy-rare-earth capacity.

This ministerial meeting pushes cooperation from "processing" toward "downstream manufacturing" — exploring domestic neodymium-magnet capacity means the resource state no longer settles for exporting ore or preliminary processing, but extends the value chain to end-component parts.

## 16.1 million tonnes of reserves and one ban

Malaysia is not short of resources. Estimated at some 16.1 million tonnes, its rare-earth reserves have long lacked extraction and processing technology. To prevent resource outflow, while building downstream industry, Malaysia has banned exporting raw rare earths.

That ban captures the core of the "resource-state dilemma": sitting on ore but without technology, selling raw material leaks value-added and risks environmental and geopolitical leverage. Hence bringing in Australian expertise and keeping capacity onshore is Kuala Lumpur's rational choice. The ministry statement said Malaysia is "committed, under its national mineral policy, to developing the full rare-earth value chain, from midstream to downstream."

## The real boundary under the de-risking narrative

Placed in the larger picture, the cooperation fits the critical-minerals "de-risking" strategy pushed by the West in recent years — reducing single-point dependence on Chinese processing. Yet the boundary is clear: although Lynas stands alone outside China, its scale remains far below China's integrated chain; the separation, refining, and magnet-making processes stay concentrated in China.

In other words, the Malaysia–Australia move is a useful experiment in supply-chain diversification, but unlikely to shift the structure soon. For Southeast Asia watchers, its significance is the signal: resource states are moving from passive suppliers to actors actively seeking value-chain upgrades, and the contest and cooperation of external capital and technology will become a main thread of the region's industrial politics.
