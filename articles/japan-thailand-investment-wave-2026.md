# 日本企业重新下注泰国：一轮投资潮正在改写旧剧本

泰国投资促进委员会（Board of Investment，简称 BOI）2026年9月7日确认，日本资金正从低成本组装转向自动化、机器人、清洁能源与更高附加值的产品。几十年来，日本资本在泰国的含义很固定——一个低成本组装汽车和家电的地方。如今这个剧本正在被重写，数字相当醒目。

## 数字：2025年对泰投资申请翻倍

2025年，日本投资者共提交302件投资促进申请，总额约1137亿泰铢（约227亿元人民币），较上年增长一倍以上，为有记录以来年度总额最高的年份之一。这股势头延续到2026年：仅上半年又新增123件申请、总额327.9亿泰铢（约65亿元人民币）。泰国仍是 BOI 口中的日本投资者"第二故乡"。但这个故乡正在被翻新，而非仅仅被居住。

## 从组装基地到技术升级

这一转变的关键不在于建更多同样的厂，而在于淘汰旧设备、采用清洁能源与数字系统、开发技术含量更高的产品。2025年汽车及零部件申请额增长57%至283.1亿泰铢（约56亿元人民币），电子电器增长76%至193.8亿泰铢（约39亿元人民币）。农业、食品与物流同样上扬。要害不在体量，而在方向——日企在升级它们持有了几十年的旧厂，而不是去别处另起炉灶。

## 四大车厂砸钱升级产线

汽车集群把故事讲得最清楚。五十铃（Isuzu）获批逾150亿泰铢（约30亿元人民币），引入自动化、机器人与清洁能源升级皮卡基地，以符合欧6排放标准。马自达（Mazda）通过 AutoAlliance 合资投入74亿泰铢（约15亿元人民币）生产新款轻混车型。三菱（Mitsubishi）将在2030年前投入160亿泰铢（约32亿元人民币）发展电动化技术并生产出口版帕杰罗。本田（Honda）计划2029年前投入120亿泰铢（约24亿元人民币）新增两条产线，使泰国成为日本以外唯一生产八款本田车型的国度。供应链也在动：日立安斯泰莫（Astemo）斥资35亿泰铢（约7亿元人民币）生产功率控制逆变器，爱信（Aisin）布局混动变速箱。

## 首次闯入高端航空供应链

新意在于钱流向了哪里。美蓓亚（NMB-Minebea）在華富里府投资26亿泰铢（约5.2亿元人民币）生产空中客车（Airbus）与波音（Boeing）的精密飞机零部件，并在大城府设研发中心——这是泰国迈入高端航空供应链的第一步，其公差要求远苛刻于汽车制造，且在东盟几乎无可匹敌的基地。松下（Panasonic）在大城府建东盟首座 MEGTRON 电路板材料基地，服务人工智能与数字需求；村田（Murata）扩产先进电容。东洋制罐（Toyo Seikan，24.7亿泰铢／约4.9亿元人民币）与岩谷（Iwatani，8.4亿泰铢／约1.7亿元人民币，采用碳捕集技术）补足了这份泰国此前几乎未曾涉足的产业组合。

## 中国电动车逼出的防御性升级

为何是现在？坦率说，是竞争压力。中国电动车品牌横扫东南亚，泰国吸引的中资电动车投资首次超过日本。日系车厂对纯电下注迟缓，转而押注混动与插电混动——这是一场精算后的赌注：认定本区域充电网络尚未准备好迎接全面电动化。因此这次升级既是雄心，更是防御：守住皮卡与运动型多用途车（SUV）的基本盘，给它们裹上电动化动力总成，指望"第二故乡"继续管用。

## K型分化：有人加码有人离场

但日本资本已不是一个故事。当五十铃、马自达、三菱、本田大笔砸钱时，斯巴鲁（Subaru）已于2024年底在泰国停产整车组装，铃木（Suzuki）也将在2025年底前关闭泰国工厂。这种分化才是头条。日本商工会议所对504家企业的调查显示，23%计划年内增加在泰投资、48%维持现状——71%在扩张或守成。能适应者留下，不能者离开。泰国的"第二故乡"正在被筛选，而非仅仅被维护。

## 就业两难：自动化不创造等量岗位

亮眼的总量数字藏着硬伤。偏向自动化与机器人的资本，并不会按等比例创造就业。劳工研究指向两极分化：自动化工程、电动车维保与研发等高技能岗位增长且薪酬更高，而重复性产线工作被稳步淘汰。低技能与中技能工人最受伤，无力承担升级成本的中小泰企供应商可能被踢出链条。泰国央行与 SCB EIC 正反复警示这种 K 型经济——大企业与技术部门向前冲，家庭与小微落在后头。

## 结语：重新定价的"第二故乡"

日本对泰的新一轮投资是真实的、庞大的，也颇具战略眼光。但它是一场重新定价，而非一次救援。"第二故乡"正围绕自动化、电动化与少数高端利基重建，过程中也正在甩掉支撑日泰关系一代人的低技能、高人头模式。曼谷理应欢迎这笔资本，也该为新模式抛下的工人与供应商早做打算。

# Japan's Firms Redouble Their Thailand Bets as a New Investment Wave Breaks the Old Mold

For decades, Japanese capital in Thailand meant one thing: a cheap place to bolt together cars and appliances. That story is being rewritten. On 7 September 2026 the Thailand Board of Investment confirmed that Japanese money is pivoting from low-cost assembly into automation, robotics, clean energy and higher-value products — and the numbers are striking.

## The numbers: Thailand applications doubled in 2025

In 2025 Japanese investors filed 302 investment-promotion applications worth about 113.7 billion baht (about US$3.4 billion), more than double the year before and one of the highest annual totals on record. The momentum carried into 2026: another 123 projects worth 32.8 billion baht (about US$990 million) in the first half alone. Thailand, the BOI likes to say, remains Japanese investors' "second home." But the home is being renovated, not simply lived in.

## From assembly base to tech upgrade

The shift is not about building more of the same. It is about retiring old machinery, adopting clean energy and digital systems, and developing products with higher technology content. Automotive and parts led 2025 applications, up 57% to 28.31 billion baht (about US$858 million); electronics jumped 76% to 19.38 billion baht (about US$587 million). Agriculture, food and logistics also rose. The point is not volume — it is direction. Japanese firms are upgrading the factories they have held for decades rather than opening greenfield sites elsewhere.

## Four carmakers pour money into their lines

The auto cluster tells the story most clearly. Isuzu won approval for over 15 billion baht (about US$455 million) to upgrade its pickup base with automation, robotics and clean energy to meet Euro 6 standards. Mazda, through its AutoAlliance venture, is putting 7.4 billion baht (about US$224 million) into a new mild-hybrid model. Mitsubishi will invest 16 billion baht (about US$485 million) by 2030 to support electrified vehicle technology and build the export Pajero. Honda plans 12 billion baht (about US$364 million) by 2029 to add two lines, making Thailand the only country outside Japan to build eight Honda models. Suppliers are moving too: Astemo is spending 3.5 billion baht (about US$106 million) on power-control inverters, Aisin on hybrid transmissions.

## Breaking into the high-end aerospace chain

What is new is where the money goes. NMB-Minebea is investing 2.6 billion baht (about US$79 million) in a Lopburi plant making precision aircraft parts for Airbus and Boeing, with an R&D centre in Ayutthaya — one of Thailand's first steps into a high-end aerospace supply chain that demands tolerances far tighter than car-making and has almost no rival base in ASEAN. Panasonic is building ASEAN's first MEGTRON circuit-board base in Ayutthaya for AI and digital demand; Murata is expanding advanced capacitors. Toyo Seikan (2.47 billion baht / about US$75 million) and Iwatani (840 million baht / about US$25 million, using carbon capture) round out a portfolio that reaches industries Thailand barely hosted before.

## A defensive upgrade forced by Chinese EVs

Why now? The honest answer is competitive pressure. Chinese electric-vehicle brands have stormed Southeast Asia, and Thailand's Chinese EV investment has for the first time overtaken Japan's. Japanese carmakers, slow to bet on pure battery EVs, are leaning into hybrids and plug-ins — a calculated wager that the region's charging network is not yet ready for full electrification. The upgrade is therefore as much defence as ambition: keep the pickup and SUV strongholds, wrap them in electrified drivetrains, and hope the "second home" stays relevant.

## The K-shape: some double down, some leave

But Japanese capital is no longer one story. While Isuzu, Mazda, Mitsubishi and Honda pour in fresh money, Subaru ended car assembly in Thailand at the end of 2024, and Suzuki will close its Thai plant by the end of 2025. The divergence is the headline. A Japanese Chamber of Commerce survey of 504 firms found 23% plan to increase Thai investment this year and 48% to hold steady — 71% expanding or holding. The firms that adapt stay; the ones that cannot, leave. Thailand's "second home" is being sorted, not merely maintained.

## The jobs dilemma: automation does not scale 1:1

The rosy aggregate figure hides a hard edge. Capital tilted toward automation and robotics does not create jobs in step with the money. Labour research points to polarisation: high-skilled roles in automation engineering, EV servicing and R&D grow and pay more, while routine line work is steadily phased out. Low- and mid-skilled workers are most exposed, and small Thai suppliers that cannot fund the jump to new standards risk being cut from the chain. The Bank of Thailand and SCB EIC have warned precisely about this K-shaped economy — big firms and tech race ahead while households and micro-enterprises lag.

## Conclusion: repricing the "second home"

Japan's new wave of investment in Thailand is real, large and strategically smart. But it is a repricing, not a rescue. The "second home" is being rebuilt around automation, electrification and a few high-end niches — and in the process it is shedding the low-skill, high-headcount model that anchored Japanese-Thai ties for a generation. Bangkok should welcome the capital. It should also plan for the workers and suppliers the new model leaves behind.
