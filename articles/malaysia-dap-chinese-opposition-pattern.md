# "马来人在朝，华人在野"：民行党去留背后的华人政治困局

8月16日，马来西亚（Malaysia）民主行动党（DAP）的4000多名全国代表将表决是否退出联邦团结政府。这看似一党之决，却可能成为马来西亚族群政治的一道分水岭：若退出成真，不仅团结政府风雨飘摇、希盟（Pakatan Harapan）存亡受考，更可能在"马来选票合流"的趋势中，加速催生"马来人在朝、华人在野"的两极格局。

民主行动党是马来西亚历史最久的多族群政党之一，长期被视为华人政治的重要代表。它的去留，折射的正是海外华人（华侨华人）在族裔化政治结构中的结构性位置——一个值得观察者反复咀嚼的样本。

## 真纳之败：一道算术题的天花板

引爆点来自选举。秘书长陆兆福（Anthony Loke）在森美兰州真纳州议席失守，该区马来选民占47.36%、未过半却仍由马来反对党拿下。更冷的数字在宏观面：2022年大选时，马来西亚半岛165个国会议席中，非马来人占多数的仅剩33席。按马来人口增长快于非马来人的趋势推算，这恐怕已是天花板。

换言之，只要柔佛、森美兰州选的投票趋势延续，民主行动党未来能斩获的席位只会更少。陆兆福形象温和、基层口碑不差，却依然无法在一个马来选民未过半的选区突围——这本身就是对"跨族群政党"天花板的注解。

## 两边不讨好：认同政治与改革的双重失速

巧思中心（Ilham Centre）在森州选举期间的民调显示，67%的马来回教徒受访者认为，民主行动党动摇君主、回教与马来人的地位。马来社会对希盟的排斥，短期内难以扭转。

与此同时，党内传统支持者也不满。其一，身份认同政治：华族选民指责党未兑现承诺，在华文教育、大学录取等课题上"货不对办"；为争取马来回教徒，首相安华（Anwar Ibrahim）的言行政策皆向右倾。其二，制度改革：具公民意识的支持者对肃贪、转型正义的缓慢甚至倒退失去耐心，转向同心党（BERSAMA）、睦达党（MUDA）等新生政党。

## 切割还是共沉沦：两难中的道德姿态

有人判断，民主行动党难再从安华政权获得加分，反而须承担团结政府犯错的风险。与其被动节节败退，不如主动切割止损，保住华族占多数的议席与槟州政权，为东山再起留薪火。

但从另一角度看，退出也可能被指责为在盟友低迷时背弃战友、过河拆桥，进而坐实"民行党只是在利用马来人"的指控，加剧马来社会的负面观感。若党为巩固华族铁票而在统考、宗教等议题上更激进，势必引发更大反感，强化"华人沙文主义政党"标签。

## "华人在野"格局：一个可能定型的时代

环顾当前，若柔森州选趋势延续，未来极可能出现"马来人在朝、华人在野"：以种族宗教叙事为主的马来右翼执政，主张改革、兼容多元的势力只能在体制外摇旗。回望过去20年，马来西亚政治反复上演多元阵线对垒单元阵线的攻防——从巫统（UMNO）主导对垒人民联盟，到希盟多元执政对垒巫统与伊斯兰党（PAS）。

这20年持续刺激马来人的焦虑，也累积非马来人的沮丧。人们有理由担忧：若两极矛盾无法化解，这一格局或在族群人口增幅悬殊下定型相当一段时间。对研究华侨华人的学者而言，马来西亚的案例提醒我们——海外华人的政治前途，往往不取决于自身努力，而嵌于居住国的族群权力结构之中。

**团结政府（Unity Government）**：2022年大选后由安华牵头、跨族群多党联合的联邦政府，旨在避免政治僵局，但内部族群利益分歧显著。

# "Malays in Government, Chinese in Opposition": The Chinese Political Dilemma Behind DAP's Fate

On 16 August, more than 4,000 delegates of Malaysia's Democratic Action Party (DAP) will vote on whether to quit the federal Unity Government. What looks like one party's decision may become a watershed in the country's ethnic politics: if it exits, not only the Unity Government but the survival of Pakatan Harapan (PH) hangs in the balance, and the trend of "Malay votes converging" could accelerate a bipolar order of "Malays in government, Chinese in opposition."

The DAP is among Malaysia's oldest multi-ethnic parties and has long been seen as a key vehicle for Chinese political representation. Its predicament reflects the structural position of overseas Chinese within an ethnicised political system — a specimen worth close study.

## The Taman Jauhar defeat: a ceiling written in arithmetic

The trigger was electoral. Secretary-general Anthony Loke lost the Negeri Sembilan state seat of Taman Jauhar, where Malays made up 47.36% — a minority, yet the seat fell to a Malay opposition party. The colder macro figure: in the 2022 general election, only 33 of Peninsular Malaysia's 165 parliamentary seats had non-Malay majorities. Given Malay population growth outpacing non-Malays, that number is likely a ceiling.

In other words, if the Johor and Negeri Sembilan voting trends persist, the DAP can only win fewer seats ahead. Loke, relatively moderate and well regarded at the grassroots, still could not break through in a seat where Malays were a minority — itself a footnote on the ceiling facing cross-ethnic parties.

## Pleasing neither side: identity politics and stalled reform

Ilham Centre's poll during the Negeri Sembilan campaign found 67% of Malay Muslim respondents believed the DAP threatened the monarchy, Islam, and Malay status. Malay rejection of PH is unlikely to reverse soon.

Meanwhile, the party's traditional base is also restless. First, identity politics: Chinese voters accuse the party of failing to deliver on promises over Chinese education and university admission, while Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim has leaned rightward in word and policy to court Malay Muslims. Second, institutional reform: civically minded supporters have lost patience with slow — even regressing — progress on anti-corruption and transitional justice, drifting toward new parties like BERSAMA and MUDA.

## Cut loose or sink together: a moral pose amid dilemma

Some argue the DAP can no longer gain from Anwar's administration but must share the blame for its missteps. Rather than retreat passively, better to cut losses actively, secure seats with Chinese majorities and the Penang government, and keep a spark for revival.

Yet exiting could also be framed as abandoning allies at their lowest and burning bridges, reinforcing the charge that "the DAP merely uses Malays," deepening Malay antipathy. Should the party adopt sharper stances on the Unified Examination Certificate or religion to lock in Chinese iron votes, it would provoke greater backlash and cement the "Chinese chauvinist" label.

## A "Chinese in opposition" era: a possible settling

Looking around, if the Johor–Negeri Sembilan trends hold, Malaysia may see "Malays in government, Chinese in opposition": Malay-right parties ruling on racial-religious narratives while reformist, pluralist forces wave flags only outside the system. Over the past two decades, Malaysian politics has replayed the clash of plural fronts against unitary ones — from UMNO-led coalitions against the precursor of PH, to PH's plural government against UMNO and PAS.

These two decades kept stoking Malay anxiety and non-Malay frustration alike. There is reason to fear that, if the polarisation is not resolved, the pattern may settle for a stretch, given the lopsided demographic growth. For scholars of the overseas Chinese, Malaysia's case is a reminder: the political fortune of a diaspora often hinges less on its own efforts than on the ethnic power structure of the country it inhabits.
