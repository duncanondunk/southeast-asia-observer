# 美国资本挺进马来西亚稀土：多元化，还是换一种依赖？

一家美国资本控股的初创企业声称将在马来西亚建起下一代稀土精炼厂。标题数字诱人：6亿美元、两座工厂、年产1万公吨的目标。但剥开这层话术，一个更尖锐的问题浮现——这究竟是真的供应链多元化，还是把对北京的依赖换成对华盛顿的依赖？

## 交易轮廓：6亿美元、两座厂、1万吨
美国资本控股的初创企业 DTEC Mineral（全称 DTEC Mineral and Metal Technology）承诺投资6亿美元（约43亿元人民币；公司披露约合7亿6700万新元，约41亿元人民币），未来三年在马来西亚分阶段兴建两座稀土提炼厂。公司首席执行员珀利（Milan Perlly）称，目标是将年产能提升至1万公吨，东海岸一座规划年产能5000公吨，西海岸再建一座。企业已与吉打、登嘉楼、霹雳及森美兰的州政府会谈，物色设厂地点。

最关键的细节藏在报道更深处。自马来西亚2024年对未加工稀土原料实施出口限制以来，DTEC 是首家获准向美国出口原矿的企业。它拿到最多700公吨未加工原料的出口许可，正筹备约30公吨的首批货样运往美国，用于成分分析与工艺确定。换言之，在马来西亚的第一座工厂尚不存在之前，加工工艺仍须先在海外摸索出来。

## 为何是马来西亚，为何是此刻
逻辑并不复杂。中国至今主导全球稀土加工，华盛顿过去数年一直在搭建不途经北京的供应线。马来西亚的特殊之处清晰：坐拥可观的离子吸附型黏土（ionic clay）矿藏，且已有虽单薄却真实存在的精炼基础。对吉隆坡而言，放松出口禁令是一次精心计算的招商——用加工能力换取外资，并力争向价值链上游挪一步。

但「为何是此刻」另有锋芒。审批落地，恰在马来西亚释放将选择性重开原矿出口的信号之后——这项自2024年锁死的政策出现转向。首个受益者竟是一家带有美国背景的企业，这已说明吉隆坡正在向哪路资本示好，也想向外释放何种地缘信号。

也不应夸大马来西亚的自主空间。北京将出口禁令的松动解读为向华盛顿的刻意靠近。中国自身的优势不靠矿石，而靠精炼化工与规模——这是一家初创企业的幻灯片无法复制的。马来西亚在腾挪，但它腾挪的框架，大半由他人设定。

## 莱纳斯的影子与马来西亚的「单厂困局」
十余年来，马来西亚的稀土故事几乎是「一家企业」的故事。澳大利亚的莱纳斯（Lynas）在彭亨州关丹运营着中国境外唯一的大型精炼厂。这座工厂既是战略资产，也是战略软肋：当提纯命脉系于珀斯而非吉隆坡，你便无从掌控自己的供应链。

DTEC 入局，理论上能稀释这种依赖。两座厂总好过一座。然而讽刺在于：新节点由美国人控股。马来西亚不过是把一个由澳大利亚掌控的单点故障，换成了第二个由华盛顿掌控的节点。股权的多元化，不等于杠杆的多元化；一座为美国供应安全而建的精炼厂，或许只会顺带服务于马来西亚产业。

## 资源储量大于现实产能
马来西亚称已探明约1610万公吨稀土资源，其中多为离子吸附型黏土——这类矿藏开采成本低，且可能含有用于高性能磁铁与先进国防装备的中重稀土。纸面上，这一数字足以改写格局。

事实并非如此，至少现在不是。这些资源大多尚未证实具备商业开采价值。「已识别资源」与「已探明可采储量」之间的落差，曾埋葬过无数稀土宏图。700公吨的出口许可，相对1万公吨的野心只是四舍五入的零头；DTEC 自己还要把样品运往海外，先搞清楚如何加工。所谓枢纽，目前仍是一份幻灯片，而非一座冶炼厂。

## 特朗普—安华协定背后的战略赌注
这一切并非凭空发生。DTEC 是去年美国总统特朗普与马来西亚首相安华签署贸易及关键矿产合作协定所点名的企业之一。建厂计划因此成为一项地缘政治交易的具象交付：美国买到了供应安全，马来西亚买到了投资与一张关键矿产博弈的入场券。

这笔交易是否惠及马来西亚自身，才是悬而未决的问题。稀土加工资本密集且环境高危——精炼产生的放射性尾矿，长期是马来西亚与莱纳斯之间的头痛难题。更多工厂意味着对废水、水源与土地的更严苛审视。吉隆坡必须想清楚：自己究竟在建设一项产业，还是在出租脚下的地质。

## 吉隆坡真正得到什么，又押上什么
乐观的读法：马来西亚成为名副其实的区域加工枢纽，攫取精炼工艺，并降低对单一海外精炼厂的依赖。怀疑的读法：它不过把一种外部依赖换成另一种，把自己锁死在开采与初步加工环节，而高毛利磁铁与组件制造留在他处，同时接下环境账单。

对东南亚观察者而言，这正是缩影。区域正被争相拉拢为中美科技角力的「不可或缺中间层」——但这场竞逐的条款，写在华盛顿与北京，而非吉隆坡。马来西亚的稀土豪赌值得紧盯，不在今年运出的700公吨，而在谁来掌控将来那1万公吨。

还有一则更安静的启示留给区域里的小国：在两个大国之间，保持「不可或缺」的奖赏，往往是被两股力量同时拉扯。

## 延伸阅读
- **离子吸附型黏土稀土（ionic clay）**：一种吸附于黏土之上的稀土矿类型，开采成本低于硬岩矿，且常富含制造高性能磁铁与先进装备所需的中重稀土。
- **莱纳斯公司（Lynas）**：澳大利亚企业，在彭亨州关丹运营中国境外唯一大型稀土精炼厂，是马来西亚长期依赖的单一海外精炼节点，也是 DTEC 入局意欲补足或竞争的标杆。
- **关键矿产协定（美马）**：特朗普与安华签署的协议，DTEC 名列其中，将双边供应链「去中国化」合作制度化。

# US-Backed Rare Earth Refineries in Malaysia: Diversification or a Rename of Dependence?

A US-capital start-up says it will build Malaysia's next rare earth refineries. The headline numbers are seductive: US$600 million, two plants, a 10,000-tonne annual target. But strip away the pitch and a harder question emerges — is this genuine supply-chain diversification, or simply dependence on Beijing exchanged for dependence on Washington?

## The deal: US$600 million, two plants, 10,000 tonnes

DTEC Mineral and Metal Technology, a start-up controlled by American capital, has pledged to invest US$600 million (about S$767 million) to build two rare earth refining plants in Malaysia over the next three years. Chief executive Milan Perlly says the goal is to lift annual output to 10,000 metric tonnes, with a 5,000-tonne facility on the east coast and a second on the west. The company has already held talks with state governments in Kedah, Terengganu, Perak and Negeri Sembilan to scout sites.

The detail that matters most is buried lower in the story. DTEC is the first firm cleared to export unprocessed rare earth feedstock to the United States since Malaysia relaxed its 2024 ban on raw rare earth exports. It has been permitted to ship up to 700 tonnes of unprocessed material, and is preparing an initial 30-tonne consignment to the US for compositional analysis. In other words, the processing know-how still has to be discovered abroad before a single Malaysian plant exists.

## Why Malaysia, and why now

The logic is not complicated. China still dominates global rare earth processing, and Washington has spent years trying to build supply lines that do not run through Beijing. Malaysia offers something specific: sizeable ionic-clay deposits and an existing, if thin, refining base. For Kuala Lumpur, loosening the export curb is a calculated pitch — trade processing capacity for foreign investment, and ideally climb one rung up the value chain.

But "why now" carries a sharper edge. The approval landed weeks after Malaysia signalled it would selectively reopen raw rare earth exports, a policy reversal locked since 2024. That the first beneficiary is a US-linked firm tells you which capital Kuala Lumpur is courting, and which geopolitical signal it wants to send.

Nor should we overstate Malaysia's agency. Beijing reads the relaxation of the export ban as a deliberate tilt toward Washington. China's own dominance rests not on ore but on refining chemistry and scale — advantages a single start-up's PowerPoint cannot replicate. Malaysia is manoeuvring, but it manoeuvres inside a structure largely set by others.

## The Lynas shadow and Malaysia's single-refinery bind

For more than a decade, Malaysia's rare earth story has been a one-company story. Australia's Lynas operates the only major refining plant outside China, in Kuantan, Pahang. That plant is both a strategic asset and a strategic vulnerability: when your refining destiny is decided in Perth rather than Kuala Lumpur, you do not control your own supply chain.

DTEC's entry would, in theory, dilute that dependence. Two plants beat one. Yet note the irony — the new node is American-owned. Malaysia would be swapping a single point of failure controlled from Australia for a second node controlled from Washington. Diversification of ownership is not the same as diversification of leverage, and a refinery built to serve US supply security may serve Malaysian industry only incidentally.

## A resource estimate larger than the reality

Malaysia says it has identified roughly 16.1 million metric tonnes of rare earth resources, much of it ionic-clay — the cheap-to-mine variety that can also carry the heavy rare earths used in high-performance magnets and advanced defence systems. On paper, that figure sounds transformative.

It is not, yet. Most of those resources have not been verified as commercially viable. The gap between "identified resources" and "proven, mineable reserves" is where countless rare earth ambitions have quietly died. The 700-tonne export licence is a rounding error against a 10,000-tonne ambition, and DTEC itself is still shipping samples overseas to work out how to process them. The "regional hub" is, for now, a slide deck, not a smelter.

## The strategic wager behind the Trump–Anwar accord

None of this is happening in a vacuum. DTEC is among the companies named in the trade and critical-minerals cooperation agreement signed by US President Donald Trump and Malaysian Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim last year. The refinery plan is thus a concrete deliverable of a geopolitical bargain: America buys supply security; Malaysia buys investment and a seat at the table of the critical-minerals contest.

Whether that bargain serves Malaysian interests is the open question. Rare earth processing is capital-intensive and environmentally hazardous — the radioactive tailings from refining have long been Malaysia's headache with Lynas. More plants mean more scrutiny of waste, water and land. Kuala Lumpur must decide whether it is building an industry or merely renting out its geology.

## What Kuala Lumpur actually gains — and risks

The optimistic reading: Malaysia becomes a genuine regional processing hub, captures refining know-how, and reduces its reliance on a single foreign refinery. The sceptical reading: it trades one external dependence for another, locks itself into extraction and preliminary processing while the high-margin magnet and component manufacturing stays elsewhere, and inherits the environmental bill.

For watchers of Southeast Asia, the episode is a microcosm. The region is being courted as the indispensable middle layer of the US–China technology contest — but the terms of that courtship are written in Washington and Beijing, not in Kuala Lumpur. Malaysia's rare earth gamble will be worth watching not for the 700 tonnes shipped this year, but for who controls the 10,000 tonnes to come.

There is also a quieter lesson for the region's smaller states: between two superpowers, the prize for staying indispensable is often to be pulled in both directions at once.

## Further reading

- **Ionic-clay rare earths**: A clay-adsorbed deposit type, cheaper to extract than hard-rock ore and often richer in the heavy rare earths critical for high-performance magnets and advanced defence technology.
- **Lynas Corporation**: The Australian firm operating the only major rare earth refinery outside China, in Kuantan, Malaysia — the benchmark DTEC's entry is meant to complement or compete with.
- **US–Malaysia Critical Minerals Agreement**: The Trump–Anwar accord under which DTEC is listed, formalising bilateral cooperation to diversify supply chains away from China.
