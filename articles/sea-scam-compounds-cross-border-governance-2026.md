# 园区不是飞地：东南亚电诈治理里的地方保护与监管落差

把东南亚边境的电诈园区称作“法外之地”，是一种方便却有误导性的说法。据公开报道，这类园区多设在边境经济特区、赌场周边或地方权力交错的控制带，看上去像主权管辖够不到的飞地；但更贴近事实的解释是，它们恰恰嵌在地方治理结构之中——依赖本地发放的牌照、租出的土地、接入的电力、默许的通行，以及一条能把人和钱送进送出的日常通道。

这个区分不是措辞之争。如果园区真在管辖之外，治理的难题就是如何把国家权力延伸进去；可如果它本就在管辖之内，难题就变成了为什么地方愿意留它。园区不是治外法权飞地，而是地方保护与跨境监管落差共同喂养的产物。只盯着联合执法的力度，会漏掉更根本的两件事：地方靠什么养它，以及被解救的人回来之后为什么仍难回到正常生活。

## 边境特区与赌场：监管真空是怎样生成的

经济特区与博彩牌照，本意都是招商引资的工具。在湄公河次区域若干边境地带，特区享有税收、土地与通关便利，赌场则天然带着现金密集、人员流动快、身份核验松的环境。当中央的监管半径长、地方的裁量权大、边境线又难以闭合，牌照经济就容易外溢：注册的是酒店、科技园或综合度假村，实际经营的却是封闭式电诈工位与宿舍。

所谓“真空”，并不是没有法律，而是多层管辖之间的责任被摊得太薄。特区管委会、地方警力、移民部门、土地出租方与中央部委各有各的依据，却很少有一个主体对从招工到资金结算的整条链条负责。监管落差的第一个形态，因此不在国境线上，而在部门与部门之间、中央文本与地方执行之间。

## 高薪招聘背后：一条被包装的输送链

园区的人力供给，靠的是一条被精心包装的输送链。招聘话术通常是客服、技术岗、包机票食宿，经社交平台、中介与熟人转介扩散；出境之后，证件被收走、行动被限制、人被在园区之间转手。多方观察认为，这类案件里当事人的同意往往是在欺骗下取得的，因而不应简单排除人口贩运的性质。

法律上的界分影响极大：一旦被归入“自愿出境、非法务工”，当事人就从应受保护的对象变成应被处罚的对象，报案意愿、身份认定与后续救助都会跟着塌掉。这条链之所以顺畅，还因为跨境的每一段都有合法外观——招工信息、旅游签、包车、口岸通关。于是出现一个尴尬局面：情报在两端都很清楚，中间的输送环节却始终没有被完整地当作一条链来管辖。

## 多国联合遣返：协作走到了哪一步

据公开报道，区域内多国已多次开展联合清查、解救与集中遣返，相关的警务联络与领保渠道也在常态化。成效是实在的：一批人得以脱身，部分资金与通信链条被切断，属地政府承受的外交压力上升，一些园区被迫关停或转移。

但遣返解决的是人回来了，不解决园区为什么还在。行动节奏不同步、情报口径不一、证据移交滞后，使园区常能在压力到来之前先搬走一批设备和人员。各类双边与次区域协作机制提供的是通道，不是统一的执法权；它们能把各方叫到同一张桌前，却不能替任何一方在境内办案。联合行动的价值因此常被高估为打击，其实更像一次压力测试：测出哪一段边境的配合最松、哪一环身份信息最难对齐、哪一级地方在关键时刻能动员得动。

## 主权、执法与证据：跨境协作的制度摩擦

主权原则与不干涉内政，是区域协作无法绕开的底色。跨境办案不能代为执法：取证要走司法协助或警务合作渠道，逮捕与起诉属属地管辖，身份核验、籍贯认定、遣返顺序又牵涉各自的移民法。于是摩擦几乎必然发生——一方手握聊天记录与转账线索，另一方需要的是本地可采的证据；一方希望尽快遣返，另一方得先走完本国程序。

承认摩擦存在，反而能让合作设计得更务实：把力气放在同步立案、证据格式标准化、受害者身份的一致认定上，而不是寄望某次高规格行动把边界抹平。真正提高治理效率的，往往是这些不出现在联合声明里的技术性安排。

## 地方财政与保护网络：园区为何拔不掉

园区给地方带来的是一整串现金流：土地租金、电费、保安、餐饮、运输、周边商铺。在财政吃紧、替代产业稀薄的边境省份，关停园区意味着同时失去税源、就业与寻租空间，阻力因此不只是腐败，也是一种真实的发展困境。

保护网络通常是分层的：土地出租方、保安、地方办事人员、武装护卫各取一环，谁都不必掌握全貌，也就谁都不必承担全责。这解释了清查、转移、重开的循环——打击针对的是可见的工位，而支撑它的合同、供电、通行与招工关系仍在原地。若没有替代财源、没有中央对地方的转移支付安排、没有面向边境青年的合法就业，任何一次成功清查都可能只是把问题挪到邻县。把园区当飞地，最容易误判的就是这一点：它不在管辖之外，而在激励之内。

## 从污名回到保护：把落差本身当作治理对象

受害者回国之后，常要面对“贪心”、“自愿”、“不听劝”这类评判。这种污名化看似只是舆论态度，后果却很具体：它压低报案率，让人不愿接受身份认定，也让家庭在要不要报警之间反复犹豫。于是同一条输送链的末端，又多了一道把人推回沉默的门槛。要接住他们，需要把是否为人口贩运受害者的认定前移并统一标准，配合心理、法律与就业支持；否则遣返只是把人从园区送回另一个困境，而园区仍能靠同样的招工话术补员。

回到最初的判断：园区不是治外法权飞地，而是地方保护与跨境监管落差共同喂养的产物。落差既在国与国之间，也在中央与地方之间、法律文本与执行之间、以及社会看待受害者的眼光里。治理的着力点因此不该只放在联合行动的声势上，而该落到补齐落差的日常工序：牌照与土地用途的穿透式审查、招聘链条的跨境可追溯、受害者身份的一致认定，以及给边境地方一条不靠灰产也能维持财政的路径。
## 延伸阅读

**经济特区（Special economic zone）**：以税收、土地与通关优惠招商引资的划定区域；在监管半径长、地方裁量大的边境地带，优惠可能被灰色业态借用为合规外观。

**人口贩运（Human trafficking）**：以欺骗、胁迫或债务束缚招募、运送并剥削人员；在欺骗下取得的同意，不排除案件的贩运性质，也不取消当事人受保护的资格。

**治外法权（Extraterritoriality）**：一国国民在驻在国不受当地管辖的特权；把电诈园区称作法外飞地，容易遮蔽它实际嵌在地方治理与财政激励之中的事实。

# Not an Enclave: Local Protection and the Regulatory Gap Behind Southeast Asia's Scam Compounds

Calling the scam compounds along Southeast Asia's borders lawless territory is convenient but misleading. By open reporting, such compounds tend to sit in border economic zones, around casinos or in stretches where several local authorities overlap, and they look like enclaves beyond the reach of state jurisdiction. The closer reading is that they are embedded in local governance — reliant on locally issued licences, leased land, connected electricity, tolerated passage, and a working channel that moves people and money in and out.

This distinction is more than wording. If the compounds truly sat outside jurisdiction, the problem would be how to extend state power into them. If they sit inside it, the problem becomes why local actors choose to keep them. A compound is not an extraterritorial enclave; it is the product of local protection and cross-border regulatory gaps feeding each other. Watching only the intensity of joint enforcement misses two more basic questions: what sustains these sites locally, and why people who are rescued still struggle to return to ordinary life.

## Border zones and casinos: how a regulatory vacuum is produced

Special economic zones and gaming licences were designed as tools to attract investment. In several border stretches of the Greater Mekong Subregion, zones carry tax, land and customs concessions, while casinos bring an environment of heavy cash, rapid turnover of people and loose identity checks. Where central oversight is distant, local discretion is broad and the border is hard to seal, licence economies tend to spill over: what is registered as a hotel, technology park or integrated resort operates in practice as enclosed scam workstations and dormitories.

The so-called vacuum is not an absence of law but a thinning of responsibility across overlapping jurisdictions. Zone authorities, local police, immigration offices, land lessors and central ministries each have their own mandate, yet rarely does one body own the whole chain from recruitment to settlement. The first form of the regulatory gap, then, is not along the national border but between agencies, and between central texts and local execution.

## Behind the high-paying job offer: a well-packaged supply chain

The compounds are staffed through a carefully packaged supply chain. The pitch is usually a customer-service or technical post with flights and accommodation included, spread across social platforms, agents and personal referrals. After departure, documents are taken, movement restricted, and people traded between compounds. Many observers note that consent in such cases is typically obtained by deception, and so should not simply rule out a characterisation of human trafficking.

The legal classification matters enormously. Once a person is filed as having gone voluntarily and worked illegally, they shift from someone entitled to protection into someone liable to penalty — and the willingness to report, the victim identification and the access to aftercare all collapse together. The chain runs smoothly because each cross-border segment has a lawful appearance: a job advertisement, a tourist visa, a chartered vehicle, a checkpoint. The awkward result is that intelligence is clear at both ends while the middle is never governed as a single chain.

## Joint repatriation: how far cooperation has actually come

By open reporting, countries in the region have repeatedly carried out joint raids, rescues and concentrated repatriations. The gains are real: people have got out, some payment links have been cut, and certain compounds have been forced to close or move.

But repatriation solves the problem of people getting home, not the question of why the compounds remain. Unsynchronised timing, inconsistent intelligence standards and slow evidence transfer mean sites can often move equipment and personnel before pressure arrives. Bilateral and sub-regional mechanisms provide channels, not a unified law-enforcement power: they can bring parties to one table but cannot run a case inside anyone's territory. The value of joint operations is thus often overstated as strike capability, when it is closer to a stress test — revealing which stretch of border coordinates least, which identity data is hardest to align, and which level of local authority can actually be mobilised.

## Sovereignty, enforcement and evidence: the friction built into cooperation

Sovereignty and non-interference are the background no regional cooperation can bypass. Cross-border cases cannot be enforced on another's behalf: evidence moves through judicial-assistance or police-cooperation channels, arrest and prosecution belong to local jurisdiction, and identity verification, nationality determination and repatriation order each engage domestic immigration law. Friction is therefore almost inevitable. One side holds chat logs and transfer trails; the other needs evidence admissible at home. One side wants swift repatriation; the other must complete its own procedures first.

Acknowledging the friction actually makes cooperation more practical: effort belongs in simultaneous case registration, standardised evidence formats and a shared understanding of victim status, rather than in hoping one high-profile operation will erase the border. What raises efficiency is usually technical work of this kind.

## Local finance and protection networks: why compounds are hard to uproot

A compound brings a whole stream of cash to a locality: land rent, electricity, security, food, transport and nearby shops. In border provinces with tight budgets and few alternative industries, shutting a site means losing revenue, jobs and rent-seeking space at once, so the resistance is not only corruption but a genuine development predicament.

Protection networks are usually layered. Land lessors, guards, local clerks and armed escorts each take one link; nobody needs to see the whole, and so nobody needs to own the whole. That explains the cycle of raid, relocation and reopening: enforcement targets visible workstations, while the contracts, power supply, passage and recruitment relations that support them stay in place. Without alternative revenue, central transfers to local budgets and lawful employment for border youth, even a successful raid may simply move the problem to the next district. Treating compounds as enclaves is precisely what invites this misreading — they are not outside jurisdiction, they are inside the incentive structure.

## From stigma back to protection: treating the gap itself as the object of governance

After returning home, victims often face judgements about greed, voluntariness or ignoring warnings. Such stigma looks like mere public attitude, but its effects are concrete: it lowers reporting rates, makes people reluctant to accept victim identification, and leaves families hesitating over whether to go to the police. The end of the same supply chain thus acquires one more threshold that pushes people back into silence. Catching them requires moving victim identification forward and harmonising the standard, backed by psychological, legal and employment support; otherwise repatriation simply carries a person from the compound into another predicament, while the site refills through the same recruitment pitch.

Back to the initial claim: a compound is not an extraterritorial enclave but the product of local protection and cross-border regulatory gaps feeding each other. The gaps lie between states, but also between centre and locality, between legal text and enforcement, and in the way society looks at victims. The leverage therefore belongs less to the spectacle of joint operations than to the daily work of closing those gaps — penetrating reviews of licences and land use, traceability of recruitment chains across borders, consistent victim identification, and a fiscal path that lets border provinces survive without grey industry.
## Further reading

**Special economic zone**: A designated area offering tax, land and customs incentives to attract investment; where oversight is distant and local discretion broad, such concessions can be borrowed as a compliant front.

**Human trafficking**: The recruitment, transport and exploitation of people through deception, coercion or debt bondage; consent obtained by fraud neither rules out trafficking nor removes a person's claim to protection.

**Extraterritoriality**: Exemption from a host state's jurisdiction; calling scam compounds lawless enclaves obscures the fact that they are embedded in local governance and fiscal incentives.
