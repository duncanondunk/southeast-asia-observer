# 一场"去中国化"的工业赌局：菲律宾制造业豪赌为何高风险

在小马科斯总统任内，菲律宾悄然接受了一套听起来简单、实则凶险的工业逻辑：建一个不依赖中国供应链的本土制造业。这套说辞很有诱惑力。当华盛顿把对华供应链依赖视为战略包袱时，把菲律宾包装成"去中国化"生产基地，既能换来安全背书，也能换来资本——美国的友岸外包（friend-shoring）资金、日本的联合融资，以及一张"产业回流"的入场券。可越往下看，这套战略越像一场赌局而非蓝图。这场"去中国化"赌局建立在一个菲律宾经济尚未挣得的预设之上：马尼拉能在切断当下支撑工厂运转的供应链的同时，凭空组装出有竞争力的工业底座。

## 一场"去中国化"的工业赌局

这套逻辑先是地缘政治的，其次才是经济的。自2023年美国、日本与菲律宾启动吕宋经济走廊（Luzon Economic Corridor）以来，华盛顿便把群岛包装成对华生产的可替代前沿。框架说得很直白：在从苏比克、克拉克延伸到马尼拉与八打雁的美方主导走廊里，布局半导体、关键矿产加工与清洁能源硬件，从而降低菲律宾乃至整个地区对北京的暴露。这场赌局的玄机在于，它想用地缘政治站位"制造"出比较优势。

## 数字：被中国供应链"喂大"的制造业

账本讲的故事不一样。过去十余年，中国多半时间都是菲律宾最大贸易伙伴，而菲律宾的拳头出口——电子产品与半导体——本质上是从海外进口高价值中间品再组装的业务，中国正是重要来源。现实是，菲律宾每多出口一个"菲律宾制造"，就从中国工厂多拉回一批零部件。官方贸易数据里，中国商品长期位居菲律宾进口来源前列，这种结构性依赖不是一句产业政策口号能一夜扭转的。

## 政策底盘：从吕宋经济走廊到芯片枢纽

平心而论，骨架是有的。吕宋经济走廊已吸引美国国际开发金融公司（DFC）的承诺与日本的基础设施融资，而华盛顿力推的"硅和平"（Pax Silica）计划，则要在新克拉克城锚定人工智能与半导体产能。美方在半导体与关键矿产领域的承诺，累计以数十亿美元计（约数百亿元人民币）。但承诺不是生产线。这些多半是框架、备忘录与早期设施——而不是能把一条走廊变成制造基地的密集供应商、分包商与零部件企业网络。

## 高成本难题：没有中国的零件，工厂转得动吗

这里有一个战略刻意回避的尴尬问题：菲律宾工厂没有中国零件，到底转不转得动？老实说，转不便宜。这个国家缺"去中国化"底座必备的三样东西——廉价的基荷电力、工业规模，以及密集的供应商网络。中国的中间品不只买得到，而且便宜、快、可定制。强行把采购移开，只会抬高单件成本，侵蚀菲律宾出口赖以生存的价格竞争力。不少本土制造商仍在从中国进货，因为没有任何替代方案能匹配那个"价格对交付"的比值。比国内生态成熟更快下达的"去中国化"命令，只会抬高每一件出厂货物的成本。

## 尴尬现实：越"去风险"，越离不开中国

有一重悖论是话术绕不开的。所谓"去风险"——回流、友岸外包、另起走廊——往往不是松开、反而是加深了对中国中间品的实际依赖，因为中国仍是全球最低成本的中间品生产者。即便是"中国+1"搬迁的优等生越南，其制造业中间品仍有超过一半来自中国工厂，它的出口繁荣也建立在中国零部件、电力设备与资本品之上。供应商基础更薄的菲律宾，暴露得更深。"多元化"的台词，撞上了采购的现实。

## 越南的前车之鉴

越南是马尼拉在下大注之前该研究的反面教材。河内花了多年招揽从中国迁出的供应链，可它的工厂仍拴在中国的中间品、中国的电力设备，以及越来越多的中国投资上。教训不是搬迁失败，而是"去中国化"在任何有意义的时间线上都只是可望而难即（aspirational），而非可达成。越南做到的，是"中国+"——增加产能与伙伴，而非删掉中国。工业底座更小的菲律宾，更不可能在越南都没能的地方一步跨到"去中国化"。

## 胜负手：能源、规模与供应商生态

三个变量将决定这场赌局是否回本。其一，电力：菲律宾电价居东南亚前列，是对每家工厂的隐形征税。其二，规模：友岸外包的激励能引来旗舰项目，却引不来撑起一个集群的长尾小供应商。其三，生态：一个半导体枢纽需要上百家配套企业，而非一家独苗。三者无一取得进展，"去中国化"标签只会描述少数样板工厂，而更广泛的经济体仍在从中国进口。产业政策不能靠一纸宣言凭空变出生态。

## 结论：能"中国+"，难"去中国"

现实的结论让两派都不舒服。"去中国化"制造底座更多是口号而非战略；可达成目标是"中国+"——分散供应商、深化与美日的纽带，并在菲律宾确有优势的领域（如业务流程外包（BPO）、农业与部分电子细分）培育真产能。吕宋经济走廊的地缘价值，在于它作为杠杆是真实的，作为自给自足则是虚的。把"去中国化"框架用力过猛，反而可能把菲律宾产品挤出全球市场，并且讽刺地加深它声称要治愈的那种脆弱。马尼拉的赌局值得下，但应当是对冲，而非宣言。

## 延伸阅读

- **友岸外包（Friend-shoring）**：把供应链迁往政治友好国家的做法，由华盛顿倡导，意在降低对华战略依赖。
- **吕宋经济走廊（Luzon Economic Corridor）**：2023年启动的美日菲基础设施与产业倡议，串接苏比克、克拉克、马尼拉与八打雁。
- **中国+1战略（China+1 strategy）**：企业在华之外增设产能、而非整体退出的多元化路径，保留对中国供应链的接入。

# Manila's 'China-Free' Gamble: Why the Philippines' Manufacturing Bet Is a High-Risk Wager

Under President Ferdinand Marcos Jr., the Philippines has quietly embraced an industrial thesis that sounds deceptively simple: build a domestic manufacturing base that does not depend on Chinese supply chains. The pitch is seductive. In an era when Washington treats supply-chain dependence on Beijing as a strategic liability, positioning the Philippines as a "China-free" production platform promises both security and capital — American friend-shoring dollars, Japanese co-financing, and a seat at the reshored manufacturing table. Yet the closer one looks, the more the strategy resembles a wager than a plan. The "de-China" (去中国化) wager rests on a premise the Philippine economy has not earned: that Manila can assemble a competitive industrial base while severing the very supply chains that currently make its factories run.

## A 'de-China' industrial wager

The logic is geopolitical before it is economic. After the 2023 launch of the Luzon Economic Corridor (吕宋经济走廊) by the United States, Japan and the Philippines, Washington began packaging the archipelago as a front-line alternative to Chinese-dominated production. The frame is explicit: build semiconductors, critical-minerals processing and clean-energy hardware in a U.S.-aligned corridor stretching from Subic through Clark to Manila and Batangas, and you reduce both Philippine and regional exposure to Beijing. The bet is that geopolitical alignment can manufacture comparative advantage.

## The numbers: a manufacturing fed by Chinese inputs

The arithmetic tells a different story. China has been the Philippines' largest trading partner for much of the past decade, and the country's flagship exports — electronics and semiconductors — are overwhelmingly assembly operations that import their high-value intermediates from abroad, China included. In practice, the more the Philippines ships out as "made in the Philippines," the more components it pulls in from Chinese factories. Official trade data show Chinese goods consistently ranking at or near the top of Philippine import sources, a structural dependence no industrial-policy slogan reverses overnight.

## The policy base: from the Luzon Economic Corridor to chip hubs

To be fair, the scaffolding exists. The Luzon Economic Corridor has attracted U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (美国国际开发金融公司) commitments and Japanese infrastructure financing, while Washington has promoted the Pax Silica (硅和平) initiative to anchor artificial-intelligence and semiconductor capacity in New Clark City. American officials have pledged sums that, in the semiconductor and critical-minerals space, run into the multi-billion-dollar range — tens of billions of pesos, or several hundred million U.S. dollars. Yet pledges are not production lines. Most of these are frameworks, memoranda and early-stage facilities, not the dense ecosystem of suppliers, subcontractors and component makers that turns a corridor into a manufacturing base.

## The cost problem: can factories run without Chinese parts?

Here is the uncomfortable question the strategy avoids: can Philippine factories actually run without Chinese parts? The honest answer is, not cheaply. The country lacks the three things a "China-free" base demands — affordable baseload power, industrial scale, and a dense supplier network. Chinese intermediates are not just available; they are cheap, fast and tailored. Forcing procurement away from them raises unit costs and erodes the price competitiveness that Filipino exports rely on. Local manufacturers, many of them, still import from China because no alternative matches the price-to-delivery ratio. A "China-free" mandate, imposed faster than the domestic ecosystem matures, simply raises the cost of every unit shipped.

## An awkward reality: the more you de-risk trade, the deeper the China reliance

There is a paradox the rhetoric cannot escape. The very act of "de-risking" — reshoring, friend-shoring, building alternative corridors — often deepens, rather than loosens, the practical reliance on Chinese inputs, because China remains the world's lowest-cost producer of the intermediates everyone needs. Even Vietnam, the poster child of "China+1" relocation, still sources more than half of its manufacturing intermediates from Chinese factories, and its export boom rides on Chinese components, electricity equipment and capital goods. The Philippines, with a thinner supplier base, is even more exposed. Diversification talking points collide with procurement reality.

## Vietnam's cautionary tale

Vietnam is the cautionary tale Manila should study before betting big. Hanoi spent years courting relocated supply chains from China, yet its factories remain tethered to Chinese inputs, Chinese power equipment and, increasingly, Chinese investment. The lesson is not that relocation fails, but that "China-free" is aspirational, not achievable, on any meaningful timeline. What Vietnam achieved was "China-plus" — adding capacity and partners, not deleting China. The Philippines, starting from a smaller industrial base, cannot credibly leap to "China-free" where Vietnam could not.

## What decides the wager: energy, scale and supplier ecosystems

Three variables will decide whether this gamble pays off. First, power: Philippine electricity is among the most expensive in Southeast Asia, a tax on every factory. Second, scale: friend-shoring incentives attract marquee projects but not the long tail of small suppliers that makes a cluster. Third, ecosystems: a semiconductor hub needs a hundred supporting firms, not one flagship. Without progress on all three, the "China-free" label will describe a handful of showcase plants while the broader economy keeps importing from China. Industrial policy cannot conjure an ecosystem by proclamation.

## Verdict: 'China-plus' is feasible, 'China-free' is not

The realistic conclusion is uncomfortable for both camps. A "China-free" manufacturing base is more slogan than strategy; the achievable goal is "China-plus" — diversifying suppliers, deepening ties with the United States and Japan, and building genuine capacity where the Philippines holds an edge, such as business-process outsourcing, agriculture and certain electronics niches. The geopolitical value of the Luzon Economic Corridor is real as leverage, not as autarky. Overplaying the "de-China" frame risks pricing Filipino products out of global markets and, ironically, deepening the very vulnerability it claims to cure. Manila's bet is worth making — but it should be hedged, not declared.

## Further reading

- **Friend-shoring (友岸外包)**: The practice of relocating supply chains to politically aligned countries, championed by Washington as a way to reduce strategic dependence on China.
- **Luzon Economic Corridor (吕宋经济走廊)**: A U.S.–Japan–Philippines infrastructure and industry initiative launched in 2023, linking Subic, Clark, Manila and Batangas.
- **China+1 strategy (中国+1战略)**: A diversification approach in which firms add production outside China rather than exiting it entirely, preserving access to Chinese supply chains.
