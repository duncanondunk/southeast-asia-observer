# 泉州闽南语博物馆揭牌：为千万南洋乡亲留住“声纹钥匙”

2026年9月30日，福建泉州古城通政巷，一座以闽南语为单一核心主题的专题博物馆正式揭牌。它是泉州首座、也大概率是国内少有的以“声音”而非“文物”为主角的县级专题馆。按主办方的说法，这座博物馆要“让文化可感、让乡音可触、让传承可为”。

这话听着像口号，但放在这场开幕的语境里，值得认真想一想。

## 一座博物馆，为何以“声音”为题

闽南语（Hokkien）不是一种摆在橱窗里的死语言。泉州方面把它称作打开古城的“声纹钥匙”——意思很直白：你听得懂几句闽南话，就摸得进这座城市的门。

博物馆设在鲤城，集方言展示、互动体验、研学教育、活态传承于一体，分“历史源流”“多彩乡音”“雅俗共赏”“薪火相传”四个篇章，把闽南讲古、闽南戏曲、闽南语歌曲这些本土文艺形式串成一条线。鲤城区委常委、宣传部部长林春晖说，它的落成标志着闽南语保护传承从“体系构建”迈向“阵地升级”。

我倒觉得，这句话点出了一个常被忽略的事实：语言保护到了今天，缺的早已不是“资料”，而是“场所”。教材、词典、音频库这些年并不少，可它们散在各处，普通人碰不到。把闽南语装进一座能走、能听、能玩的馆里，等于在祖籍地给这门方言立了一个固定的“家”。

## 闽南语：南洋华侨华人的共同乡音

真正让这座博物馆超出“地方文化”意义的，是它和南洋的关系。

闽南语是东南亚华侨华人中最通行的汉语方言。从泉州、漳州、厦门走出去的移民，构成了新加坡、马来西亚、菲律宾、印尼、泰国等地华人社会的主体。今天新加坡的“福建话”、马来西亚北部的“福建话”、菲律宾华人社区的“咱人话”，追根溯源都在这条闽南语脉里。许多东南亚华人家庭，祖辈一句普通话不会，却能用闽南语把族谱、食谱和童谣原样传下来。

所以“乡音可触”四个字，对海外乡亲的分量，可能比对本省年轻人还重。对一个出生在槟城或马尼拉的第四代华人来说，闽南语往往不是“方言”，而是他和“根”之间仅剩的那根活线——听得懂奶奶的祷词、庙里的签诗、歌台上的哭调，他才觉得自己“连得上”。泉州这座馆，某种程度上就是为这些回不来的耳朵准备的一把钥匙。

## 从“体系构建”到“阵地升级”

也要看到，闽南语博物馆不是孤立的。它和“泉腔鲤音”闽南语公益自学平台、乡音讲堂、计划推出的系列课程，是一整套组合拳。

这背后是一条清晰的侨务逻辑：新时代聚侨，不能只靠血缘叙事，还得有可参与、可带走的“文化产品”。语言是最低门槛的入口——你不必认祖归宗、不必改护照，只要愿意学一句“食饱未”，就算接上了线。把母语保护做成面向公众的体验项目，本质上是在为离散社群提供一个轻量、低风险的身份锚点。比起宏大的“文化认同”口号，这种“先让你玩起来”的思路，反而更可能留住年轻人。

## 数字化能否留住流动的乡音

博物馆的互动区用了触控发音、翻牌答题、AI换装、歌曲互动、声音邮局等数字化展项，还允许观众录制“专属乡音寄语”。

我对这部分最看好，也最存疑。看好，是因为它把单向展示变成了双向参与——尤其对远道而来的侨胞子弟，录一段自己的声音带回去，比看展板有意义得多。存疑，是因为方言的命根子在“日常使用”，不在“展馆体验”。一座馆再好，也救不了一种在家里不再被说的语言。

说到底，闽南语在南洋的存续，取决于槟城咖啡店里的闲聊、新加坡巴刹里的砍价、庙会戏台上的唱念，能不能一代代接下去。泉州把“声纹”存下来、把“钥匙”造出来，是尽了一份祖籍地的本分；但把钥匙转进锁里、把门推开，终究要靠海外的华人家庭自己。

这或许正是这类博物馆最诚实的定位：它不替谁守住乡音，只是确保——当有一天你想找回来时，那条路还在。

## 延伸阅读

**闽南语（Hokkien）**：又称福建话，属汉语闽语分支，是东南亚华人社会最通行的汉语方言之一，在新马菲印尼泰均有大量使用者。

**侨乡（Qiaoxiang）**：指海外移民集中的祖籍地，以福建、广东、海南、广西等地为代表；侨乡文化保护常兼有凝聚海外侨胞的功能。

**下南洋（Migration to Nanyang）**：近代闽粤民众向东南亚的大规模移民潮，奠定了今日东南亚华侨华人的方言与宗族格局。

# A Museum for Hokkien: Quanzhou Opens a Sound Archive for the Nanyang Diaspora

On 30 September 2026, in Tongzheng Lane inside the old town of Quanzhou, Fujian, a museum devoted to a single subject — the Minnan (Hokkien) language — was officially opened. It is the first such dedicated museum in Quanzhou, and probably one of the few county-level museums in China built around "sound" rather than artefacts. The stated mission is to make culture perceptible, the homeland tongue touchable, and inheritance actionable.

That triad sounds like boilerplate. In this particular opening, it deserves a closer look.

## Why a museum built around sound

Hokkien is not a dead language behind glass. Quanzhou calls it the "sound-print key" to its ancient city — the plainer point being: understand a few words of Minnan speech and you can walk through the city's door.

The museum, in Licheng district, combines dialect display, interactive experience, study-tour education and living transmission. Four chapters — "Origins," "Many Voices," "High and Low," "Passing the Torch" — weave together local storytelling (讲古), opera and Hokkien song. Lin Chunhui, the district propaganda chief, called the opening a step from "system-building" to "platform-upgrading" in dialect preservation.

That phrase, I think, names an overlooked truth: language protection today is no longer short of materials but of places. Textbooks, dictionaries and audio archives have multiplied, yet they sit scattered and unreachable to ordinary people. To house Hokkien in a walkable, listenable, playable building is to give the dialect a fixed "home" in its ancestral county.

## Hokkien: the shared mother tongue of the Nanyang

What lifts this museum beyond "local culture" is its tie to the Nanyang.

Hokkien is the most widely spoken Chinese dialect among Southeast Asian ethnic Chinese. Migrants from Quanzhou, Zhangzhou and Xiamen form the core of the Chinese communities in Singapore, Malaysia, the Philippines, Indonesia and Thailand. The "Hokkien" of Singapore, northern Peninsular Malaysia and Philippine Chinese homes all trace back to this Minnan stream. For many overseas families, the grandparents spoke no Mandarin at all, yet passed down genealogies, recipes and nursery rhymes intact in Hokkien.

So the words "touchable homeland tongue" may weigh more on overseas kin than on local youth. To a fourth-generation ethnic Chinese born in Penang or Manila, Hokkien is often not a "dialect" but the last living thread to their "roots" — understand grandmother's prayers, the temple lot-poetry, the operatic sobs on a song stage, and they feel connected. In a sense, Quanzhou's museum is a key made for ears that may never return.

## From "framework" to "platform"

It should also be read as part of a set, not a standalone. The museum sits alongside the "Quanqiang Liyin" public self-study platform, a dialect lecture hall and planned serial courses.

Behind this is a clear diaspora logic: bonding overseas Chinese today cannot rest on kinship narrative alone; it needs participatory, takeaway "cultural products." Language is the lowest-threshold entry — you need not claim ancestry or change a passport; learning one phrase like "have you eaten?" already connects the line. Turning mother-tongue preservation into a public experience is, at bottom, offering the dispersed community a light, low-risk anchor of identity. Compared with grand slogans about "cultural identity," this "let them play first" approach is more likely to keep the young.

## Can digitisation keep a living dialect alive

The interactive zone uses touch-to-pronounce, flip-card quizzes, AI costume-swapping, song interaction and a "voice post office," and lets visitors record a personal "hometown-tongue message."

This is the part I find most promising and most doubtful. Promising, because it turns one-way display into two-way participation — for diaspora youth who travel far, recording their own voice to take home means more than any panel. Doubtful, because a dialect lives by daily use, not by exhibition. No museum, however fine, saves a language no longer spoken at home.

In the end, Hokkien's survival in the Nanyang depends on idle chat in a Penang kopitiam, haggling in a Singapore market, singing on a temple stage — passed generation to generation. Quanzhou has stored the "sound-print" and forged the "key," doing its duty as the ancestral county. But turning the key in the lock is for overseas Chinese families themselves.

Perhaps that is the honest positioning of such museums: they do not guard the tongue for anyone; they only make sure that, when one day you want it back, the road is still there.

## Further reading

**Hokkien (Minnan dialect):** Also called "Hokkien" or "Fujianhua," a branch of Chinese Min speech and among the most spoken Chinese dialects in Southeast Asian ethnic-Chinese communities.

**Qiaoxiang (侨乡):** Ancestral hometowns of overseas migrants, concentrated in Fujian, Guangdong, Hainan and Guangxi; cultural preservation there often doubles as diaspora cohesion.

**Migration to Nanyang (下南洋):** The modern mass emigration from Fujian and Guangdong to Southeast Asia that shaped today's dialect and clan map of the region's Chinese communities.
