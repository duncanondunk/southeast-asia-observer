# 武术落地海外：华侨华人如何把拳脚变成文化桥梁

**副标题**：当武术在达喀尔青奥会首次成为奥运系列赛事的正式比赛项目，真正把这门技艺播向世界的，是一代代华侨华人开设的武馆、校园课程与赛事平台——而非任何宏大的官方叙事。

中国侨网（China Qiao Wang）近日刊发的一组报道，把镜头对准了海外习武、传武的普通人。达喀尔青奥会将首次把武术列为奥运系列赛事的正式比赛项目，这则消息很容易被归入「体育」版块，但其意义远不止奖牌。数十年来，中国武术在海外的传播，靠的从来不是大使馆或文化中心，而是开武馆的侨胞、教功夫的老师、社区里的领头人。他们从临街铺面与学校礼堂里，把这门技艺一代代传了出去。

## 达喀尔时刻：迟到的正式承认

武术进入青奥会，是国际武术联合会与中国体育界数十年努力的制度性成果。但「正式」二字的落地，始终滞后于地面上的现实。早在任何奥运旗帜升起之前，中国武术就已经是纽约、墨尔本、马六甲等地移民社区的日常风景。达喀尔的意义，不应被理解为武术全球生命的开端，而应被视为机构终于追上了那群默默做了半个世纪文化播种的华侨华人。

这一区分至关重要。评论者惯于把文化传播想象成从中心向外辐射的单向流动。武术的故事恰好相反：边缘——海外的华侨华人社区——搭建起了基层基础设施，中心如今只是在追认它。

## 从少林到纽约：先行者

最经典的传武模式，是师徒制或家族式的武馆。美国武术家协会会长蓝超海2004年参与开办纽约少林寺功夫中心，至今已教授数千名学员。他的教学分班进行：小朋友培养兴趣，青少年学习防身自卫，中老年以养生调理为主。这是一种披着传统外衣的小生意模式——而它之所以奏效，正因为它在社区最需要的地方出现。

蓝超海的话也耐人寻味。他说：「中国功夫不以武欺人，但也不任人欺负，关键是让学员懂得自卫与克制的分寸。」这是剥去术语后的软实力：一门教人自信而不好斗的技艺。对于在身份认同中摸索的二代移民而言，武馆提供了一个有分寸的空间，让他们可以认领自己的文化传承（heritage），而不必臣服于它。

## 学校成为新武馆：澳大利亚与语言捆绑

在澳大利亚，传播的主渠道是课堂。登封市少林武术协会国际特邀主席李复新在澳开办标准中文学校多年，武术常年列入课程。他的观点是：语言是外在表现形式，武术是内在修炼方式，二者同属推广中华文化的重要载体。澳大利亚的武术推广多依托社会俱乐部，而李复新特别提到一个良性循环：不少澳大利亚人赴中国学武后，回国开设武馆、公开收徒。

这里有一处值得驻足的细节。李复新呼吁传武人打破「传内不传外」的门户之见，并借鉴跆拳道等项目的考级激励机制。换言之：如果武术想让孩子坚持下去，就必须借用让韩国武术成为全球特许经营业态的那套「游戏化」设计。事实证明，光靠文化自豪感还不够，你还需要一架可以逐级攀登的目标阶梯。

## 马来西亚（Malaysia）的制度化路径：武术进了体育课

马来西亚展示了一旦离散社群的努力遇上国家承认会发生什么。这个国家已将武术列为学校正式体育项目。马来西亚武术总会总会长颜天禄表示，这让武术班有了明确的课程依托，学生参赛、训练有了清晰的认可路径，教练进校园也有了稳定的制度依据。在马六甲州，武术培训从小学、中学一直延伸到大学，学员超过1000人，形成持续输送新苗的通道；马来西亚国家武术队中，有多名选手来自马六甲。武术总会正准备复办全国武术锦标赛。

马来西亚之所以成为值得研究的案例，在于其华人社区规模大、根基深、组织力强。当西方的武术还依赖个体武馆时，在马来西亚，它已被织入公立教育体系——一种文化所能拥有的最有效分发网络。

## 拳脚之外：离散社群真正输出的东西

退一步看，真正被输出的并非招式，而是一种面向世界的姿态。海外的武术传播者，正在贩卖一个关于中国文化的特定叙事：有纪律、不好斗、重养生、亲家庭。在一个「中国影响力」常被置于零和（zero-sum）安全语境下讨论的时代，郊区里的一间功夫班，反倒是一位格外令人卸下防备的大使。

这正是离散社群主导的文化工作所具备的安静优势。它自愿、在地、人对人。它从不以宣传自居，而恰恰是这一点，让它比那些抢占头条的「文化中心」（cultural centre）更耐久。

## 难解之题：可持续性与本真性

这一切远未尘埃落定。对魅力型个人师父的依赖，本身就是一种脆弱——武馆主一旦退休或迁居，整条输送链可能崩塌。借鉴跆拳道的考级体系固然务实，却也引出本真性的拷问：多少程度的适配算吸收，多少程度又算稀释？而奥运承认固然可喜，却也暗含把武术重新编码为一项剥去哲学内核的「运动」的风险——恰恰丢掉了蓝超海所珍视的「禅武合一」。

华侨华人已经完成了最难的部分：他们把武术种进异国的土壤，并让它活过了数十年。真正悬而未决的问题是，下一章——奥运化、制度化、游戏化的武术——还能否保住它当初值得被输出的那些东西。

# Wushu Goes Global: How Overseas Chinese Turn Martial Arts into a Cultural Bridge

**Subtitle**: As wushu debuts at the Dakar Youth Olympics, a quiet diaspora effort — studios, school programmes and tournaments — is doing what state-led cultural diplomacy often cannot.

China Qiaowang's recent round-up trains its lens on the ordinary people who teach and practise martial arts abroad. At the upcoming Youth Olympic Games in Dakar, wushu will appear for the first time as an official event in the Olympic series. The story is easy to shelve under "sport," yet its significance reaches well beyond medals. For decades, the global spread of Chinese martial arts was carried not by embassies or cultural centres, but by studio owners, instructors and community leaders among the overseas Chinese — who passed the art down from storefronts and school halls, generation after generation.

## A milestone at Dakar

Wushu's inclusion in the Youth Olympics is the institutional capstone of decades of campaigning by the International Wushu Federation and Chinese sporting bodies. But the word "official" has always lagged the reality on the ground. Long before any Olympic banner was raised, Chinese martial arts were already everyday scenery in immigrant neighbourhoods from New York to Melbourne to Melaka. The Dakar moment is best read not as the birth of wushu's global life, but as the moment the institutions finally caught up with the overseas Chinese who had been sowing the cultural seed for half a century.

That distinction matters. Commentary tends to imagine cultural diffusion as a one-way flow radiating outward from a centre. The wushu story inverts it: the periphery — the overseas Chinese communities — built the grass-roots infrastructure, and the centre is now merely ratifying it.

## From the Shaolin temple to New York storefronts

The classic model of transmission is the master-led or family-run studio. Lan Chaohai, president of the US Wushu Association, helped open the New York Shaolin Temple Kung Fu Center in 2004 and has since taught several thousand students. His classes are tiered: children build interest, teenagers learn self-defence, older adults focus on wellness. It is a small-business model wrapped in tradition — and it works precisely because it meets communities where they are.

Lan's framing is also telling. "Chinese kung fu does not use force to bully others, but neither does it let others bully you," he says. "The point is to teach students the measure between self-defence and restraint." That is soft power stripped of its jargon: a discipline that sells self-confidence without aggression. For second-generation immigrants feeling their way through identity, the studio offers a measured space to claim a heritage without submitting to it.

## Schools as the new dojo

In Australia, the main channel is the classroom. Li Fuxin, international honorary chairman of the Dengfeng Shaolin Wushu Association, has run the Standard Chinese School for years with wushu built into the curriculum. His argument: language is the external form, wushu the internal cultivation — two carriers of the same culture. Australia's wushu promotion leans on social clubs, and Li notes a healthy pipeline: Australians who train in China often return home to open their own studios and take on public students.

Here is a detail worth pausing on. Li urges practitioners to abandon the old "teach insiders, not outsiders" gatekeeping and to borrow the ranking-and-belt incentive systems from taekwondo. In plain terms: if wushu wants children to stay, it must borrow the gamified design that turned Korean martial arts into a global franchise. Cultural pride, it turns out, is not enough; you also need a ladder of achievable goals.

## Malaysia's institutional path

Malaysia shows what happens when a diaspora's effort meets state recognition. The country has listed wushu as a formal school sport. Gan Tian Lok, president of the Malaysian Wushu Federation, says this gives classes a clear curricular footing, gives students a recognised path to compete and train, and gives coaches a stable institutional basis to enter schools. In Melaka state, wushu classes run from primary through university, with more than 1,000 students and a steady stream of new talent; several national-team athletes come from Melaka. The federation is preparing to revive the national wushu championship.

Malaysia matters as a case study because its Chinese community is large, deeply rooted and well organised. Where wushu in the West still depends on individual studios, in Malaysia it has been woven into the public education system — the single most effective distribution network a culture can have.

## Beyond kicks and punches

Step back, and the real export is not technique but a posture toward the world. The overseas carriers of wushu are selling a particular story about Chinese culture: disciplined, non-aggressive, health-oriented, family-friendly. In an era when "Chinese influence" is so often framed in zero-sum security terms, a suburban kung fu class is a remarkably disarming ambassador.

This is the quiet advantage of diaspora-led cultural work. It is voluntary, local and person-to-person. It never bills itself as propaganda, and precisely for that reason it outlasts the cultural centres that grab the headlines.

## The hard questions

None of this is settled. The reliance on charismatic individual masters is itself a fragility — when a studio owner retires or moves, the whole pipeline can collapse. Borrowing taekwondo's ranking system is pragmatic, yet it raises a question of authenticity: how much adaptation counts as absorption, and how much as dilution? And the Olympic recognition, welcome as it is, risks recoding wushu as a sport stripped of its philosophical content — the very "chan-wu he yi," the unity of Zen and martial arts, that Lan Chaohai prizes.

The overseas Chinese have done the hard part: they planted wushu in foreign soil and kept it alive for decades. The open question is whether the next chapter — Olympic, institutionalised, gamified — can hold on to what made it worth exporting in the first place.
