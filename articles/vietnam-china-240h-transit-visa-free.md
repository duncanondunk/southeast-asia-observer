# 十天、免签：中国对越过境免签放开，究竟释放什么信号

2026年8月20日，中国国家移民管理局发布公告，持普通护照的吉尔吉斯斯坦、越南公民自此可适用中国240小时（十天）过境免签政策，以及海南30天入境免签政策。数字上看，这只是一次小幅调整——240小时过境免签的适用国由55国增至57国，海南30天入境免签适用国由59国增至61国。但值得停下来细想。在中国与周边国家关系每日都在走钢丝的东南亚，对越南公民单方面免签，绝非单纯的行政微调，而是中国所谓"周边外交"的一枚小巧而刻意的棋子，也折射出北京在东南亚争夺亲近与好感的底层逻辑。

这件事容易被忽略，因为它机制上太技术化。但累积的方向很清楚，而且已经清楚了很多年。中国的对外人员流动制度一直在稳步放宽，越南的纳入只是最新一步。真正有意思的问题不是"今年能多来多少游客"，而是"这一步暴露出这套制度本身的逻辑是什么"。

## 政策本身：从55国到57国

机制很简单。持普通护照、且持有已确定日期与行程、前往第三国或地区的联程客票的越南或吉尔吉斯斯坦公民，如今可经北京、上海等65个对外开放口岸免签入境，在规定区域内停留不超过240小时。同一批旅客也可由海南对外开放口岸入境，在海南全省免签停留不超过30天。停留期间可从事旅游、商贸、访问、探亲等短期活动；工作、学习、新闻采访等仍须事先办妥签证。移民管理局把这步定义为"稳步推进高水平对外开放、推动构建周边命运共同体的务实举措"，意在"进一步促进双方互信合作"，让旅游商贸、访问投资"更加便利高效"。

两点值得注意。其一，增量极小，但弧线不小。中国过境免签制度2013年起步时仅72小时、只限少数城市，后扩至144小时，2024年底起在数十个口岸通行240小时；适用国数量同步攀升。越南的名字，只是这份被有意拉长、而非偶然变长的名单上最新的一行。其二，官方措辞并非闲笔。当边境管理部门用"周边命运共同体"的语言来解释一次免签，它其实是在精确地告诉我们：这一姿态要被读作善意，而不只是便利化。

## 过境免签的本质：一次"转机红利"

免签到底放开了什么，标题往往夸大。这不是旅游签证。要使用它，旅客必须处于"过境"状态——持有前往第三国或地区的已确认联程票。它的好处是在中国境内中转时，可免签停留最多十天，而非以中国为最终目的地去旅行。这个约束决定了谁真正受益：经北京或上海中转前往东京、悉尼、顺道在中国多留几天的转机客。

读对了，这项政策关乎枢纽野心甚于待客之道。北京和上海想成为亚洲航空流动的"连接组织"，而慷慨的中转窗口，正是你从香港、仁川、新加坡手里抢转机流量的办法。这份"红利"流向中国枢纽，以及它们所支撑的短停访客经济。它是披着外交外衣的流动基础设施——这本身没有错，前提是我们把它的名字叫对，而不是把一项路由激励误认成开放边境。

## 为什么是越南：周边外交的一枚棋子

两个新入列国家中，吉尔吉斯斯坦是更安静的故事——一个中亚上合伙伴，本就是北京"周边"外联的自然对象。越南才是那个有意思的。河内与北京共享共产党血脉，贸易纽带深厚，但双方也深陷持久的南海争端，以及一场低烈度战略竞争——越南拉拢美国、日本、印度以平衡中国分量。在此背景下，对越南公民单方面免签，是一条经由最柔软渠道送达的增信姿态：动的是人，不是舰船或声明。

关键在于，海南30天入境免签，对越南旅客才是更实在的一半。海南与越南北部隔海相望、一水之隔；岛上一个月、免签证，是真正用得上的度假产品，而非过境脚注。如果北京想给一段被政治持续吹冷的关系注入民间暖意，这座岛是它手里最务实的杠杆——也最不容易在安保版面上引发头条。

## 海南方阵：最务实的开放端口

海南值得单独成行分析，因为30天政策做到了过境政策做不到的事：它让越南人以为休闲而来，而非作为一个路过旅客。对一个被北京定为自贸港与旅游名片的省份而言，扩大免签池就是直接的刺激。越南游客地理相近、价格敏感，正是一座热带海岛最想要的市场；而免签去掉了抑制短途休闲旅行最常见的两道摩擦——成本与手续。

但请注意这杠杆的不对称。中国单向开门。越南公民进入海南或中转窗口以外的中国，仍面临签证要求；而中国公民赴越，依的是河内自己的电子签与入境规则，而非对等的中式豁免。这一姿态慷慨，却也是单向的——这是北京当下开展流动外交的特征，而非缺陷，也提醒我们：这一口径下的"开放"，意思是"我们会让你更容易进来"，而非"我们会与你相向而行"。

## 不对等的事实：单向免签与软实力的天花板

这里是被欢喜头条忽略的锋利边缘。中国免签圈在清零后迅速扩大，总量数字好看。但架构依然不对称：北京单方面给予便利，对等互惠是例外而非常态。对越南公民而言，这意味着进出中国更便利，但中国人反向赴越并无对等便利——这一模式在多数新纳入国身上重复。

这种不对称未必是失败。一个有严格边境管控的大国，可以把单向开放当作信心的信号——"我们开放，来看看"——而不必让渡自身的准入裁量。风险在修辞层面。当同一项政策被包装为构建"周边命运共同体"，"伸出的邀请"与"未被回应的邀请"之间的落差，读起来会是等级而非伙伴。软实力在门双向摆动时最有效；一扇单向的门，无论多宽，都有天花板。十天免签生成的暖意可以是真的，却被互惠的沉默所框定。

## 结语：开放叙事与未竟的互免

那么，两份名单上多了两个国家，我们该如何看待？若只数今年受影响的旅客，没多少。若把它读成中国对外流动制度十年有意拓宽的一个数据点，则很多——这套制度如今横跨其中亚与东南亚两条周边弧线，并用最谦卑的器具——中转柜台免盖的一枚护照章——来诉说某种关于开放的事。

对越南而言，信号是双重编码的：一项实在的旅游与中转便利，也是一句安静的提醒——北京更愿意用签证与海滩度假村、而非手边更锋利的器具，去争夺越南的好感。河内是否会以自己的豁免相向，是改天的问题，且大概率缓慢。在此之前，这240小时，是就"亲近"而下的一笔小巧、有用、且刻意单向的赌注——形式上慷慨，目的上算计，在不愿给予什么这件事上，反而袒露无遗。

# Ten Days, No Visa: What China's Transit-Free Opening to Vietnam Really Signals

On 20 August 2026, China's National Immigration Administration (NIA) announced that ordinary passport holders from Kyrgyzstan and Vietnam may now use the country's 240-hour transit visa-free policy and Hainan province's 30-day visa-free entry scheme. The adjustment is numerically small — it lifts the 240-hour transit programme from 55 to 57 participating countries and Hainan's inbound visa-free list from 59 to 61 — but it is worth pausing over. In a region where Beijing's relations with its neighbours are a daily balancing act, a quiet waiver of the visa requirement for Vietnamese citizens is not merely an administrative tweak. It is a small, deliberate instrument of what China calls "peripheral diplomacy," and it says something about how Beijing intends to compete for proximity and goodwill in Southeast Asia.

The story is easy to miss because the mechanism is technical. Yet the cumulative direction is clear, and it has been clear for years. China's external mobility regime has been widening steadily, and Vietnam's inclusion is the latest step. The interesting question is not "how many tourists will this move this year" but "what does the move reveal about the logic of the regime itself."

## The policy: from 55 to 57 countries

The mechanics are straightforward. A Vietnamese or Kyrgyz citizen holding an ordinary passport and a connecting ticket with a confirmed date and itinerary to a third country or region may now enter China visa-free through any of 65 open ports — Beijing, Shanghai and others — and remain within designated areas for up to 240 hours. The same travellers may also land in Hainan through the province's open ports and stay anywhere in Hainan for up to 30 days. During the stay they may tour, trade, visit or see relatives; work, study and journalism still require a visa obtained in advance. The NIA framed the step as a "practical measure to steadily advance high-level opening-up and build a community with a shared future with neighbouring countries," meant to "further promote mutual trust and cooperation" and make tourism, trade and investment "more convenient and efficient."

Two observations follow. First, the increment is tiny, yet the arc is not. China's transit visa-free scheme began in 2013 at 72 hours for a handful of cities, expanded to 144 hours, and since late 2024 has run at 240 hours across dozens of ports; the country count has climbed in step. Vietnam's name is simply the newest on a list that has been lengthening by design, not by accident. Second, the official wording is not incidental. When a border-control agency explains a visa waiver in the language of "a community with a shared future," it is telling us precisely how the gesture is meant to be read: as goodwill, not merely as facilitation.

## What transit visa-free really is: a "transit dividend"

It is easy to overstate what a transit visa-free grants, and headlines routinely do. This is not a tourist visa. To use it, the traveller must be in transit — holding a confirmed onward ticket to a third country or region. The benefit is the right to break a journey on Chinese soil for up to ten days without a visa, not to visit China as a final destination. That constraint decides who actually benefits: transfer passengers routing through Beijing or Shanghai to Tokyo or Sydney who decide to add a few days in China on the way.

Read correctly, the policy is less about hospitality than about hub ambition. Beijing and Shanghai want to be the connective tissue of Asian air travel, and a generous transit window is how you pull transfer traffic away from Hong Kong, Incheon or Singapore. The "dividend" flows to Chinese hubs and to the short-stay visitor economy they anchor. It is mobility infrastructure dressed as diplomacy — and there is nothing wrong with that, provided we name it correctly rather than mistaking a routing incentive for an open border.

## Why Vietnam: a piece in peripheral diplomacy

Of the two new entrants, Kyrgyzstan is the quieter story — a Central Asian SCO partner for whom Beijing's "peripheral" outreach is a natural fit. Vietnam is the interesting one. Hanoi and Beijing share a Communist Party lineage and deep trade ties, but they are also locked in a persistent South China Sea dispute and a low-grade strategic competition in which Vietnam has courted the United States, Japan and India to balance Chinese weight. Against that backdrop, a unilateral visa facilitation for Vietnamese citizens is a confidence-building gesture delivered through the softest possible channel: people moving, not ships or statements.

Crucially, Hainan's 30-day visa-free entry is the more consequential half for a Vietnamese traveller. Hainan sits a short hop across the sea from northern Vietnam; a month on the island, no visa, is a genuinely usable holiday product, not a transit footnote. If Beijing wants to put people-to-people warmth into a relationship that politics keeps cool, the island is the most practical lever it has — and the one least likely to provoke a headline in the security pages.

## The Hainan vector: the most practical opening

Hainan deserves its own line of analysis because the 30-day scheme does what the transit policy cannot: it lets a Vietnamese arrive for leisure rather than as a passing-through passenger. For a province that Beijing has designated a free-trade port and a tourism showcase, widening the visa-free pool is a direct stimulus. Vietnamese visitors, geographically proximate and price-sensitive, are exactly the market a tropical island wants, and a waiver removes the single most common frictions — cost and paperwork — that suppress short-haul leisure travel.

But note the asymmetry of the lever. China opens its door one way. Vietnamese citizens still face visa requirements to enter China beyond Hainan or the transit window, and Chinese citizens travelling to Vietnam do so under Hanoi's own e-visa and entry rules, not a reciprocal Chinese-style waiver. The gesture is generous, yet it is also one-directional — a feature, not a bug, of how Beijing presently conducts mobility diplomacy, and a reminder that "opening up" in this register means "we will let you in more easily," not "we will meet you halfway."

## The asymmetry: one-way visa-free and the ceiling of soft power

Here is the sharp edge that the cheerful headlines miss. China's visa-free circle has expanded rapidly since the end of zero-COVID, and the aggregate numbers look impressive. Yet the architecture remains asymmetrical: Beijing grants facilitation unilaterally, and reciprocity is the exception rather than the rule. For Vietnamese citizens this means easier passage through and to China, but no matching ease for Chinese heading the other way — a pattern repeated with most of the newly added states.

That asymmetry is not necessarily a failure. A large country with strict border controls can use one-way opening as a signal of confidence — "we are open, come and see" — without surrendering its own admission discretion. The risk is rhetorical. When the same policy is packaged as building a "community with a shared future," the gap between the invitation extended and the invitation not returned can read as hierarchy rather than partnership. Soft power works best when the door swings both ways; a one-way door, however wide, has a ceiling. The warmth generated by a ten-day waiver can be real, but it is bounded by the silence on reciprocity.

## Conclusion: the openness narrative and the unfinished reciprocity

So what should we make of two countries added to a list? Not much, if we count only the travellers affected this year. A great deal, if we read the move as a data point in a decade-long, deliberate broadening of China's external mobility regime — one that now stretches across both its Central Asian and Southeast Asian peripheries, and that uses the humblest of instruments, a passport stamp waived at a transit desk, to say something about openness.

For Vietnam, the signal is doubly coded: a practical tourism and transit convenience, and a quiet reminder that Beijing prefers to compete for Vietnamese goodwill through visas and beach resorts rather than through the sharper instruments available to it. Whether Hanoi reciprocates with its own waiver is a question for another day, and probably a slow one. Until then, the 240 hours stand as a small, useful, and deliberately one-sided wager on proximity — generous in form, calculated in purpose, and revealing in what it declines to offer.
