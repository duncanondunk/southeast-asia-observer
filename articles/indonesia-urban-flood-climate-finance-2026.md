# 适应资金流向可计价的工程：印尼城市洪涝背后的估值盲区

雅加达的洪水常被当作季节新闻，实则叠加了两类缓慢位移：城市地面在下沉，海平面在抬升。排水渠、泵站与河网整治不断加码，潮汐仍会在雨季顺着低洼街区倒灌。到2026年，随着迁都努山塔拉推进，一个问题反而更清楚：当国家叙事转向新都，留下来的城市该由谁、按什么口径来保护。

气候适应融资被寄予厚望，但据公开报道与多方观察，资金的实际流向长期偏向可计价的工程——有造价、有招标、有可交付物、可写进项目评估的混凝土。最需要保护的人群往往最难估值：非正式聚落、租户、无产权证的家庭、靠日薪维生的住户。本文的核心判断由此而来：适应资金总是流向可计价的工程，而非最需要保护但最难估值的人群。

## 一座在下沉的城市与不断抬高的水位

雅加达长期依赖地下水，抽取与高层建筑荷载让部分地区持续沉降；与此同时海平面上升、潮汐顶托，风暴潮与季节性降雨彼此叠加。当地俗称的潮汐洪水（rob）并不限于暴雨，晴天的涨潮也能漫过堤岸。一般而言，这类复合风险很难归因到单一成因，治理却惯于按单一部门切分，于是「水从哪里来」与「地为什么在降」被写进两份不同的报告。

排水与河网整治因此成为第一反应：疏浚河道、加高堤岸、增设泵站、清理侵占河道的建筑。这些措施确有实效，却也把问题表述成「水太多」，而回避了「地面在降」「人住在哪里」。据公开报道，历次整治多伴随沿河聚落的清退，工程进度与社会成本被分开记账：前者有验收单，后者往往只出现在事后争议里。

## 迁都之后，被留下的城市

迁都努山塔拉是一项长期制度工程，意在疏解雅加达的人口与行政负荷，并带动加里曼丹的开发。但国家层面的注意力与财政叙事一旦转向新都，旧都的适应需求并不会随之消失：它仍是金融、就业与港口的集中地，仍有大量人口每天与潮水共处。首都的称谓可以搬走，暴露在风险中的人搬不走。

由此形成一种错位。新都可以按最新标准从零规划，旧都却要在既有管网、既有产权、既有聚落上做增量修补；政治上「迁都」容易被讲成问题的解决，工程上它只是把一部分风险留在原处。多方观察认为，判断一份适应承诺是否可信，恰恰要看那些不具备象征意义、也不易写进宣传材料的日常维护——清淤、泵站电费、排水口的垃圾，年复一年，无人剪彩。

## 河网整治与可计价的工程

适应资金偏好工程，有其技术理由。排水渠、泵站、海堤、水库都有清晰的工程量清单、造价、工期与验收标准，可纳入成本效益分析，也便于多边开发银行与气候基金的项目审批、采购与审计。相较之下，社区预警、疏散演练、租户补偿、生计恢复，收益分散、周期长、计量口径不齐，很难在一份项目文件里排成整齐的表格。

于是「可计价」变成一道隐形筛子。同样一笔钱，修一段堤可以立刻形成可核查的资产，而让低洼住户免于反复搬迁的价值，往往只能以「避免的损失」间接估算。据公开报道，项目文件里最难填的常常不是工程参数，而是受益人的社会基线。工程并非不该做；问题在于，当计价体系只认混凝土，保护就会被悄悄等同于建设，凡是无法折成工程量的需求，都显得像附加题。

## 门槛：谁能拿到气候适应资金

获取国际适应资金本身是一道门槛。项目需经国家指定主管机构与认证实体，配齐可行性研究、环境与社会影响评估、采购规则、赠款或还款安排，还要证明额外的气候理据。对中央部会与大型承包商而言这是流程，对地方社区、小型非政府组织而言，往往是一整套负担不起的咨询、翻译与合规成本。

更关键的是能力分布。最脆弱的城市往往财政最紧、数据最薄、土地台账最不完整，恰恰最不擅长把需求写成符合模板的项目书。一般而言，资金会先抵达有能力申报的一端，再层层下沉；下沉过程中，最难估值的人群继续被排在后面。这不是某个机构的偏见，而是计价口径与申报制度共同产生的系统性漏损——它运转得越规范，越容易把不规范的生活排除在外。

## 保险、土地权属与最难估值的人群

保险常被提出作为补充，但它要求可界定的标的、可核实的地址与可保利益。河岸与低洼地带的非正式聚落，权属不清、租约口头、房屋无正式门牌，一旦受灾，既难投保也难理赔；搬迁补偿又常以产权证明为前提。于是最暴露于洪水的人，最不具备被金融工具识别的资格。风险最集中的地方，恰恰是可保性最稀薄的地方。

土地权属还牵出搬迁争议。加固堤岸或退让河道需要腾地，居民担心“以安全为名”的清退把生计推得更远；缺少可信的补偿与回迁安排，工程反而加深不信任。多方观察认为，脆弱性之所以难以货币化，不是因为它小，而是因为它落在家庭、邻里与非正式营生里，没有发票、没有台账、没有市场报价。

## 把估值口径转向人

要修正偏向，不必否定工程，而是扩大什么算「适应」。社区层面的小额补助、参与式风险绘图、租户与无产权住户的补偿资格、疏散与预警的常态化经费，都可以有清晰的绩效指标；把避免的搬迁成本、减少的停工期、生计恢复速度纳入成本效益，保护就不再只能靠混凝土来证明。

更根本的是把治理读数换一换：少看开工里程，多看反复受灾的户数、理赔覆盖、回迁比例与社区对方案的接受度。回到开头，适应资金流向可计价的工程，是制度可理解的偏好，不是阴谋；但要让资金抵达最需要保护的人，就得先承认有些价值不该、也不能只按工程造价来算。对留下的雅加达如此，对东盟其他沿海城市亦然。
## 延伸阅读

**地面沉降（Land subsidence）**：地下水超采与建筑荷载导致的地表缓慢下沉，是雅加达沿海洪涝区别于一般暴雨内涝的结构性成因，单靠排水工程无法逆转。

**气候适应融资（Climate adaptation finance）**：用于降低气候脆弱性的公共与多边资金，通常经国家指定主管机构与认证实体审批，偏好有造价、可审计、可验收的工程项目。

**努山塔拉（Nusantara）**：印尼规划中的新首都，位于加里曼丹，承载疏解雅加达负荷与区域开发的双重目标，也使旧都的适应投入面临叙事与财政上的挤压。

# Adaptation Money Flows to What Can Be Priced: The Valuation Blind Spot Behind Indonesia's Urban Flooding

Jakarta's floods are usually treated as seasonal news, yet they compound two slow movements: the ground is sinking and the sea is rising. Drains, pumps and river works keep being added, and tides still push into low-lying districts during the rains. By 2026, as the capital relocation to Nusantara advances, one question has grown sharper: when the national narrative turns to a new capital, who protects the city left behind, and by what measure?

Much is expected of climate adaptation finance, but by public reporting and much commentary its actual flow has long favoured what can be priced — concrete with a bill of quantities, a tender, a deliverable, a line in a project appraisal. The people most in need of protection are often the hardest to value: informal settlements, tenants, households without title, families living on daily wages. Hence the central judgement here: adaptation money flows to projects that can be priced, not to the people who most need protecting and are hardest to value. 

## A sinking city and a rising waterline

Jakarta has long depended on groundwater; extraction and the load of high-rise buildings keep parts of the city subsiding, while sea level rises and tides back up the rivers. What locals call tidal flooding (rob) is not confined to heavy rain — a high tide on a clear day can overtop a bank. Generally such compound risk resists attribution to a single cause, yet administration habitually splits it among single agencies.

Drainage and river works are therefore the first response: dredging channels, raising banks, adding pumps, clearing structures that encroach on waterways. These measures do work, but they also frame the problem as too much water rather than sinking ground and where people live. By public accounts, successive clean-ups have accompanied the removal of riverside settlements, with engineering progress and social cost booked separately: the former has a completion certificate, the latter tends to surface only in later disputes.

## After the capital moves, the city left behind

Relocating the capital to Nusantara is a long institutional project, meant to ease Jakarta's population and administrative load and to open up Kalimantan. But once national attention and the fiscal narrative shift to a new capital, the old city's adaptation needs do not disappear: it remains the centre of finance, employment and ports, and large numbers of people still live with the tide each day. The title of capital can be moved; the people exposed to the risk cannot.

A mismatch follows. A new capital can be planned from scratch to current standards; the old one must retrofit existing pipes, titles and settlements. Politically, relocation is easily told as the solution, while in engineering terms it simply leaves part of the risk where it was. Much commentary holds that the credibility of an adaptation promise is best judged by the unglamorous daily maintenance that carries no symbolic weight and seldom reaches promotional material — dredging silt, the electricity for pumps, rubbish at the outfalls, year after year.

## River works and projects with a price tag

The preference for engineering has a technical rationale. Drains, pumps, sea walls and reservoirs come with clear bills of quantities, costs, schedules and acceptance standards; they fit cost-benefit analysis and the approvals, procurement and audit of multilateral development banks and climate funds. Community early warning, tenant compensation and livelihood recovery, by contrast, resist a common metric.

"Priceable" thus becomes an invisible sieve. The same sum spent on a stretch of embankment instantly creates a verifiable asset, while sparing low-lying households repeated displacement can only be estimated indirectly as losses avoided. By public reporting, the hardest fields in a project document are seldom engineering parameters but the social baseline of beneficiaries. Engineering is not the mistake; the mistake is letting a valuation system that recognises only concrete equate protection with construction.

## The threshold: who can actually access adaptation finance

Accessing international adaptation finance is itself a threshold. A project must pass through a national designated authority and accredited entities, with feasibility studies, environmental and social assessment, procurement rules, grant or repayment arrangements, and a demonstrated climate rationale. For a central ministry or large contractor this is process; for a local community or small non-governmental organisation it is often a set of consulting, translation and compliance costs it cannot afford. 

What matters more is the distribution of capacity. The most vulnerable cities tend to have the tightest budgets, thinnest data and least complete land records — and are precisely the least able to phrase their needs in a compliant proposal. Generally, funds reach the end that can apply first and then filter down; during that filtering, the hardest-to-value people stay at the back. This is not the bias of any one institution but a systemic leak produced by valuation and application rules together: the more regularly the system runs, the more easily it excludes lives that are not regular.

## Insurance, land tenure and the people hardest to value

Insurance is often proposed as a complement, but it requires a definable subject, a verifiable address and an insurable interest. In informal settlements along riverbanks and low ground, tenure is unclear, leases oral and houses without formal numbering; after a flood they are hard either to insure or to claim against, and relocation compensation usually presupposes proof of title. Those most exposed to water are thus least legible to financial tools. Where risk is most concentrated, insurability is thinnest.

Land tenure also produces disputes over relocation. Strengthening an embankment or yielding a river corridor requires clearing space, and residents fear that clearance "in the name of safety" will push their livelihoods further away; without credible compensation and return arrangements, works deepen distrust. Much commentary holds that vulnerability resists monetisation not because it is small but because it sits in households, neighbourhoods and informal earnings — with no invoice, no ledger, no market quote. 

## Turning the valuation lens toward people

Correcting the bias need not reject engineering; it means widening what counts as adaptation. Small community grants, participatory risk mapping, eligibility of tenants and untitled households for compensation, and routine funding for warnings and evacuation can all carry clear performance indicators. Fold avoided displacement, reduced days of lost work and speed of livelihood recovery into cost-benefit, and protection no longer has to be proved only in concrete. 

More fundamentally, change the governance reading: look less at kilometres begun and more at households repeatedly flooded, insurance coverage, return rates and community acceptance of a scheme. To return to the opening point, adaptation money flowing to priceable projects is an intelligible institutional preference, not a conspiracy; but getting it to the people who most need protection requires admitting that some value should not, and cannot, be reckoned only by construction cost. That holds for the Jakarta left behind, and for other coastal cities across ASEAN.
## Further reading

**Land subsidence**: The slow sinking of the surface caused by groundwater over-extraction and building loads; a structural cause of Jakarta's coastal flooding that distinguishes it from ordinary rainfall waterlogging, and one that drainage works alone cannot reverse.

**Climate adaptation finance**: Public and multilateral funding for reducing climate vulnerability, usually channelled through a national designated authority and accredited entities, with a preference for costed, auditable projects with verifiable deliverables.

**Nusantara**: Indonesia's planned new capital in Kalimantan, intended both to relieve Jakarta's load and to open up regional development, and a project that also squeezes the narrative and fiscal space available for adaptation in the old capital.
