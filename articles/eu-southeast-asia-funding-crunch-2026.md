# 欧盟的东南亚攻势撞上资金墙：当布鲁塞尔收紧钱袋，中国正拉开身位

据《南华早报》报道，一场资金危机正威胁到欧盟扩大在东南亚影响力的雄心。布鲁塞尔陷入预算僵局，可能拖慢这片快速增长地区的基建投资；而资金缺口一旦扩大，受冲击的将是东盟（ASEAN）的绿色转型与互联互通目标——偏偏此时，北京正借机巩固其经济主导地位。分析人士普遍认为，欧盟将把国内开支压力置于海外承诺之上；欧洲或许还能靠「更高质量、更可持续」的投资立足，却很难在资金体量上与中国正面对撞。

## 钱袋收紧：净出资国的「断供」冲动

矛盾的根源，是欧盟 2028 至 2034 年多年期财政框架（MFF）谈不拢。去年 7 月，欧盟委员会提出总额接近 2 万亿欧元（约 15.4 万亿元人民币）的预算案，其中「全球欧洲」（Global Europe）外部行动经费高达 2000 亿欧元（约 1.54 万亿元人民币），远高于目前的 795 亿欧元（约 6120 亿元人民币）。这一方案立刻遭到「节俭阵营」——德国、奥地利、丹麦、荷兰、芬兰、瑞典——的强烈抵制，它们要求砍掉数千亿欧元才肯放行（预算须全体成员国一致同意）。

为打破僵局，欧盟理事会轮值主席国爱尔兰提出折中：把「全球欧洲」压至 1489 亿欧元（约 1.15 万亿元人民币），较原案砍约 25%。欧洲议会激烈反弹，但分析人士普遍怀疑议员们守不住这道防线——「出资国施加的压力太强，议会翻盘的概率极低」。

## 缺口落在哪里：东盟的绿色转型与互联互通

首当其冲的，是欧盟—东盟（EU–ASEAN）「全球门户」（Global Gateway）战略。该战略 2022 年启动，目标是以 100 亿欧元（约 770 亿元人民币）的公共资金，在 2027 年前支撑东盟的基础设施建设。资金一旦收紧，受阻的正是东盟的绿色转型与区域互联互通——而这恰恰是与北京竞争经济影响力的关键场域。对欧盟而言，东盟是拥有 6.8 亿人口、庞大中产与活跃制造、服务、科技经济的巨型市场。

## 中国没有闲着：领先正在扩大

北京的经济影响力并未停步。从「一带一路」（Belt and Road）到清洁能源制造——太阳能板、电池、电动车——再到深度嵌入区域供应链，中国以国家背书的速度与资本持续扩张。当布鲁塞尔还在围绕可持续采购的细则争论时，中国国有企业已在浇筑混凝土。东盟国家更在意眼前的经济存续、就业与实体联通，而非谁的标准更高；在「慢而苛」与「快而实」之间，多数政府会用脚投票。

## 两套蓝图，两种节奏：全球门户 vs 一带一路

欧盟的「全球门户」全球盘子在 3000 亿欧元（约 2.3 万亿元人民币）量级，被定位为对标「一带一路」、更透明高标准的替代方案。但欧洲的打法高度依赖公共赠款、私营资本动员与严格合规——环境、劳工、治理条条框框一道不少。结果是，东盟政府想升级一条交通走廊或建一座光伏电站，往往要先闯过没完没了的环评、磋商与欧式规范。中国则给出速度与确定性：由国家背书的资本即时到位，且极少干预当地政治。蓝图很美，节奏却慢了半拍。

## 布鲁塞尔的困局：战略雄心撞上预算现实

更深的约束来自欧洲自身。五个成员国的债务已超过国内生产总值（GDP）的 100%，国际货币基金组织（IMF）警告，若无财政纪律，欧洲公债未来 15 年可能翻倍。在美、中之间左右为难、国内负债高企的背景下，「国内议题优先」成为主调。一位驻布鲁塞尔的中国问题分析师说得很直白：若预算非砍不可，被动刀的很可能是对外行动，「因为对各国政府而言，它本就不是最高优先级」。

## 东南亚怎么看：欢迎欧盟，但不愿只押一边

对东盟来说，欧盟是值得欢迎的「第三选项」——战略自主、标准高、可持续，可对冲单一依赖。学者米什拉（Rahul Mishra）指出，东盟与欧盟在利益、诉求与关切上高度契合。欧洲也未必非要比拼资金体量，用「高质量投资」立身本是一条差异化路线。问题在于，耐心有限：当欧洲的资金以层层文书的形式抵达，而中国的基建以钢筋水泥的形式落地，选择往往是务实而非意识形态的。

## 区域镜鉴：援助退潮时的规则之争

这件事的启示在于：地缘影响力很少由漂亮的外交公报决定，而由谁修通了贸易与能源的实体通道决定。若布鲁塞尔无法用可靠、快速的资金兑现其战略修辞，便可能在世界最具活力的增长区沦为经济配角。要止住与中国的差距扩大，欧盟必须改革其金融架构——简化审批、尊重当地政治现实、真正下场承担风险，而非指望发展中国家在「官僚纠缠」与「已建成公路」之间选前者。否则，资金墙只会越垒越高。

## 延伸阅读

**全球门户（Global Gateway）**：欧盟 2021 年推出、规模约 3000 亿欧元的全球基建战略，以「透明、高标准」对标中国「一带一路」，但在东南亚落地常受资金与审批拖累。

**去风险（De-risking）**：欧盟对华政策基调，将中国同时视为伙伴、竞争者与安全挑战，主张「去风险而非脱钩」，但在预算吃紧下对外行动资源承压。

**净出资国（Net payer）**：欧盟中出资额高于所获拨款的成员国（如德、荷、北欧诸国），在预算谈判中主导紧缩，直接决定「全球欧洲」等对外经费的生死。

# The EU's Southeast Asia Push Hits a Funding Crunch as China Widens Its Lead

A funding crunch is threatening to stifle the European Union's ambition to expand its influence in Southeast Asia. According to the South China Morning Post, Brussels is tangled in a budget deadlock that could slow infrastructure investment across one of the world's fastest-growing regions. If the gap widens, the casualty will be ASEAN's green transition and connectivity goals — precisely the arena where Beijing is consolidating its economic clout. Analysts broadly expect the EU to put domestic spending pressure above overseas commitments; Europe may still stand on "higher-quality, sustainable" investment, but it cannot match China's financial firepower head-on.

## The tightening purse: the net payers' urge to cut

The root problem is the impasse over the EU's 2028–2034 Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF). Last July the European Commission proposed a budget of nearly €2 trillion (~US$2.25 trillion), including €200 billion (~US$220 billion) for "Global Europe," its external-action instrument — up from the current €79.5 billion (~US$85 billion). The plan met swift resistance from the "frugal" bloc — Germany, Austria, Denmark, the Netherlands, Finland and Sweden — which demanded cuts of several hundred billion euros before granting the unanimous approval the budget requires.

To break the stalemate, the Irish presidency of the Council of the EU proposed a compromise trimming Global Europe to €148.9 billion (~US$160 billion), roughly a 25% cut from the original. The European Parliament pushed back hard, but analysts doubt lawmakers can hold the line. "The pressure from the countries that pay for the EU budget is so strong that the probability of Parliament successfully lifting Global Europe again is very low," one London School of Economics scholar noted.

## Where the gap lands: ASEAN's green transition and connectivity

First in the line of fire is the EU–ASEAN Global Gateway strategy. Launched in 2022, it aims to bolster the region's infrastructure with €10 billion (~US$11.2 billion) in public funding through 2027. A funding squeeze directly hinders ASEAN's green transition and regional connectivity — exactly the frontier where Brussels competes with Beijing for economic influence. For the EU, ASEAN is a massive market of more than 680 million people with a huge middle class and a dynamic manufacturing, services and technology economy.

## Beijing is not idle: the lead is widening

China's economic clout has not paused. From the Belt and Road Initiative to clean-energy manufacturing — solar panels, batteries, electric vehicles — to deep integration into regional supply chains, Beijing keeps expanding with state-backed speed and capital. While Brussels debates the fine print of sustainable procurement, Chinese state-owned enterprises are pouring concrete. Southeast Asian governments care more about immediate economic survival, jobs and physical connectivity than about whose standards are higher; between "slow but rigorous" and "fast but real," most vote with their feet.

## Two blueprints, two tempos: Global Gateway versus the Belt and Road

The EU's Global Gateway, a roughly €300 billion (~US$320 billion) global infrastructure strategy, is positioned as a transparent, high-standard alternative to the Belt and Road. But Europe's playbook leans heavily on public grants, private-capital mobilization and strict compliance — environmental, labour and governance boxes all must be ticked. The result: when an ASEAN state wants to upgrade a transit corridor or build a solar farm, it first navigates endless impact assessments, consultations and European norms. China offers speed and certainty: state-backed capital arrives on time, with minimal interference in local politics. The blueprint is beautiful; the tempo is half a beat behind.

## Brussels' dilemma: strategic ambition meets budget reality

Deeper constraints come from Europe itself. Debt across five member states exceeds 100% of GDP, and the IMF warns European public debt could double over the next 15 years without fiscal discipline. Balancing strained ties with Washington and Beijing against mounting domestic liabilities, "domestic issues prevail" has become the dominant refrain. A Brussels-based China analyst put it bluntly: if something must be cut from the MFF, it is likely to be external action, "because it is just not the highest priority for national governments amid the ongoing financial squeeze."

## How Southeast Asia sees it: welcome the EU, but don't bet on one side

For ASEAN, the EU is a welcome "third option" — strategic autonomy, high standards, sustainability, a hedge against single-dependency. Scholar Rahul Mishra notes that ASEAN and the EU share a wide range of interests, aspirations and concerns. Europe need not match China's financial scale; staking out "high-quality investment" is a legitimate differentiation. The problem is patience. When European money arrives as paperwork and Chinese infrastructure arrives as reinforced concrete, the choice tends to be pragmatic rather than ideological.

## Regional mirror: the rules fight as aid recedes

The lesson is that geopolitical influence is rarely decided by polished diplomatic communiqués, but by who builds the physical pathways of trade and energy. If Brussels cannot back its strategic rhetoric with reliable, fast-moving capital, it risks becoming a minor economic player in the world's most dynamic growth region. To stop the gap with China from widening, the EU must overhaul its financial architecture — streamline approvals, accept local political realities, and put real skin in the game, rather than expect developing nations to choose red tape over finished roads. Otherwise, the funding wall only grows taller.

## Further reading

**Global Gateway**: The EU's 2021 global infrastructure strategy, worth roughly €300 billion, pitched as a transparent, high-standard counterweight to China's Belt and Road — yet in Southeast Asia its rollout is often slowed by funding and approval bottlenecks.

**De-risking**: The EU's China-policy framing that treats Beijing simultaneously as partner, competitor and systemic rival, advocating "de-risking, not decoupling" — even as tighter budgets squeeze the resources for external action.

**Net payer**: EU member states whose contributions exceed what they receive (Germany, the Netherlands and the Nordic states among them); they drive austerity in budget talks and effectively decide the fate of external instruments such as Global Europe.
