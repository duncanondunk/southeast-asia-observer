# 印尼2027财年锚定6%增长：十五年来最猛一脚油门，钱从哪来？

9月29日，印尼国会全体会议表决通过2027财年国家预算案，把明年的经济增长目标定在6%。这个数字比本财年的5.4%高出一大截，更是2012年以来的最高水平。总统普拉博沃（Prabowo Subianto）要的是什么，不言自明：把这个东南亚最大经济体彻底加速。但油门踩下去，油表却已经在报警。

## 六年最高：普拉博沃把油门踩到底

6%是什么概念？这是印尼自2012年以后的最高年度增长目标。换言之，过去十几年里，雅加达从未如此明确地要把增速推上一个台阶。普拉博沃上任以来，口号一直是“加快发展”——从免费营养餐计划（Free Nutritious Meals (MBG)），到32个国企牵头的战略基建项目，再到如今写入预算的6%目标，一条主线很清楚：用更快的增长，证明“印尼崛起”不是空话。

据路透社和彭博社报道，这份预算案把国家支出定为4106.3万亿印尼盾（约2900亿新元，约合1.54万亿元人民币），收入则为3435.1万亿印尼盾，经济增长目标6%，整体通胀率2.5%。对一个长期在5%上下徘徊的经济体来说，这等于主动把标杆抬高了一截。

## 财政账本：赤字已逼近红线

雄心越大，账本越紧。预算案把明年的赤字目标定在占国内生产总值（GDP）的2.4%，即671.2万亿印尼盾（约480亿新元，约合3460亿元人民币）；而2026年的预测赤字是占GDP的2.85%。表面看是收窄了，但请注意：印尼法定的赤字上限是3%。2.4%离红线只剩0.6个百分点。

这意味着什么？意味着政府几乎没有多少“加杠杆”的空间去刺激经济。新任财政部长苏瓦哈希说得直白：这份预算要同时满足两个看似矛盾的需要——既要应对全球不确定性，又要在不牺牲财政可持续性的前提下加速发展。换句话说，既要踩油门，又不能让油箱见底。这本身就是一道高难度的财政走钢丝。

## 两个乐观假设正在被现实打脸

预算里藏着两个关键假设，而它们现在就已经站不住脚。

其一是油价。政府预计原油均价为每桶75美元（约540元人民币）。可现实是，受地缘政治冲突加剧影响，布伦特国际基准油价已经突破每桶100美元（约720元人民币）。油价假设的缺口，直接意味着能源补贴和财政收入要重新算账。

其二是汇率。预算设定1万7500印尼盾兑1美元的平均汇率目标，而目前汇率已逼近1万8000印尼盾的心理关口。本币走弱，进口更贵、偿债更吃力，预算的缓冲空间被进一步压缩。

国会预算委员会主席赛义德也坦言，单靠国家预算无法实现6%的增长，政府必须推动高附加值出口、吸引更多投资，并改善贸易治理、加快工业化。话虽在理，可这些恰恰都不是一年预算能立竿见影的事。

## 免费餐与国企：增长的两条腿

那么增长的动力靠什么？从这份预算和普拉博沃的施政脉络看，主要是两条腿。

一条是民生与福利支出，典型就是免费营养餐计划。这个计划在政治上是加分项，在财政上却是持续放血的伤口——每个月都是实打实的支出，且难以在短期内转化为可计量的GDP。

另一条是国企牵头投资。8月底雅加达在东京一口气推介32个国企牵头的战略基建项目，总规模约207.64万亿印尼盾（约合125亿美元，约900亿元人民币）。用成熟资产换日本的技术、资本与长期伙伴，逻辑讲得通；但落地速度、主权平衡与回报周期，都是问号。

两条腿都还不够稳。这也是为什么市场会把这份预算当成一场“信心测试”：雅加达到底能不能在不突破3%赤字上限的前提下，把普拉博沃的雄心落到实处？

## 投资者在问：这次能兑现吗

回到最根本的问题：6%的增长，靠什么来？

印尼的增长长期绑定大宗商品价格——棕榈油、煤炭、镍。全球不确定性升温时，商品周期的冷暖就直接决定增速。预算把油价压在75美元（约540元人民币），等于赌地缘政治会降温；把汇率钉在17500，等于赌资本外流会收敛。两者都偏乐观。

更关键的是制度约束。尽管国会议员在斟酌修订《国家财政法》，但苏瓦哈希明确说，政府会继续在现行法律框架下运作、遵守赤字限制，修法讨论应当“建设性”地进行。这句话的潜台词是：别指望靠改法律来腾挪空间。

说到底，6%是一个政治信号，更是一道经济考题。普拉博沃用预算把调门拉满，剩下的，要交给大宗商品、外资流向和财政纪律来回答。投资者等的，正是这个答案是不是靠得住。

## 延伸阅读

**国内生产总值（GDP）**：一国在一定时期内生产的全部最终商品与服务的市场价值总和，是衡量经济增速的核心指标；印尼法定财政赤字上限为GDP的3%。

**国企（State-Owned Enterprise）**：由政府控股的企业，在印尼承担大量基建与战略投资职能，是普拉博沃加速增长战略的重要抓手。

**免费营养餐计划**：普拉博沃推动的全民福利项目，旨在改善儿童营养，但持续扩大的财政支出也引发对预算可持续性的担忧。

# Indonesia Sets a 6% Growth Target for 2027: The Most Aggressive Accelerator in 15 Years, But Where Will the Money Come From?

On 29 September, Indonesia's parliament passed the 2027 fiscal-year state budget, setting next year's economic growth target at 6%. That is a full step up from this year's 5.4% and the highest annual target since 2012. President Prabowo's intent is unmistakable: take the brakes off Southeast Asia's largest economy. But the harder he presses the accelerator, the louder the fuel-light is already buzzing.

## The most ambitious target in 15 years

What does 6% mean? It is Indonesia's highest annual growth target since 2012 — in other words, for more than a decade Jakarta has never so explicitly tried to push the growth rate onto a higher rung. Since taking office, Prabowo's refrain has been "accelerate development": from the free-meals programme to the 32 state-firm-led strategic infrastructure projects unveiled in Tokyo, down to the 6% target now written into the budget, one thread runs through it all — use faster growth to prove that "Indonesia's rise" is not an empty slogan.

According to Reuters and Bloomberg, the budget sets state expenditure at 4,106.3 trillion rupiah (about S$290 billion, ~US$217 billion) and revenue at 3,435.1 trillion rupiah, with a 6% growth target and 2.5% overall inflation. For an economy that has long hovered around 5%, this is deliberately raising the bar.

## The fiscal math: the deficit is already near the line

The bolder the ambition, the tighter the ledger. The budget sets next year's deficit at 2.4% of GDP — 671.2 trillion rupiah (S$48 billion, ~US$35 billion) — down from a projected 2.85% of GDP in 2026. On the surface that looks like tightening, but note: Indonesia's statutory deficit ceiling is 3%. At 2.4%, only 0.6 percentage points remain.

What does that mean? It means the government has almost no room to lever up and stimulate the economy. New finance minister Suahasil Nazara put it plainly: the budget must serve two seemingly contradictory needs at once — respond to global uncertainty while accelerating development without sacrificing fiscal sustainability. In plain terms: keep the foot on the gas, but don't let the tank run dry. That is a high-wire fiscal act in itself.

## Two optimistic assumptions are being disproved

The budget hides two key assumptions, and both are already shaky.

First, oil. The government assumes an average crude price of US$75 per barrel. The reality: geopolitically driven conflict has pushed the Brent international benchmark above US$100 per barrel. The gap in the oil assumption means energy subsidies and fiscal revenue must be recalculated.

Second, the exchange rate. The budget assumes an average rate of 17,500 rupiah to the US dollar, yet the currency has already approached the 18,000 psychological threshold. A weaker domestic currency makes imports dearer and debt servicing harder, further compressing the budget's buffer.

Said Abdullah, head of the parliament's budget committee, conceded that the state budget alone cannot deliver 6% growth; the government must drive higher-value-added exports, attract more investment, and improve trade governance and industrialisation. Sound advice — but none of those are things a single-year budget can deliver overnight.

## Free meals and state firms: the two engines

So where will the growth come from? Reading the budget and Prabowo's policy trail, the answer is essentially two legs.

One is welfare spending, epitomised by the free nutritious-meals programme. Politically it scores points; fiscally it is a chronic bleed — real expenditure every month that is hard to convert into measurable GDP in the short run.

The other is investment led by state-owned enterprises. At the end of August, Jakarta pitched 32 state-firm-led strategic infrastructure projects in Tokyo, worth about 207.64 trillion rupiah (~US$12.5 billion). The logic of trading mature assets for Japanese technology, capital and long-term partnership holds; but the speed of execution, the balance of sovereignty, and the payback period are all open questions.

Both legs are still unsteady. That is precisely why the market reads this budget as a "confidence test": can Jakarta actually deliver on Prabowo's ambition without breaching the 3% deficit ceiling?

## Investors are asking: can this time be different

Back to the fundamental question: what will 6% growth rest on?

Indonesia's growth has long been tied to commodity prices — palm oil, coal, nickel. When global uncertainty rises, the swing of the commodity cycle decides the pace directly. The budget bets on US$75 oil, in effect wagering that geopolitics will cool; it pegs the exchange rate at 17,500, in effect wagering that capital outflows will subside. Both lean optimistic.

The institutional constraint matters more. Although lawmakers are mulling amendments to the State Finance Law, Suahasil was explicit that the government will keep operating within the current legal framework and respect the deficit limit, and that the debate on revisions should be "constructive." The subtext: don't expect legal amendments to create room to manoeuvre.

At the end of the day, 6% is a political signal and an economic exam paper. Prabowo has maxed out the volume through the budget; what remains will be answered by commodities, capital flows and fiscal discipline. What investors are waiting for is whether that answer holds up.

## Further reading

**Gross Domestic Product (GDP):** The total market value of all final goods and services produced in a country over a period; the core gauge of growth speed. Indonesia's statutory fiscal-deficit ceiling is 3% of GDP.

**State-Owned Enterprise (SOE):** A government-controlled company that carries much of Indonesia's infrastructure and strategic investment — a key instrument in Prabowo's acceleration strategy.

**Free Nutritious Meals programme:** Prabowo's flagship welfare scheme to improve child nutrition; its expanding fiscal cost has raised questions about budget sustainability.
