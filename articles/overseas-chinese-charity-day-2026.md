# 当慈善成为一种习惯：中华慈善日里的华侨华人善行

9月5日既是中国的“中华慈善日”，也是联合国设定的“国际慈善日”。今年中国新闻网（中新社）连线了散居多个大洲的华侨华人，得到的画面平静却有启示：如今侨胞做慈善，已不再是偶一为之的姿态，而是织进了日常的习惯。

## 从侨汇到责任捐赠

在二十世纪的大部分时间里，华侨华人的行善冲动只通过一个朴素渠道表达，那就是侨汇。寄回家的钱盖起了村庄、学校和祠堂。正如马达加斯加华商总会会长蔡国伟所言，那份对家乡的惦念从未褪色。但他与妻子许鸿案近日捐出1000万元人民币，用于福建石狮的公益慈善大楼建设；他紧接着补充说，如今侨胞的慈善已经越出祖籍国，延伸到他们身在的住居国。

这一转向——从“惦念祖（籍）国”到“回馈住在国”——正是新一代华侨华人慈善的主线。参与的方式更多元，涉及的领域也更广：应急救灾、教育助学、长者照护、儿童福利，都已纳入其中。

## 散居善意的版图

中新社汇集的案例横跨三大洲。在博茨瓦纳，华人慈善基金会已运转二十余年，在当地“国际儿童村”等机构设立三处华人慈善关爱中心，修缮社区诊所并捐建候诊室。基金会主席南庚戌说得平实：在住居国做公益，是在一次次善举中与当地民众增进了解。

在纳米比亚北部的奥汉圭纳省，侨商不久前落成了一座城市文化公园，为当地居民提供了难得的公共休闲空间。在西班牙，瑞安同乡会会长潘文尧长期支持海外华文教育，也曾参与在四川成都捐资建立非营利医疗机构。在智利，圣地亚哥的华侨华人妇女联合会定期走访养老院、儿童福利机构，以及当地一个乳腺癌互助组织。

合在一起看，这些不是零散的逸闻，而是一张清晰可见的全球性、草根性的散居善意网络——它的根，与其说扎在外交指令里，不如说扎在移民社区的日常生活之中。

## 新生代的专业化

报道中反复出现的一个音符是代际。潘文尧观察到，许多华侨华人新生代如今把慈善当作专业而非义务：比起传统的“一笔捐了事”，他们更看重可衡量的效率与长期的社会回报。

这是最值得留意的部分。第一代侨胞慈善是个人的、随机的；正在浮现的一代则是制度化的——基金会、职业培训、面向青年创业的帮扶项目，目标都在可持续的影响。以博茨瓦纳基金会为例，其下一阶段将重点放在职业技能培训与青年创业上。换言之，慈善正被折叠进同一种发展逻辑，而这套逻辑早已驱动着华侨华人的商业网络。

## 不止于钱：信任与双向融入

这为何重要？对住居国社会而言，侨胞慈善那种稳定而不张扬的存在，是信任的静默建造者。纳米比亚捐建者曹淑华援引一条传统的商业伦理——义与利须并重——来解释为何在当地经营的企业也应当回馈社会。她说，结果是社区纽带在每一次馈赠中越束越紧。

对侨胞自身而言，慈善实践正在成为双向融入的渠道：它宣示归属，赢得善意，也让社区在本地的制度面前变得可辨识。这比通常笼罩华侨华人报道的投资与侨汇叙事更柔和、更缓慢，也或许更持久。

## 克制的研判

把话说满是一种错误。此处描述的慈善绝大多数是草根的、自愿的；它并非由北京所指派，也不应以任何协调意义上的“软实力工程”去解读。它的意义在别处——在于华侨华人公民社会的缓慢成熟，在于一个正在学习成为利益相关者、而非仅仅是过客的散居群体。

在专为慈善设立的这一天，这些社区给出的教益朴素而真实：善意若重复得足够久，便不再是姿态，而成了习惯。而最终把散居族群织入周遭社会的，是习惯，不是头条。

## 延伸阅读

- **侨乡（Qiaoxiang）**：中国南方侨民祖籍地，历史上靠侨汇与宗亲网络维系与海外社群的联系。
- **离散软实力（Diaspora soft power）**：移民社群借由文化、慈善与日常存在所发挥的非正式影响，而非国家政策的延伸。
- **侨团（Overseas Chinese association）**：侨居地的自愿性社区组织——商会、宗亲与同乡会、妇女联合会等——是散居生活的锚点。

# When Giving Becomes a Habit: Overseas Chinese Philanthropy on China Charity Day

September 5 marks both China's "Charity Day" and the UN's International Day of Charity. This year China News Service reached out to overseas Chinese communities across several continents, and what came back was a quietly revealing portrait of how the diaspora now practices charity — not as a one-off gesture, but as a routine woven into daily life.

## From remittances to responsible giving

For most of the twentieth century, the charitable instinct of the overseas Chinese expressed itself through a single, unglamorous channel: remittances. Money sent home built villages, schools, and clan halls. As Cai Guowei, head of the Madagascar Chinese Chamber of Commerce, put it, that hometown attachment has never faded. But he and his wife recently donated 10 million yuan (about US$1.4 million) to a public-welfare building in their ancestral home of Shishi, in Fujian province — and he is quick to add that the diaspora's charity now reaches beyond the ancestral land to the countries where they live.

That shift — from "thinking of the motherland" to "giving back to the host society" — is the headline story of a new generation of overseas Chinese philanthropy. The forms have multiplied, and so have the fields: disaster relief, education, elder care, children's welfare.

## A map of diaspora goodwill

The examples China News Service gathered span three continents. In Botswana, the Chinese Charity Foundation has run for over two decades, setting up three "care centres" at institutions such as the SOS Children's Village in Gaborone, renovating community clinics, and building waiting rooms. Its chair, Nan Gengxu, frames the work plainly: public welfare in the host country is how Chinese residents come to know their neighbours.

In northern Namibia's Ohangwena region, overseas Chinese merchants recently completed a city cultural park that gives local residents a rare public leisure space. In Spain, the Rui'an association's Pan Wenyao has long supported overseas Chinese education and helped fund a non-profit medical institution in Chengdu. In Chile, the Chinese Women's Federation in Santiago regularly visits care homes, children's welfare institutions, and a local breast-cancer mutual-aid group.

Read together, these are not scattered anecdotes. They sketch a distinctly global, grassroots network of diaspora goodwill — one rooted less in diplomatic instruction than in the daily life of immigrant communities.

## The new generation's professionalism

A recurring note in the reporting is generational. Pan Wenyao observes that many young overseas Chinese now treat charity as a profession rather than a duty: they favour measurable efficiency and long-term social return over the traditional one-time donation.

This is the part worth watching. The first wave of diaspora charity was personal and ad hoc. The emerging wave is institutional — foundations, training programmes, youth entrepreneurship projects aimed at durable impact. Botswana's foundation, for instance, says its next phase will emphasise vocational training and youth startups. Charity, in other words, is being folded into the same developmental logic that already drives overseas Chinese business networks.

## Beyond money: trust and two-way integration

Why does this matter? For host societies, the steady, unglamorous presence of diaspora charity is a quiet builder of trust. Cao Shuhua, the Namibia donor, invokes a classical commercial ethic — that righteousness and profit must be weighed together — to explain why a business operating locally should also give back. The result, she says, is a community tie that grows tighter with every gift.

For the diaspora itself, charitable practice is becoming a channel of two-way integration: it signals belonging, earns goodwill, and renders the community legible to local institutions. That is softer, slower, and arguably more durable than the investment-and-remittance narrative that usually dominates coverage of overseas Chinese.

## A restrained reading

It would be a mistake to overstate this. The charity described here is overwhelmingly grassroots and voluntary; it is not directed from Beijing, and it should not be read as a soft-power operation in any coordinated sense. Its significance lies elsewhere — in the slow maturation of overseas Chinese civil society, and in a diaspora that is learning to be a stakeholder, not just a sojourner, in the places it calls home.

On a single day set aside for charity, the lesson from these communities is modest but real: goodwill, repeated often enough, stops being a gesture and becomes a habit. And habits, not headlines, are what ultimately knit a diaspora into the societies around it.

## Further reading

- **Qiaoxiang (侨乡)**: The emigrant hometowns of southern China whose ties to overseas communities were historically sustained by remittances and clan networks.
- **Diaspora soft power (离散软实力)**: The informal influence a migrant community exerts through culture, charity, and everyday presence rather than state policy.
- **Overseas Chinese associations (侨团)**: Voluntary community organisations — chambers of commerce, surname and hometown clubs, women's federations — that anchor diaspora life abroad.
