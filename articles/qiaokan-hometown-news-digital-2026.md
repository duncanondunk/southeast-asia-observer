# 侨刊乡讯的数字化转型：一份刊物与它正在消散的「共同地方感」

在侨乡的乡镇档案室里，侨刊乡讯是最不起眼的一类出版物：薄薄一册，内页是宗祠重修与寻人启事。它不进报摊，而是按名单一期期寄往海外，寄到马来西亚的会馆、新加坡的宗亲会、欧美的同乡会。长期以来，它被视作侨乡与海外社群之间一条安静的信息纽带。到2026年，讨论已从「要不要继续办」变成「还能以什么形态存在」。

数字化常被说成技术升级：把纸版搬上网、开个公众号、建几个群，纽带似乎自然延续。但据公开报道与多方观察，被动摇的不是印刷与邮资，而是刊物赖以成立的「共同地方感」——散居各地的人默认彼此关心同一个镇、同一条河、同一座祠堂，愿意在同一份版面上被同时告知。社交平台把这份关切切成无数个群，却不再共享同一张版面。侨刊的危机，与其说是读者流失，不如说是读者仍在、作为公共空间的「地方」散了。

## 一份刊物如何把侨乡寄到海外

侨刊乡讯（qiaokan）从来不止于新闻报道，更像一份定期更新的地方公报：谁家子女升学、哪条村道修通、祠堂祭祖的日期、捐款的去向、寻亲与遗产启事，都挤在同一册里。单条看都琐碎，合起来却构成一个可辨认的「地方」——一个即便数十年未归、仍被持续更新的原乡。

关键在于「被同时告知」。刊物按期出版、按名单寄送，所有收件人拿到同一版本、同一组事实、同一套措辞；它不按个人推送，也不因亲疏增删，因而天然带有公共性。地方侨务部门、侨联与编委会长期承担组稿与寄送，刊物于是在事实上成为准制度化的联络：把分散的侨胞定期拉回同一叙事平面。它的价值不能只按发行量衡量，因为它定义了「谁算自己人」「哪些事值得被记录」。

## 纸张、邮资与逐渐老去的读者

最先被看见的是成本。印刷、装订、国际邮资、逐一核对名单，都是刚性支出；编委会多由退休教师、老侨务工作者与乡贤义务支撑，经费通常来自地方专项或乡亲捐款。据公开报道，不少侨刊长期在「少印几期」与「少寄几地」之间取舍。

读者结构同样在变。第一、二代侨胞往往仍认这份刊物，年轻一辈的成长与教育重心已落在居住国，名单多年未更新，地址失效、刊物退回渐成常态。这容易被误读成「读者老去了」，仿佛搬到手机上年轻人就会回来。但把问题归为读者老化，恰好掩盖了更要紧的一层：需求并未消失，被替换的是「一起读」，而不是「想知道」。

## 群消息取代了定期刊物

微信群、公众号与短视频确实补上了纸质刊物做不到的部分：即时、免费、可语音、可转发。据公开报道，许多编者也同时经营若干群，刊物与群早已并行两套节奏。

变化发生在结构里。从「公共版面」退到「群内通知」，信息按亲缘、地缘、辈分、行业被切成无数小圈子；同一件事在不同群有不同版本、不同详略，甚至不同解释。过去「全体同时收到同一页」的事实平面被拆成互不照面的碎片；群消息滚动即逝、无编审、无合订本，一句更正往往追不上最初的转发。当“公共”二字被悄悄抽走，联络更快更密，记忆反而更薄。

## 方言、代际与读不下去的乡音

侨刊的文字里藏着地方质地：宗族称谓、地名俗写、方言词、只有本乡人懂的俗称。闽南语、粤语、潮汕话、客家话的书面化本就勉强，早年编者常以借字与注音勉强落纸，读者靠共同经验补全。这些不规整之处，正是刊物能被「自己人」辨认的原因，却也是它最难被标准化、最难被机器检索的部分。

代际差异在阅读端被放大。年轻一代多以居住国语言与普通话为主，方言听得懂、读整册却吃力；语音消息反而绕过文字，把乡音留在声音里而非版面上。于是最地道的表达越来越依赖即时口语，最需长期保存的文字却越来越通用、可译。加注与双语对照固然有帮助，但翻译本身就是筛选：译得越通顺，越要把不可译的称谓抹平。方言不是包装，而是「共同地方感」的载体；数字化要处理的，是让语言能力不同的后代仍落在同一份可共享的记录上。

## 档案化：把散落的消息重新收拢

侨批档案（qiaopi）陆续列入世界记忆名录，提供了一个先例：看似零散的民间文书，完全可能被制度性地收拢、编目并长期保存。侨刊性质相近，它不是单条新闻，而是地方社会连续数十年的自我记录——谁捐了款、哪年修了路、哪一房回了乡，串起来就是一部可查的社群史。过刊合订本、邮寄名单、编委信函，甚至更正启事，都是材料。

地方档案馆、图书馆与高校近年在扫描、元数据、全文检索上已有积累，侨务部门也适合牵头标准与经费。但难点同样清楚：版权与个人信息如何处理、谁裁定收录范围、数字化是否等于可检索与可长期迁移。尤其要分清：把群消息批量导出并不等于档案化，群里的碎片带着临时语境与未核实表述，直接归档只会把噪声固化。档案化的目标，是重新做出一处所有人都能回看的公共版面。

## 地方侨务部门该扮演什么角色

角色该从「办刊」转向「搭台」。与其亲自做一份越来越像宣传册的公众号，侨务部门更可资助过刊数字化、牵头元数据与开放接口、撮合会馆与馆藏机构、把散在各地的合订本集中编目。这些是单个群、单个编委会做不了，却决定纽带能否延续的基础工作。

边界尤其需要克制。公共性来自被共同承认，而非被统一发布；一旦刊物被读成单向宣传栏，读者与编委之间脆弱的信任会流失得比邮资上涨更快。数字化的目标既不是复活纸质刊物，也不是追平群消息的速度，而是回答一个更朴素的问题：当人们已被分进无数个群，还有没有一处版面，让不同代际、不同方言、不同居住国的人承认彼此在看同一份关于同一地方的记录。侨刊真正的资产从来不是纸，而是这份被共同承认的注意力。
## 延伸阅读

**侨刊乡讯（Hometown newsletter）**：由侨乡地方定期编印、按名单寄送海外侨胞的民间刊物，兼有地方公报、宗族联络与寻人启事功能，是观察侨务政策基层实践的常见材料。

**侨批（Qiaopi letters）**：近代海外华侨寄回家乡、附汇款的信函合称，因兼具金融与家族史料价值被列入世界记忆名录，其整理经验常被借用于侨刊档案化。

**侨乡（Qiaoxiang）**：华侨华人主要原籍地，侨刊、侨批、会馆与侨务部门在此叠加，构成海外社群与故土之间持续的制度性联系。

# The Digital Crossroads of Qiaokan: A Shared Sense of Place Cut into Countless Group Chats

In the reading rooms of China's emigrant hometowns, the qiaokan, or hometown newsletter, is the least conspicuous publication on the shelf: a slim volume of notices about ancestral halls, paved lanes and appeals to trace relatives. It never reaches a newsstand. It is mailed, issue by issue, against a standing list — to clan associations in Malaysia, lineage societies in Singapore, hometown clubs in the West. For decades it has been a quiet information bond between the qiaoxiang and communities overseas. By 2026 the question has shifted from whether to keep publishing to what form it can take.

Digitisation is usually presented as a technical upgrade: move print online, open a public account, set up a few chat groups, and the bond carries on. Yet, as public reporting and much commentary suggest, what has been shaken is not printing or postage but the shared sense of place the newsletter rested on — the assumption that people scattered across continents still care about the same town, the same river, the same hall, and are willing to be told about them together on one page. Social platforms have cut that attention into countless groups, none sharing a page. The crisis is less a loss of readers than a loss of "place" as common space: the readers remain, but the public surface they shared has gone.

## How a newsletter mailed the hometown overseas

The qiaokan was never merely a news sheet. It worked as a periodically updated local gazette — school places, paved roads, rite dates, donations, notices tracing kin — all crowded into one volume. Each item was trivial alone; together they made a recognisable place, an original home still being updated after decades away.

The mechanism of being told together matters. An issue went out on schedule to a fixed list, and every recipient held the same edition, facts and wording; it pushed nothing at individuals and varied nothing by closeness, so it carried a built-in publicity. Local diaspora-affairs offices, returned-emigrant federations and volunteer boards long handled copy and mailing, making the newsletter a quasi-institutional contact that drew dispersed emigrants back onto one narrative plane. Its value cannot be measured by circulation, for it defined who counted as one of us and which events deserved recording.

## Paper, postage and a readership growing old

The difficulty visible first is cost. Printing, binding, international postage and checking a list name by name are rigid expenses; boards are mostly sustained by retired teachers, veteran diaspora-affairs workers and willing elders, with funds from earmarked local budgets or donations.

The readership is changing too. First- and second-generation emigrants often still claim the publication, while the young have been educated and settled entirely in the country of residence; lists go years without updating and copies are returned as a matter of routine. This is readily misread as readers having aged, as if a phone would bring the young back. Attributing the problem to age conceals the more important layer: demand has not vanished, and what has been replaced is reading together, not the wish to know.

## Group messages replace the periodical

Chat groups, public accounts and short videos genuinely supply what print could not: immediacy, zero cost, voice notes, effortless forwarding. By public reporting, many editors run several groups alongside the newsletter, so the two have long been parallel tempos.

The change is structural. Retreating from a public page to notices inside a group, information is cut into small circles along kinship, native place, generation and trade: the same event takes different versions and explanations in different groups. The old factual plane — all recipients receiving one page together — is broken into fragments that never meet. Messages scroll away, resist search, have no editor or bound volume, and a correction seldom catches the first forward. Once "public" is quietly withdrawn, contact is faster and denser, memory thinner.

## Dialect, generation and a mother tongue no longer read

The texture of the place is embedded in the writing: lineage terms, vernacular spellings of place names, dialect words, nicknames only natives know. Written Hokkien, Cantonese, Teochew and Hakka were always makeshift; early editors forced them onto paper with borrowed characters and glosses, and readers filled the gaps from shared experience. Those irregularities let "one of us" recognise the publication — and make it hardest to standardise or index by machine.

The gap widens at the reading end. The young operate in the language of residence and standard Chinese; they may follow a dialect by ear but reading a whole issue is harder. Voice notes bypass writing, keeping the mother tongue in sound rather than on the page, so the most authentic expression leans on instant speech while the text most needing preservation becomes generic and translatable. Glosses and bilingual layouts help, but translation is a filter: the smoother the rendering, the more it flattens untranslatable kin terms. Dialect is not packaging but the carrier of a shared sense of place; digitisation must let descendants of different linguistic ability still land on one shared record.

## Archiving: gathering scattered messages back in

Qiaopi letters — remittance correspondence from overseas Chinese — being inscribed on the Memory of the World register offers a precedent: stray private documents can be gathered, catalogued and preserved institutionally. The qiaokan is similar: not a single news item but a community's self-record over decades — who donated, which year a road was built, which lineage branch returned — a searchable social history when strung together. Bound back issues, mailing lists, editors' letters, even corrections are material.

Local archives, libraries and universities have accumulated practice in scanning and metadata, and diaspora-affairs offices can coordinate standards and funding. The difficulties are plain: copyright and personal information, who decides inclusion, whether digitisation means searchable and migratable over time. One distinction matters: exporting group messages in bulk is not archiving, for chat fragments carry temporary context and unverified claims, and filing them fixes the noise. Archiving should make again a public page anyone can return to.

## What local diaspora-affairs offices should do

The role should shift from running a publication to building the platform. Rather than producing an ever more brochure-like public account, offices can fund digitisation of back issues, lead on metadata and open interfaces, bring clan and hometown associations together with holding institutions, and centralise the cataloguing of volumes scattered across countries. No single group or board can manage this, yet it decides whether the bond survives.

Restraint at the boundary matters. Publicity comes from being jointly acknowledged, not issued uniformly; once a newsletter reads as a one-way bulletin board, trust drains faster than postage rises. The goal is neither to resurrect print nor to match the speed of a chat, but to answer a plainer question: once people are sorted into countless groups, is there still a page on which different generations, dialects and countries of residence acknowledge one record of one place? The real asset was never paper but this mutually acknowledged attention.

**Qiaopi letters**: Remittance correspondence sent home by emigrants in the modern era, inscribed on the Memory of the World register for its combined financial and family-history value; its cataloguing is often borrowed for qiaokan archiving.

**Qiaoxiang**: The home counties of overseas Chinese, where newsletters, remittance letters, clan associations and diaspora-affairs offices overlap to sustain an institutional link between communities abroad and their native place.
