# 电动车热潮的另一面：中国车企"自带供应链"，东南亚零部件厂被挤到路边

据《南华早报》11月2日报道，东南亚电动汽车销量正一路狂飙，但站在聚光灯之外的，是印尼、泰国和马来西亚成千上万家中小零部件厂。它们正被两股力量同时挤压：一边是燃油车（内燃机，ICE）零件需求持续下滑；另一边，中国车企带着自己的供应商网络长驱直入，本地厂根本挤不进电动车的供应链（supply chain）。一句话——电动化来了，但东南亚的"配套厂"却被留在了路边。

## 一道被忽略的裂缝：销量狂飙，本土厂却没分到羹

东南亚车市近年最热闹的关键词是"电动化"。中国品牌凭借价格与速度，迅速把电动车（EV）铺进泰国、印尼和马来西亚的展厅。可是，销量红火不等于本地产业得益。据《南华早报》引述雅加达经济与法律研究中心（CELIOS）执行主任比马（Bhima Yudhistira Adhinegara）的话，中国电动车投资给既有本地零部件企业带来的生意"少得可怜"。他的判断很直白：日本燃油车与中国的电动车，压根不是同一套供应链。

## 数字说话：本地含量从七成掉到两三成

最能说明问题的是"本地含量"（local-content）这条硬指标。据马来西亚媒体今年3月报道，本国组装的电动车，其本地含量仅为20%至30%；而外资品牌的燃油车，本地含量最高可达70%。换句话说，一辆中国品牌电动车在东南亚下线，约七成的价值仍留在进口零件和原有的中国供应商手里。更麻烦的是，内燃机车的零件需求还在往下掉——中小厂两头落空。

## 比亚迪的"马来西亚折返"：建厂不成，改代工

最具体的案例落在马来西亚。比亚迪（BYD）原本要在霹雳州丹绒马林（Tanjung Malim）自建整车厂，却因与马来西亚投资、贸易及工业部（MITI）在出口比例和定价条件上谈不拢而告吹；随后在10月15日改弦易辙，转而与本地代工厂Inokom合作，在吉打州居林（Kulim, Kedah）的既有厂房里做代工组装（CKD）。表面看仍是"本地生产"，但本地附加值依旧有限——这正是前述20%至30%本地含量困境的缩影。

## 日系供应链与中国电动车，根本不是一回事

为什么日本车能带起本地配套，中国电动车却带不动？核心在供应链逻辑不同。日本车企在东南亚经营数十年，早已把成千上万家本地二三级供应商织进网络；中国车企则多沿用国内的成熟供应商，核心零部件要么整机进口，要么由跟随出海的中国供应商提供。泰国大城银行（Krungsri）旗下研究机构在《2026—2028行业展望》报告中直言，本地供应商在成本与专有技术上双双落后，难以进入中国电动车的供应链。

## 混动与售后：中小厂的最后两扇窗

中小厂并非全无出路。分析师指出，混合动力（hybrid，油电并用）和内燃机车的维修保养市场，是眼下最现实的两扇窗。泰国混合动力车的生产，仍能带动传统发动机零件与电气系统零件的需求；而老车续命，也让售后替换件市场有了支撑。问题是，转型的过渡期谁来兜底？比马提醒，印尼的小厂已经因为内需疲弱、油价高企而不得不缩减规模、压低质量甚至重组债务，"政府根本没有给小型内燃机供应商任何过渡激励"。

## 政策能补位吗：本地含量规则与政府之手

把中小厂拉进电动车时代，需要政府这只手。比马呼吁印尼政府用"本地含量规则"（local-content rules）做硬约束——要求电动车的投资与销售必须满足一定比例，强制吸收本地小厂。马来西亚并非没有动作：2025年5月，马来西亚投资发展局（MIDA）曾撮合23家本地零部件供应商、12家技术供应商与奇瑞（Chery）对接，达成4项涵盖零件、技术转移与联合投资的商业协议。但业内人士也提醒，供应商从备产到回本往往要六到十年，政策与投资必须稳定可预期，否则"投资人不敢掏支票簿"。

## 对东南亚工业化的提醒：电动化不等于本地化

把视野拉高一层，这篇文章真正值得警醒的，是东南亚"以市场换技术、以销量促升级"的旧剧本，正在电动车时代失灵。过去靠日系整车厂，东南亚（尤其是泰国这个"东南亚底特律"）长出了完整的零部件生态；如今中国车企带着闭环供应链南下，销量亮眼却未必转化为本地就业与产业能力。对东盟（ASEAN）各国而言，电动车是机会，但"本地含量规则"、技术转移与中小企业扶持，才是把销量变成工业实力的那几道闸门。否则，东南亚很可能只是多了一个热闹的展厅，却错失又一次真正的产业升级。

## 延伸阅读

**本地含量规则（local-content rules）**：要求整车一定比例的价值在本地采购的强制性规定，是东南亚各国把外资造车转化为本地产业能力的核心政策工具。

**代工组装（CKD, completely knocked down）**：以全散件形式进口、在目的地国完成总装的制造模式，本地附加值通常低于整车厂（OEM）自建产线。

**东盟（ASEAN）**：东南亚国家联盟，其成员国的汽车产业政策正从"欢迎外资"转向"要求落地建厂与本地配套"的第二阶段。

# The other side of the EV boom: Chinese carmakers bring their own supply chains, sidelining Southeast Asia's parts makers

(South China Morning Post, 2 November 2026) Southeast Asia's electric-vehicle sales are climbing fast, but offstage are the thousands of small and mid-sized component factories in Indonesia, Thailand and Malaysia. They are being squeezed from both sides: demand for internal-combustion-engine (ICE) parts keeps falling, while Chinese carmakers arrive with their own locked-in supplier networks, leaving local firms unable to break into the EV supply chain. Electrification is here — yet Southeast Asia's supplier base is being left at the kerb.

## A crack overlooked: sales soar, local factories left out

The buzzword in Southeast Asia's auto market is "electrification." Chinese brands, on price and speed, have rapidly filled showrooms across Thailand, Indonesia and Malaysia. But booming sales do not automatically mean local industry benefits. As the South China Morning Post quotes Bhima Yudhistira Adhinegara, executive director of Jakarta's Centre of Economic and Law Studies (CELIOS), Chinese EV investment has generated "very little business" for established local component makers. His blunt verdict: Japanese gasoline cars and Chinese EVs are simply not the same supply chain.

## The numbers: local content drops from 70% to 20–30%

The clearest metric is "local content." Malaysian media reported in March that domestically assembled EVs contain just 20 to 30 per cent local content, against up to 70 per cent for foreign-branded ICE cars. In other words, when a Chinese-branded EV rolls off a Southeast Asian line, roughly 70 per cent of its value still sits in imported parts and pre-existing Chinese suppliers. Worse, ICE parts demand is still sliding — smaller firms are caught in the middle.

## BYD's Malaysian U-turn: plant scrapped, CKD instead

The most concrete case is in Malaysia. BYD had planned to build its own assembly plant in Tanjung Malim, Perak, but the plan collapsed over a dispute with the Ministry of Investment, Trade and Industry (MITI) on export quotas and pricing conditions. On 15 October the carmaker pivoted, partnering local contract assembler Inokom to assemble passenger vehicles at an existing facility in Kulim, Kedah. On the surface it is still "local production," yet local value-add stays limited — a textbook illustration of the 20–30 per cent local-content trap.

## Japanese supply chains and Chinese EVs are not the same

Why did Japanese cars build local ecosystems while Chinese EVs do not? The logic differs. Japanese automakers, present for decades, wove thousands of local tier-2 and tier-3 suppliers into their networks; Chinese carmakers mostly reuse mature domestic suppliers, importing major components or sourcing from Chinese suppliers that followed them abroad. Krungsri Research, the research arm of Thailand's Bank of Ayudhya, put it plainly in its 2026–28 industry outlook: local vendors lag on both cost and proprietary technology and struggle to enter Chinese EV supply chains.

## Hybrids and aftersales: the last two windows for smaller firms

Smaller firms are not without options. Analysts point to hybrids (pairing a petrol engine with an electric motor) and the ICE repair-and-maintenance market as the most realistic near-term windows. Hybrid production in Thailand still drives demand for both conventional engine parts and electrical-system components, and owners holding onto older cars sustain the replacement-parts market. The problem is the transition gap. Bhima warns that Indonesian small firms, hit by weak consumer spending and rising fuel costs, are already downsizing, cutting quality or restructuring debt — "there are no transitional incentives from the government for small ICE suppliers."

## Can policy step in? Local-content rules and the state's hand

Pulling smaller firms into the EV era needs the state. Bhima urges Jakarta to use local-content rules as a hard constraint — requiring that a set share of EV investment and sales absorb local small manufacturers. Malaysia is not idle: in May 2025 the Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA) brought 23 local parts suppliers and 12 technology suppliers together with Chery, yielding four commercial agreements on components, technology transfer and joint investment. But industry observers also caution that suppliers need six to 18 months to prepare for full production and roughly a decade to see returns; policy and investment must be stable, or "investors need to see stability … before they take out their chequebooks."

## A warning for Southeast Asia's industrialisation: electrification ≠ localisation

Zoom out, and the real caution here is that Southeast Asia's old playbook — "market for technology, sales for upgrading" — is faltering in the EV age. Decades of Japanese assemblers helped the region, especially Thailand as the "Detroit of Southeast Asia," grow a complete parts ecosystem. Now Chinese carmakers arrive with closed-loop supply chains: impressive sales, but not necessarily local jobs or industrial capacity. For ASEAN states, EVs are an opportunity, yet local-content rules, technology transfer and SME support are the gates that turn sales into industrial strength. Otherwise Southeast Asia may end up with a busy showroom — and miss yet another real upgrade.

## Further reading

**Local-content rules:** Mandatory requirements that a set share of a vehicle's value be sourced domestically — the core policy tool for converting foreign carmaking into local industrial capacity across Southeast Asia.

**CKD (completely knocked down):** A manufacturing mode that imports vehicles as full kits for final assembly in the destination country; local value-add is typically lower than a fully built original-equipment-manufacturer (OEM) plant.

**ASEAN:** The Association of Southeast Asian Nations, whose members' auto policies are shifting from "welcome foreign investment" to a second stage of "build locally and source locally."
