# 日本的冷链棋局：日系初创企业如何悄悄重排东南亚食材供应链

当日本制造从东南亚（Southeast Asia）车间节节退守，日系初创企业却把旗子插进了区域的冰箱——这折射出日本竞争方式的一次微妙转向。

## 命题：把日本式「产地直送」搬进东南亚

11月4日，《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》报道，一批日系背景的初创企业正在马来西亚（Malaysia）和越南（Vietnam）悄悄扩张生鲜食品的冷链分销网络，目标是从农户直接把食材送到餐厅与零售终端。其中的标杆是 Secai Marche——这家2018年成立于东京的公司，运营着一个连接种植者与餐饮零售商的产地直送平台，目前已撮合超过4000种农产品，并建有一座分四个温区的冷库。这座冷库本身就是一种宣言：东南亚缺的，正是这种「不断链的冷链」。

说白了，这门生意的逻辑并不复杂——减少损耗、砍掉中间商、用需求预测把供给与订单对齐，再赚走高端生鲜那块更肥的利润。《日经亚洲》特别点出，这一模式已经引起日本大型物流企业与综合商社的合作兴趣。在一个很大比例的食材仍要靠分散、无温控的湿货市场流通的区域，这套逻辑很难被轻易否定。

## 冷链缺口，恰是日本的看家本领

东南亚的粮食系统，若以现代物流的标准衡量，无疑是欠账累累。农产品要穿行于群岛与半岛之间，动辄千里，可冷库、冷藏车与可靠的最后一程配送却始终零散薄弱。结果便是惊人的食物损耗与剧烈的价格波动——而这恰恰是精密物流运营商声称能解决的问题。

这恰恰是日本企业苦心经营多年的领地。数十年服务于一个对新鲜度、可追溯性与卫生标准极为挑剔的本土市场，让雅玛多（Yamato）、日本通运（Nippon Express）这类企业积累了一套「品质物流」的打法，区域内少有对手能及。当中国资本在东南亚的硬件层面——港口、铁路、工厂——占据主导，日本的相对优势正越来越多地藏身于那层「看不见的架构」：标准、冷气与信任。Secai Marche 的赌注，正是把「日本品质」包装成基础设施来出口，而不只是出口制成品。

## 时机：商社与航运巨头下注

这个时点绝非偶然。Secai Marche 的股东名单，读起来像一份日本机构耐心的点名：早期支持者包括乐天资本、Monex Ventures 与 Spiral Ventures，并获日本经济产业省（METI）资助；2025年10月，它在日本通运控股旗下基金领投的A轮中融资；2026年1月，引入航运巨头日本邮船（MOL）的企业风投 MOL PLUS 作为战略投资人；2026年5月，有上百年历史的农业集团 welzo 入股，并与子公司 JAHT 签署三方联盟。雅玛多控股的企业风投也已入场。

信号再清楚不过：日本物流与农业的锚点企业，正把东南亚从「点」的改良——一座冷库、一条线路——推向「面」的基础设施：一条跨越国境、连海上运输都贯穿温控思想的日本标准冷链。对东京而言，本土市场萎缩使出口通道变得紧迫；对区域而言，正在崛起的中产阶级开始愿意为过去得不到的「可靠」付费。

## 「日本品质」作为软性基础设施

剥开物流术语的外壳，真正被出售的其实是「安心」。吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）或胡志明市一家通过日系冷链采购的餐厅，买的远不止冷藏蔬菜，而是可追溯、食品安全以及与「日本」二字绑定的可靠感。在食品安全事件与供给冲击已成常态的年代，这份安心本身就标着溢价。

这是一种极具日本特色的「经济治国术」。当中国以高铁、港口、产业园等旗舰硬资产投射存在感时，日本倚仗的却是标准、认证与不动声色的运营卓越。冷链扩张正是一场软性基础设施的落子：它把日本规范植入区域日常商业，却无需铺设一公里铁轨。对于一个眼睁睁看着制造业份额在区域内不断流失的强国而言，这与其说是「重返」，不如说是一场体面的退守——退守到成本竞争难以撼动的利基地带。

## 高端细分市场的天花板

再精巧的模型也有天花板。眼下的冷链产地直送，本质上仍是一桩高端生意，客群锁定在吉隆坡、新加坡、胡志明市那批高端餐厅、酒店与富裕城市消费者。利润是实的，体量却薄，客户高度集中于少数富裕飞地。

竞争则极为凶悍。Grab 与 Gojek 早已以惊人规模在区域内运送餐饮与货物；本地湿货市场依旧便宜、熟悉，且深深嵌入日常生活；更大的区域分销商更能以价格碾压一家「品质优先」的新来者。从「服务头部餐厅」扩到「喂饱大众市场」，需要资本、密度与耐心——就连腰包深厚的日本后台也可能被考验。真正的问题是：「日本品质」能否便宜到走入寻常百姓家，还是注定只能停留在它无力重塑的市场表层，做一层精品点缀？

## 这折射出日本东南亚打法的转向

退一步看，这场冷链下注，恰是日本区域重新定位的一则精妙隐喻。当中国电动车侵蚀日系车企在东南亚的基本盘，当日系制造商在消费电子与家电领域节节退让，东京并未试图夺回车间。它选择的是在「精度、标准与信任仍受尊重」的层面加倍下注：食品物流、适老科技、脱碳装备与高端零部件。

这是对冲，而非复兴。区域无疑获得了更优的冷链与更具韧性的食物供给——这是货真价实的公共品。但东南亚经济的战略架构，依旧向「谁建得更大、更便宜、更无处不在」的一方倾斜。日本正为自己在边缘地带谋求一个舒适、高利润的栖位。这，或许已是它现实所能指望的上限。

## 结语

日系冷链初创企业在马来西亚与越南的扩张，很容易被读成一个不起眼的小生意。但它更宜被视作一个信号。当日旧工业优势褪色，其企业正把旗子插进区域的冰箱——出售的不再是货物，而是「到手的一定新鲜、安全、可追查」的那份安静的自信。东南亚得到了更好的食物物流，日本得到了一个成本本身难以撼动的据点。双方各有所得；只是其中一方，正在以规模铸造未来。

**产地直送（Farm-to-table）**：跳过中间批发层级、由平台直接对接农户与餐饮零售端的供应模式；日系初创以数字化与需求预测提高其履约效率。

# Japan's Cold-Chain Replay: How Tokyo-Linked Startups Are Rewiring Southeast Asia's Food Supply

As Japanese manufacturers retreat from Southeast Asia's shop floors, Tokyo-linked startups are planting flags in the region's fridges — a telling shift in how Japan competes.

## The pitch: farm-fresh, Japanese-style

On 4 November, Nikkei Asia reported that a cluster of Japan-linked startups is quietly expanding cold-distribution networks for fresh food across Malaysia and Vietnam, aiming to move produce directly from farms to restaurant and retail buyers. The flagship name is Secai Marche, a Tokyo-founded company (2018) that runs a business-to-business farm-direct platform connecting growers with restaurants and retailers across the region. It already handles more than 4,000 agricultural products and operates a warehouse with four separate temperature zones — the physical proof of a "cold chain" its backers believe Southeast Asia badly lacks.

The pitch is simple and, on paper, attractive: cut the spoilage and the middlemen, use demand forecasting to match supply with orders, and capture the fatter margins that premium fresh produce commands. Nikkei noted that the model is now drawing partnership interest from major logistics operators and Japanese trading houses. For a region where a large share of food still moves through fragmented, temperature-blind wet markets, the logic is hard to dismiss.

## A cold-chain gap Japan knows how to fill

Southeast Asia's food system is, by any measure, under-served by modern logistics. Produce travels long distances across archipelagos and peninsulas, yet cold storage, reefer trucks and reliable last-mile delivery remain patchy. The result is staggering food loss and volatile prices — exactly the inefficiency a precision-logistics operator promises to solve.

This is terrain where Japanese firms have a genuine, hard-won edge. Decades of serving a demanding domestic market — with exacting standards on freshness, traceability and hygiene — gave companies like Yamato and Nippon Express a playbook for "quality logistics" that few regional players can match. Where Chinese capital has dominated Southeast Asia's hardware — ports, rails, factories — Japan's comparative advantage increasingly lies in the invisible layer: the standards, the cold, the trust. Secai Marche's bet is that "Japanese quality" can be packaged and exported as infrastructure, not just as manufactured goods.

## Why now: trading houses and shippers place their bets

The timing is not accidental. Secai Marche's cap table reads like a roll-call of Japanese institutional patience: early backers included Rakuten Capital, Monex Ventures and Spiral Ventures, with a grant from the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. In October 2025 it closed a Series A with Nippon Express Holdings' fund; in January 2026 it took strategic money from MOL PLUS, the corporate venture arm of shipping giant Mitsui O.S.K. Lines; and in May 2026 the century-old agricultural group welzo invested and signed a three-party alliance with its subsidiary JAHT. Yamato Holdings' venture arm has also come on board.

The signal is clear. Japan Inc's logistics and agri anchors are moving from treating Southeast Asia as a "point" improvement — one warehouse, one route — to building "area" infrastructure: a seamless, Japanese-standard cold chain spanning borders, including cross-border sea freight. For Tokyo, a shrinking home market makes export outlets urgent; for the region, a rising middle class is starting to pay for reliability it could not get before.

## "Japanese quality" as soft infrastructure

Strip the logistics jargon away and what is really on sale is assurance. A restaurant in Kuala Lumpur or Ho Chi Minh City that sources through a Japanese-run cold chain is buying more than chilled vegetables; it is buying traceability, food safety and a brand of reliability associated with Japan itself. In an era when food scares and supply shocks are routine, that assurance carries a premium.

This is economic statecraft of a distinctly Japanese kind. Where China projects presence through flagship hard assets — high-speed rail, ports, industrial parks — Japan leans on standards, certification and quiet operational excellence. The cold-chain push is a soft-infrastructure play: it plants Japanese norms into the region's daily commerce without laying a single rail line. For a power watching its manufacturing share erode across the region, the strategy is less a comeback than a graceful retreat into niches where cost competition cannot easily dislodge it.

## The ceiling of a premium niche

For all its elegance, the model has a ceiling. Cold-chain farm-to-table is, at present, a premium proposition aimed at upmarket restaurants, hotels and affluent urban consumers in Kuala Lumpur, Singapore and Ho Chi Minh City. The margins are real but the volumes are thin, and the customers are concentrated in a handful of wealthy enclaves.

The competition is formidable. Grab and Gojek already move food and goods across the region at enormous scale; local wet markets remain cheap, familiar and deeply embedded in daily life; and larger regional distributors can undercut a quality-led newcomer on price. Scaling from "serve the top restaurants" to "feed the mass market" requires capital, density and patience that even deep-pocketed Japanese backers may test. The open question is whether "Japanese quality" can ever be cheap enough to go mainstream — or whether it is destined to remain a boutique layer atop a market it cannot reshape.

## What it reveals about Japan's Southeast Asia playbook

Step back, and the cold-chain bet is a neat metaphor for Japan's broader regional repositioning. As Chinese electric vehicles erode the Japanese carmakers' Southeast Asian stronghold, and as Japanese manufacturers cede ground in consumer electronics and appliances, Tokyo is not trying to win the shop floor back. Instead it is doubling down on the layers where precision, standards and trust still command respect: food logistics, ageing-society technology, decarbonisation kit and high-end components.

This is a hedge, not a resurgence. The region gains undeniably better cold chains and a more resilient food supply — a genuine public good. But the strategic architecture of Southeast Asia's economy still tilts toward whoever builds the larger, cheaper, more ubiquitous system. Japan is securing a comfortable, high-margin perch in the margins. That may be the most it can realistically hope for.

## The bottom line

The expansion of Japan-linked cold-chain startups across Malaysia and Vietnam is easy to read as a small business story. It is better read as a tell. As Japan's old industrial advantages fade, its firms are planting flags in the region's refrigerators — selling not goods but the quiet confidence that what arrives will be fresh, safe and accounted for. Southeast Asia gets better food logistics. Japan gets a foothold that cost alone cannot dislodge. Both sides get something; only one of them is building the future at scale.
