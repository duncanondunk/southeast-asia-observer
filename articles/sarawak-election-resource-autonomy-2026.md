# 砂拉越锁定12月12日州选：一场关于石油主权与自治权的公投

砂拉越第13届州选举定于2026年12月12日投票，提名日定在11月28日，提前投票安排在12月8日。根据选委会公布的数据，82个州选区共有2058781名合格选民，预计选举预算为1.7494亿令吉（约2.9亿元人民币）。表面看，这只是一场地方选举；但据《南华早报》报道，真正的看点不在谁胜谁负，而在于执政的砂拉越政党联盟（GPS）能否借这场选举，向联邦政府索要更大的经济自主权——尤其是油气资源的监管权。一场没有悬念的投票，正在变成对「砂拉越优先」路线的变相公投。

## 选举日程：没有悬念，却意味深长

砂拉越政党联盟（GPS）已宣布将竞逐全部82个议席。这个由多个本土政党组成的联盟在2021年州选中横扫76席，如今优势依旧稳固。反对党方面，民主行动党（DAP）仅计划角逐7个、至多8个选区——这恰恰说明，在GPS压倒性的「造王者」地位面前，反对力量的活动空间已被压缩到城市华人聚居区等少数阵地。

那么，一场几乎注定结果的选举，为何仍值得观察？答案在于叙事。GPS竞逐每一个席位，意在强化「砂拉越优先」的政治口号，凸显自身无需依赖马来半岛伙伴、便能独立主导本州事务的能力。在联邦谈判的前夜，这是一次精心设计的实力展示：无论全国政局如何摆动，GPS都将是联邦治理格局中一个独立而强悍的权力掮客。

## 石油之争：Petros 对阵 Petronas

选战背后，最尖锐的张力来自油气。砂拉越通过全资拥有的砂拉越石油公司（Petros），正试图加强对本州油气储量的监管权，这直接挑战了联邦的马来西亚国家石油公司（Petronas）在管理全国碳氢资源上的垄断地位。说白了，争议焦点就是：究竟由哪一层的机构、哪一家公司，来为砂拉越价值巨大的油气活动定规矩。

数据最能说明分量。砂拉越一州，就占了马来西亚石油与天然气蕴藏量的61%；邻州沙巴占19%，东马两地合计高达80%。这意味着，以砂拉越为核心的这场对峙，牵动的不只是本州，而是整个联邦的能源命脉。砂拉越方面要求，必须明确掌握每日120亿标准立方英尺天然气的来源与交付安排——这不是小数目，而是维系本州发电厂与工业用气的基本盘。

## 自治诉求：1963年协定的旧账

油气之争，根子上是自治权之争。砂拉越依据《1963年马来西亚协定》（MA63）主张特殊地位——这部协定规定了沙巴与砂拉越加入联邦时的安排。砂拉越首席部长阿邦佐哈里在10月31日公开呼吁民众支持加强对战略资源的控制，并以砂拉越接管民都鲁港、投资联昌银行（Affin Bank）为例，说明「把资产握在自己手里」已不只是口号。

但法律路径并不平坦。港口与海港通常属州政府管辖，除非被宣告为联邦管辖；而石油权限则牵涉联邦与州权限清单中的明文条文，二者不可混为一谈。若双方迟迟谈不拢，联邦法院已排定在2027年1月25日至29日开庭审理。对GPS而言，把宪法争议变成选举口号固然能凝聚选票，却也让妥协在政治上变得昂贵——这正是观察家警告的风险。

## 联邦政治的「造王者」：GPS 与安华的微妙平衡

把镜头拉远，砂拉越州选其实是一场联邦政治的微缩剧。GPS在国会中是安华·依布拉欣总理政府的支持力量，是名副其实的「造王者」；而安华领导的希望联盟（PH）近来在州选战场上却接连吃瘪——沙巴、柔佛、森美兰相继失分，马六甲也将在11月14日（早于砂拉越提名日）迎来投票。

这就形成了一组相互掣肘的压力：Petronas为联邦财政贡献收入，而GPS又在国会支撑安华的执政。尤索夫伊萨研究所（ISEAS）的分析便指出，安华面对的是两难——既要维护联邦的财政与宪法权威，又不能轻易得罪这位关键的盟友。于是，这场州选被许多分析人士解读为：不仅是对GPS执政成绩的公投，更是对砂拉越向布城（即联邦政府所在地）争取更多让步能力的考验。

## 华侨华人的算盘：资源政治下的少数族群

任何关于砂拉越的讨论，都绕不开当地的华人族群。华人约占砂拉越总人口的四分之一，是举足轻重的商业力量，也是若干城市选区的关键票仓。民主行动党（DAP）仅争7到8席，折射的正是华人聚居城市里有限的博弈空间——在GPS的本土主义叙事之下，华人政党的角色更像「守成型」而非「进攻型」。

更复杂的是经济层面。资源民族主义与自治议程，对当地华商而言是一把双刃剑：一方面，州政府强化对油气、港口与金融资产的本地控制，可能带来「本地化红利」，让熟悉在地网络的华商受益；另一方面，联邦与州之间的持续博弈，又给能源价格、基建审批乃至中资项目的确定性蒙上变量。事实上，中资在砂拉越的经济足迹早已深厚——从水电枢纽到液化天然气产业链，再到纵贯婆罗洲的泛婆罗洲大道，中国企业的参与让这场「资源控制权」之争，多了几分区域地缘经济的意味。对华人商界而言，如何在联邦与州、本地与外来之间找准位置，是一道现实的算术题。

## 风险与变数：选举口号会否堵死和解窗口

选战升温，最现实的担忧是：竞选语言会不会把Petros与Petronas之间的和解空间压缩殆尽？全球亚洲顾问公司的分析师扎哈鲁丁便警告，当宪法争议沦为选举口号，任何一方的公开让步都将付出高昂的政治代价，司法裁决本身也会变得「政治上危险」。

眼前的任务是赶在联邦法院2027年1月开庭前敲定和解：确保砂拉越供气稳定、厘清两家公司的权责、谈妥财务条款。一个独立技术委员会正依据2025年5月的联合宣言审查双方分歧；双方也在讨论成立合资机构从事天然气勘探与生产，甚至考虑从纳土纳附近供气。若僵局拖延，打击的将是投资意愿、石油计划进度，以及整个区域的能源供给稳定——这无论对GPS、对安华，还是对普通砂拉越人，都不是好消息。

## 结语：一场地方选举，一面联邦制的镜子

砂拉越第13届州选举，表面是地方政治的一次常规轮换，内里却是马来西亚联邦制张力的一面镜子。一个资源富集的东马州属，如何与半岛中心重新谈判权力与财富的分配，这道题目没有标准答案。无论12月12日的结果如何，油气主权与自治权的议题，只会在这片土地上越喊越响。而对于当地华人族群、对于深嵌其中的中资项目来说，读懂这场「公投」的潜台词，或许比盯紧计票数字更为重要。

# Sarawak sets December 12 for state polls: a referendum on oil sovereignty and autonomy

Sarawak's 13th state election is set for 12 December 2026, with nomination on 28 November and early voting on 8 December. According to the election commission, 2,058,781 eligible voters are spread across 82 state constituencies, and the estimated budget is RM174.94 million (about US$38 million). On its face, this is a routine local contest. Yet as the South China Morning Post reports, the real stakes lie not in who wins or loses, but in whether the ruling Gabungan Parti Sarawak (GPS) can use the ballot to extract greater economic autonomy from the federal government — above all, regulatory control over oil and gas. A vote with no suspense is quietly turning into a referendum on the "Sarawak First" agenda.

## The calendar: no suspense, yet full of meaning

GPS has declared it will contest all 82 seats. The coalition of homegrown parties swept 76 seats in the 2021 state election and remains firmly entrenched. On the opposition side, the Democratic Action Party (DAP) plans to contest only seven, perhaps eight, constituencies — a telling sign that, against GPS's overwhelming "kingmaker" dominance, the space for rival forces has been squeezed into a handful of urban, ethnic-Chinese strongholds.

So why does a contest with a foregone conclusion still matter? The answer is narrative. By contesting every seat, GPS reinforces its "Sarawak First" slogan and advertises an ability to steer state affairs without depending on peninsula-based partners. On the eve of federal bargaining, this is a calculated show of strength: whichever way national politics swings, GPS will remain an independent and formidable power broker in the federal architecture.

## The oil fight: Petros versus Petronas

Beneath the campaign lies the sharpest tension of all — hydrocarbons. Through Petros, a wholly state-owned company, Sarawak is trying to tighten its regulatory grip over the state's oil and gas reserves, directly challenging the federal Petronas's monopoly over managing the country's hydrocarbon resources. Put plainly, the dispute is about who gets to set the rules for Sarawak's immensely valuable petroleum activities.

The numbers reveal the weight. Sarawak alone holds 61 per cent of Malaysia's oil and gas reserves; neighbouring Sabah holds 19 per cent, meaning East Malaysia together accounts for 80 per cent. This means the standoff centred on Sarawak reaches far beyond the state — it touches the energy lifeline of the entire federation. Sarawak demands clear visibility over the source and delivery of 1.2 billion standard cubic feet of gas per day, not a trivial figure but the bedrock supply for the state's power plants and industries.

## The autonomy claim: old scores from the 1963 Agreement

The oil dispute is, at root, a fight over autonomy. Sarawak asserts a special status under the Malaysia Agreement 1963 (MA63), the founding compact that set out the terms under which Sabah and Sarawak joined the federation. On 31 October, Chief Minister Abang Johari publicly urged voters to back stronger control of strategic resources, citing Sarawak's takeover of Bintulu port and its investment in Affin Bank as proof that "holding assets in our own hands" is no longer mere rhetoric.

The legal path, however, is uneven. Ports and harbours generally fall under state jurisdiction unless declared federal; petroleum powers, by contrast, sit in the explicit lists dividing federal and state authority, and the two cannot be conflated. If the two sides fail to settle, the Federal Court has already scheduled hearings for 25–29 January 2027. For GPS, turning a constitutional dispute into an election slogan may rally voters, but it also makes compromise politically expensive — precisely the risk analysts warn about.

## Kingmaker in federal politics: GPS and Anwar's delicate balance

Zoom out, and the Sarawak poll is really a miniature of federal politics. GPS is a pillar of support for Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim's government in Parliament — a genuine kingmaker — yet Anwar's Pakatan Harapan (PH) has stumbled badly in recent state contests: losses in Sabah, Johor and Negeri Sembilan, with Malacca voting on 14 November, ahead of Sarawak's nomination day.

This produces a set of cross-pressures. Petronas fills federal coffers, while GPS props up Anwar's administration in Parliament. As analysis from the ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute notes, Anwar faces a dilemma: defend the federation's fiscal and constitutional authority without alienating a crucial ally. Small wonder many observers read this state election as not only a verdict on GPS's record, but a test of Sarawak's capacity to wrest more concessions from Putrajaya, the federal administrative capital.

## The Overseas Chinese calculus: a minority amid resource politics

No discussion of Sarawak is complete without its ethnic Chinese community. Ethnic Chinese make up roughly a quarter of the state's population — a pivotal commercial force and a decisive bloc in several urban seats. DAP's decision to contest only seven or eight seats reflects the constrained room for manoeuvre in Chinese-majority cities: under GPS's nativist narrative, the Chinese party's role looks more defensive than aggressive.

The economic dimension is more intricate. Resource nationalism and the autonomy agenda cut both ways for local Chinese businessmen. On one hand, the state government's tightening control over oil and gas, ports and financial assets could yield a "localisation dividend" that favours merchants with deep grassroots networks. On the other, the protracted federal–state tug-of-war casts uncertainty over energy pricing, infrastructure approvals and the predictability of Chinese-funded projects. Indeed, Chinese capital's footprint in Sarawak is already deep — from hydropower hubs to the liquefied natural gas chain to the Pan-Borneo Highway — and Beijing's involvement lends the struggle for "resource control" an extra layer of regional geo-economic meaning. For the Chinese business community, finding its footing between federal and state, local and foreign, is a practical arithmetic problem.

## Risks and variables: will campaign slogans slam the settlement window shut?

As the campaign heats up, the most immediate worry is whether electoral language will crush the room for a Petros–Petronas settlement. Ahmad Zaharuddin, an analyst at Global Asia Advisors, warns that once a constitutional dispute becomes an election slogan, any public concession by either side carries a heavy political price, and even a judicial ruling turns "politically dangerous."

The task at hand is to clinch a settlement before the Federal Court sits in January 2027: secure stable gas supply for Sarawak, clarify the two companies' responsibilities, and agree on financial terms. An independent technical committee is reviewing the differences under a joint declaration issued in May 2025; the parties are discussing a joint venture for gas exploration and production, and even the possibility of supply from near the Natuna waters. If the impasse drags on, what suffers is investor appetite, the pace of petroleum projects, and the stability of the region's energy supply — bad news for GPS, for Anwar, and for ordinary Sarawakians alike.

## Conclusion: a state election, a mirror of federalism

Sarawak's 13th state election looks, on the surface, like a routine rotation in local politics; underneath, it is a mirror held up to the strains of Malaysian federalism. How a resource-rich East Malaysian state renegotiates the distribution of power and wealth with the peninsula centre is a question without a neat answer. Whatever the result on 12 December, the issues of hydrocarbon sovereignty and autonomy will only grow louder across this land. And for the local ethnic Chinese community, and for the Chinese-funded projects woven into it, reading the subtext of this "referendum" may matter more than watching the vote count.

## Further reading

**Malaysia Agreement 1963 (MA63):** The founding compact under which Sabah and Sarawak joined the federation of Malaysia, on the basis of which both states claim special status — in immigration, justice and resources — above ordinary states, the legal root of East Malaysia's autonomy claims.

**Putrajaya:** Malaysia's federal administrative centre, commonly used as a metonym for the federal government; "wresting concessions from Putrajaya" means negotiating resource and fiscal powers with the federal centre.
