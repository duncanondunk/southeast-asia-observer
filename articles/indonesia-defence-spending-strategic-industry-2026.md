# 印尼军费只占0.8%：真正落后的不是百分比，是产线

据联合早报报道，印度尼西亚总统普拉博沃上台两年来大举添置武器，但国防开支占国内生产总值的比重仍在低位徘徊。智库学者呼吁把这一比重提到1.5%，另一名学者则提醒：买战斗机不能只买回一架飞机，还要买回一条产线。这两种声音看似在争论钱多钱少，实际上指向同一个问题——印尼想买的到底是装备，还是制造装备的能力？

## 0.8%的账本：预算涨了，比重没涨

据《雅加达环球报》报道，在印尼外交政策年度论坛上，智库实验室45（LAB 45）高级顾问安迪·维贾延托（Andi Widjajanto）提出一个直白的数字：把国防开支占国内生产总值的比重提高到1.5%。他给出的背景更刺眼——2000年至2024年，这个比重平均只有0.68%；普拉博沃政府上台两年来军购新闻接连不断，2026年正式预算中的国防占比也只微升到0.8%。

一句点评：这条曲线说的不是印尼“没花钱”，而是“花钱的速度没追上经济总量”。军购是新闻，比重是账本。新闻可以连续两年刷屏，账本却几乎原地踏步。

安迪的原话并不委婉：“我们在军事装备现代化方面行动迟缓，这就是我们相较于周边邻国显得落后的原因。”需要说清楚的是，比重低并不自动等于战力弱：印尼人口超过2.7亿，现役兵力约40万，经济体量居东盟之首，分母大本身就压低了比例。但比值衡量的是优先次序——当GDP增长而国防份额不动，意味着安全议题在预算桌上并没有真正往前挪。

## 新加坡的3%：一个容易被误用的标杆

普拉博沃本人也表达过类似焦虑。据联合早报报道，他今年9月与资深媒体人交流时以新加坡为例说：“数十年来，新加坡始终把军费维持在国内生产总值3%左右……新加坡虽是小岛国，却把独立自主视为重中之重。他们不介意维持3%的比重，因为他们拒绝受制于他国。”

这番话值得琢磨，但不宜照抄。新加坡是城市国家，实行全民服役与后备动员，地缘处境、人口规模、财政结构都与群岛国家印尼完全不同。3%之所以能维持，靠的是一套与之配套的制度，而不是把这个数字写进预算案就能生效。把新加坡的百分比当成印尼的目标值，是把结果当成了方法。

不过普拉博沃真正强调的并不是数字，而是“拒绝受制于他国”。这一表述点出了问题的实质：军费比重之争，背后是战略自主的成本由谁承担。如果自主只体现在采购清单上，却没有本国的维修、弹药与零部件体系，那么一旦供应关系生变，清单越长反而越脆弱。

## 从“买军火”到“买产业”：维蒙特的那句话

印尼国际伊斯兰大学社会学院院长维蒙特（Vimont）给出了另一条思路。据联合早报报道，他呼吁雅加达在购买战斗机时，必须确保交易能为包括上游原材料及零部件供应商在内的国内产业带来实质效益。“不应止步于单纯购买军火，”他说，“这些交易须带动本土战略工业发展。国防领域涉及诸多相关产业，例如电气系统和不锈钢制造……战略工业是发达经济体的标志。”

这句点评点到了要害：国防采购的溢出效应不会自动发生。一份合同写进“本地化条款”只是起点，真正决定收益的是条款能否量化——本地供应商能拿到多少份额？技术转让转让的是总装图纸还是工艺参数？认证标准由谁制定？没有这些细节，“带动本土工业”很容易变成新闻稿里的修辞。

维蒙特举的例子很具体：电气系统、不锈钢制造。这些不是高精尖的宣传词，而是可以被中小企业承接的工序。对一个拥有庞大制造业基础却长期依赖进口装备的国家而言，把这些工序留在国内，比多买几架飞机更能改变产业结构。这也解释了为什么“战略工业”被他视为发达经济体的标志——它衡量的是一国能否把订单转化成能力，而不是能否签下订单。

## 可汗战机与兵工厂：两份合同的含金量

印尼政府多次强调，军购协议包含带动本土工业发展的条款。据联合早报报道，国防部以去年采购48架土耳其“可汗”（KAAN）战斗机为例指出，相关协议包含“建立本土工业基地”的内容。另据路透社报道，印尼防长夏弗里在8月与中国防长董军和外长王毅会谈后宣布，两国计划在印尼联合设立一家兵工厂，生产弹药和火箭，并可能从中国引进技术，预计2027年投产。

两份合同的性质并不相同。战机合同买到的是交付即形成的能力，工业基地条款能否兑现，要看后续订单、产线建设与工程团队；兵工厂项目买到的是一种逐年积累的能力——标准、工艺、人员与供应链一旦落地，会长期留在本国。换句话说，前者是消费，后者更接近投资。

但要防止另一种误读：组装不等于吸收。把零件装成整机，与能自主设计、改进和维修是两回事。兵工厂2027年能否如期投产、投产后能否稳定供货、国产化比例能提到多少，才是检验合作成色的尺度。若只宣布开工不问产出，那么“联合生产”与“联合采购”没有本质区别。

## 多供应商的红利与账单

印尼的采购清单横跨多个国家：法国的“阵风”战斗机、土耳其的“可汗”与无人机、韩国的KF-21合作项目、法国的潜艇技术转让、意大利赠送并于今年9月抵达的“加里波第”号航母，以及中国的弹药工厂。这种被外界称为“加多加多”的多头下注，红利显而易见——不依赖单一供应国，就有更多讨价还价的余地，也不至于因一国断供而全线停摆。

账单也同样真实。每套装备都带着自己的通信架构、软件接口、维护规程、训练体系和后勤标准。来源越多元，把这些系统整合进同一支部队就越困难。据有关报道，印尼在整合不同来源装备时面临互操作性（interoperability）挑战，侦察、决策、通信、攻击与保障各环节要无缝衔接并不容易。

点评一句：多元化把成本从采购价格转移到了全生命周期。签约时看似拿到了更优惠的条件，服役后却要为兼容、培训和备件支付长期费用。对预算本就紧张的雅加达来说，这笔账不能只算在合同签订那天。

## 预算结构才是硬约束

回到安迪的数字。1.5%是一个政治目标，不是魔法阈值。设想到2027年印尼把国防比重真的提到1.5%，如果新增开支大部分流向人员与日常运转，采购与工业投资能分到多少仍是未知数。据相关报道，印尼国防预算中相当一部分用于军人薪资与日常维持，真正用于装备采购的比例有限。

这才是“本土工业条款”面临的最大约束。技术转让、本地供应商培育、质量认证体系都需要跨年度的稳定投入，而采购计划往往随政府任期和财政年度波动。当预算吃紧时，最先被压缩的通常是那些短期看不到战果的项目——恰恰是工业基础建设。

换句话说，如果没有多年期的财政承诺，补偿贸易条款可能异化成另一种成本转移：要求外国承包商在印尼采购零部件、培训工人，却不为这些要求单独买单。表面看本地企业拿到了订单，实则成本已被计入合同价，最终仍由本国财政承担。这不能算真正意义上的工业政策。

## 地区意义：东盟的自主如何计价

印尼是东盟人口与经济体量最大的国家，国防开支占比却长期低于地区内多个邻国。这种反差本身就是一种信号：东南亚国家的战略自主，越来越不能以“不选边站”的外交表态来衡量，而要看能否独立维护装备、补充弹药、训练人员。

普拉博沃政府把军购与本土工业绑定，也在重塑对外关系的结构。武器贸易不再是简单买卖：与土耳其的合作带来第五代战机技术，与法国的交易附带潜艇建造与转让，与中国的兵工厂项目则把技术标准、零部件供应和人员培训长期捆绑在一起。这些联系一旦形成，调整的成本远高于更换一型飞机。

需要克制的一点是：这些合作不应被简单解读为选边。印尼长期坚持自由积极的外交政策，同时与多个大国保持安全合作。真正的判断标准不是它向谁买武器，而是这些交易是否提升了它说不的能力——能不能在供应链被切断时仍维持基本运转，才叫自主。

## 结论：该盯的是产线，不是百分比

把军费从0.8%提到1.5%，当然有意义，但那只是必要条件。对印尼而言，更值得追问的是：48架“可汗”战的本地化条款最终落实了多少份额？中印尼兵工厂能否在2027年如期投产并稳定供货？有多少本国企业能通过国防认证、进入供应链？新增预算中有多少真正流向研发与制造，而不是薪资与维持？

这些问题的答案不会出现在军购新闻的标题里，却决定了印尼是在建设一支军队，还是在租借一支军队。国防现代化的难点从来不是签下最大的订单，而是让订单结束时，国内留下些什么。

需要补一句：安迪提出的1.5%与印尼长期0.68%的均值之间，差的不只是0.8个百分点，而是一整套把预算转化为产线的制度安排。这也是为什么“军费涨了”与“工业没起来”可以同时成立。对任何把战略自主当作目标的东南亚国家来说，这条道理都成立。

## 延伸阅读

**国防工业基础（Defence industrial base）**：指一国在本土设计、生产、维修和升级武器装备的能力，涵盖从原材料到系统集成的完整链条，是战略自主的物质前提。

**补偿贸易条款（Offset agreement）**：军购合同中要求卖方在买方国进行采购、技术转让或本地生产安排的条件，成效取决于本地含量比例、可量化指标与执行监督。

**战略自主（Strategic autonomy）**：国家在安全与外交决策中不受外部力量强制的能力，其基础不仅是不结盟的政策宣示，更包括独立的装备维护、弹药供应与工业体系。

# Indonesia's 0.8% defence budget: the real gap is the production line, not the percentage

Indonesia has bought aircraft, submarines and now an aircraft carrier at a striking pace since President Prabowo took office. Yet its defence spending as a share of GDP has barely moved. One think-tank adviser wants that share raised to 1.5 per cent; another argues that buying a fighter jet should bring home more than the jet itself — it should bring home a production line. Both complaints point to the same question: is Jakarta purchasing equipment, or the capacity to make equipment?

## The 0.8% ledger: a bigger budget, the same weight

According to The Jakarta Globe, Andi Widjajanto, a senior adviser at the think tank LAB 45, told Indonesia's annual foreign policy forum that the country should raise defence spending to 1.5 per cent of GDP. The context he supplied was harsher: the share averaged just 0.68 per cent between 2000 and 2024, and even after two years of headline-grabbing procurement, the formal 2026 budget lifted it only slightly, to 0.8 per cent.

Worth noting: that curve does not say Indonesia has spent nothing. It says spending has failed to keep pace with the size of the economy. Procurement makes news; the ratio keeps the ledger. Headlines can run for two straight years while the ledger barely shifts.

Andi was blunt: "We are slow in modernising our military equipment, and that is why we lag behind our neighbours." A low ratio does not automatically mean weak forces — Indonesia has more than 270 million people, roughly 400,000 active personnel and the largest economy in ASEAN, all of which inflate the denominator. But a ratio measures priority. When GDP grows and the defence share stands still, it means security has not actually moved up the budget table.

## Singapore's 3%: a benchmark easily misread

Prabowo has voiced similar concern. According to Lianhe Zaobao, speaking to veteran journalists in September he pointed to Singapore: "For decades Singapore has kept defence spending at around 3 per cent of GDP. Though a small island, it treats independence as paramount. They do not mind maintaining 3 per cent because they refuse to be subordinated to others."

The remark deserves attention, but not imitation. Singapore is a city-state with universal conscription and a reserve mobilisation system; its threat perception, population and fiscal structure are unlike Indonesia's. The 3 per cent holds because a whole set of institutions supports it — writing the number into a budget does not reproduce those institutions. Treating Singapore's percentage as Jakarta's target confuses the outcome with the method.

What Prabowo stressed, however, was not the number but the refusal to be subordinated. That is the substance of the debate: who pays for strategic autonomy? If autonomy is expressed only through a procurement list, with no domestic maintenance, ammunition or components sector behind it, then a longer list makes the country more fragile, not less, the moment a supplier relationship turns sour.

## From buying guns to buying industry: Vimont's point

Vimont, dean of the Faculty of Social Sciences at Indonesia's International Islamic University, offered a different route. According to Lianhe Zaobao, he urged Jakarta to ensure that fighter-jet deals deliver tangible benefits to domestic industries, including upstream raw-material and component suppliers. "We should not stop at simply buying weapons," he said. "They must drive the development of local strategic industries. Defence touches many related sectors — electrical systems and stainless-steel manufacturing, for example. Strategic industry is a hallmark of developed economies."

That is the nub of it. Spillovers from defence procurement do not happen by themselves. Writing a "localisation clause" into a contract is only a start. What matters is whether the clause can be measured: what share goes to local suppliers? Does technology transfer cover assembly drawings or process parameters? Who sets the certification standards? Without those details, "supporting local industry" stays as rhetoric in a press release.

His examples are deliberately unglamorous: electrical systems, stainless steel. These are not promotional buzzwords but processes that small and medium-sized firms can actually handle. For a country with a large manufacturing base that still imports much of its equipment, keeping those processes at home changes the industrial structure more than adding a few aircraft. That is why he treats strategic industry as a marker of development — it measures whether a country can turn orders into capabilities.

## KAAN and the ammunition factory: what the contracts are worth

The Indonesian government has repeatedly said its arms deals contain provisions for domestic industry. According to Lianhe Zaobao, the Defence Ministry cited last year's purchase of 48 Turkish KAAN fighter jets, whose agreement includes "establishing a domestic industrial base." Reuters reported that after August talks with Chinese Defence Minister Dong Jun and Foreign Minister Wang Yi, Defence Minister Sjafrie Sjamsoeddin announced plans for a jointly built arms factory in Indonesia producing ammunition and rockets, with possible Chinese technology transfer and production expected in 2027.

The two contracts are different in kind. A fighter-jet deal buys capability that exists on delivery; whether an industrial-base clause materialises depends on follow-on orders, plant construction and engineering staff. An ammunition factory buys a capability that accumulates: standards, processes, personnel and supply chains, once established, stay in the country. One is consumption; the other is closer to investment.

But another misreading must be avoided: assembly is not absorption. Putting parts together is not the same as designing, improving and maintaining a system independently. Whether the factory starts production on schedule in 2027, whether it delivers consistently, and how far local content can rise — these are the measures that count. If only the groundbreaking is announced and output is never asked about, "joint production" is indistinguishable from "joint purchasing."

## The dividend and the tax of many suppliers

Indonesia's order book spans countries: French Rafale fighters, Turkish KAAN jets and drones, a Korean KF-21 partnership, French submarine technology transfer, an Italian carrier gifted and delivered in September, and a Chinese ammunition plant. This multi-supplier strategy — popularly described as "gado-gado" — has an obvious dividend. Dependence on any single source falls, leaving more room to bargain and less exposure if one country cuts supply.

The bill is equally real. Every system brings its own communications architecture, software interfaces, maintenance procedures, training regime and logistics standards. The more diverse the sources, the harder it is to integrate them into one coherent force. Reports note that Indonesia faces interoperability challenges, and that seamless links between reconnaissance, decision-making, communications, strike and sustainment are not easily achieved.

Put simply: diversification moves cost from the purchase price to the life cycle. A government may secure better terms at signature, then pay for compatibility, training and spares for decades. For a budget as tight as Jakarta's, that calculation cannot stop at contract day.

## The budget structure is the hard constraint

Back to Andi's number. A target of 1.5 per cent is a political goal, not a magic threshold. Imagine Indonesia actually reached it by 2027: if most of the additional money went to salaries and day-to-day operations, how much would be left for procurement and industrial investment? Reports suggest a substantial share of the defence budget already goes to personnel and upkeep, leaving a limited portion for new equipment.

That is the real constraint on local-industry clauses. Technology transfer, supplier development and quality certification all need stable, multi-year funding, while procurement plans move with election cycles and annual budgets. When money is tight, the first items cut are usually those with no visible short-term result — precisely industrial-base work.

Without a multi-year fiscal commitment, offset provisions can degenerate into a cost shift: foreign contractors are told to source components and train workers in Indonesia, but nobody budgets separately for those requirements. Local firms may win orders, yet the cost is already embedded in the contract price and ultimately borne by the national treasury. That is not industrial policy in any meaningful sense.

## The regional meaning: how ASEAN prices autonomy

Indonesia is ASEAN's largest country by population and economic weight, yet its defence share has long trailed several neighbours. The contrast is itself a signal: strategic autonomy in Southeast Asia can no longer be measured by declarations of non-alignment. It has to be measured by whether a country can maintain its equipment, replenish ammunition and train its own people.

Tying procurement to domestic industry is also changing the structure of Indonesia's external relations. Arms trade is no longer a simple sale: cooperation with Turkey brings fifth-generation fighter technology, the French deal carries submarine construction and transfer, and the Chinese ammunition plant binds standards, component supply and training together over the long term. Once such links form, unwinding them costs far more than swapping an aircraft type.

One caution is in order. These deals should not be read simplistically as choosing sides. Indonesia has long pursued an active, independent foreign policy and maintains security cooperation with several major powers. The meaningful test is not who sells the weapons but whether the transactions improve Indonesia's ability to say no — whether it can keep running when a supply line is cut. That is what autonomy means.

## Conclusion: watch the production line, not the percentage

Raising defence spending from 0.8 to 1.5 per cent matters, but it is only a necessary condition. The questions that count are these: how much of the localisation clause attached to the 48 KAAN jets will actually be delivered? Can the joint ammunition plant start production in 2027 and supply reliably? How many Indonesian firms will pass defence certification and enter the supply chain? How much of any budget increase will reach research and manufacturing rather than salaries and upkeep?

None of those answers will appear in a procurement headline, yet they determine whether Indonesia is building an army or renting one. The hard part of military modernisation has never been signing the largest order; it is what remains at home when the order is complete. That lesson holds for any Southeast Asian country that treats strategic autonomy as a goal.

One last point: the distance between Andi's 1.5 per cent and the long-run average of 0.68 per cent is not merely 0.8 percentage points. It is a whole set of institutions for turning budget into production lines. That is how "spending is up" and "industry has not followed" can both be true at once.

## Further reading

**Defence industrial base (国防工业基础)** — A country's capacity to design, produce, maintain and upgrade weapons domestically, spanning raw materials to systems integration; the material prerequisite for strategic autonomy.

**Offset agreement (补偿贸易条款)** — A procurement condition requiring the seller to source, transfer technology or manufacture in the buyer's country. Its value depends on local-content ratios, measurable targets and enforcement.

**Strategic autonomy (战略自主)** — The ability to make security and foreign-policy choices free of external coercion. Its foundation is not a declaration of non-alignment but independent maintenance, ammunition supply and an industrial base.
