# 棉兰一期夏令营，与北京对印尼华人离散群体的耐心赌注

## 一场夏令营，一道更长的影子
2026年9月16日，在苏门答腊北部的棉兰市，一期为期两周的夏令营在舞龙舞狮与武术表演中开营，横幅上写着「中华文化大乐园」。在外人看来，这不过是一档寻常的暑期文化项目：棉中中小学的华裔青少年跟着从广西师范大学远道而来的老师学书法、国画、剪纸与扎染。温和，甚至讨喜。但把镜头拉远，这期营地是一扇小巧而整齐的窗，映出更宏大的东西——北京正以系统、耐心的姿态，维系着与全球最大华裔离散群体之一的文化纽带，并让它保持温热与亲近。

## 为何是棉兰，为何是现在
棉兰举足轻重。这座北苏门答腊首府是印尼华人社会的历史重镇之一，坐拥延续数百年的华校、宗亲会与粤语—闽南—客家交融的商贸文化。选择棉中中小学作承办方绝非偶然：这是华文教育被定为非法之前那个时代的活标本。2026年的这期营地，恰逢印尼与中国关系温热而审慎的时刻——雅加达一面争取北京的投资，一面守护自身不结盟的自主；而华人少数族裔，在1965至1998年反华暴力过去数十年后，仍是一个政治敏感话题。文化营，正是最柔软的切入点。

## 禁令之后：离散族群重新找回自己的语言
在苏哈托「新秩序」（1966至1998年）的大多数年份里，华语、华校与华族文化表达被一概禁止；华人被强推同化。1998年改革时期（Reformasi）后禁令解除，一代印尼华人开始重新拾回父辈被迫藏起的遗产。如今对华文教学的需求，远远超出合格师资的供给。北京的回应是一套分层供给：大学里的孔子学院（Confucius Institute）、与私立华校的结对，以及此类短期、高认同的营地。这期营地并非制造学习欲望，而是去接住离散社群自己重新发现的那种需求。

## 广西不动声色的外交足迹
注意谁来操盘。营地由广西海外联谊会主办、广西师范大学承办——并非某个北京部委。这是去中心化的软实力。广西与越南接壤，自诩为中国面向东盟的门户，已把文化外交做成省一级的生意。南宁永久承办中国—东盟博览会；如今其高校在区域各地办起离散族群营地。话说直白些：面向华侨华人的文化外联，已不再是中央政府的专属领地——省级政府竞相争当中国在海外那张亲切的面孔。

## 社群早已自带的文化
开营仪式很说明问题。当地学生自己表演了舞龙舞狮；中国老师则献上歌曲、武术与民乐。换言之，营地强化的是社群本来就持有的文化，而非植入一种外来文化。对于首先是印尼公民的华裔青年而言，这一区分至关重要。风险所在——也是东道国政府之所以留意——在于「文化传承」与「忠诚归属」可能模糊。雅加达的容忍，取决于营地读起来是文化、而非政治。

## 算算这期营地的真实分量
规模让戏剧性降温。「中华文化大乐园」项目每年在数十个国家办数十期此类营地，但每一期只触达几十到数百名青少年。印尼华人的规模约在二百万至三百万之间。所以这并非大规模的思想灌输；它是关系性与象征性的——一面横幅、一份情谊、一位被记住的老师。对北京而言，其价值是累积性的：一代对中华文化怀有温热私人联结的离散青年，是任何未来影响力角逐中一笔耐久的资产。

## 软实力还是文化传承：雅加达的红线画在哪里
区域内的批评者警告这是「长臂」文化伸手；辩护者则称这是渴望寻根的离散青年应有的正当遗产。真相两者皆是，而那条线是被谈判出来的，并非固定。印尼历届政府总体上容许此类活动，只要它们停留在文化层面、不侵蚀国家认同。北京方面则刻意把营地框定为「中华文化」而非「中国政治」——这是一个让它留有余地的慎重选择。

## 小小营地背后的大赌注
放眼东南亚——马来西亚、泰国、菲律宾皆有类似营地——棉兰这期只是更大幅织锦中的一针。随着大国竞争加剧，北京正悄悄地把本区域华裔社群，从需要撇清的包袱，重新视为一道耐久、亲和的人桥。棉兰一所校园里的两周营地，改变不了任何力量均势。但把它乘以年头、乘以省份、乘以一份份情谊，便成了一套战略：耐心、廉价，且极难反制。

## 延伸阅读
- **中华文化大乐园（Chinese Culture Grand Park）**：北京面向海外华裔青少年的旗舰短期文化营项目，由省级海外联谊会与结对高校承办。
- **改革时期（Reformasi）**：印尼1998年的政治转折，解除了苏哈托时代对华语、华校与公开文化表达的禁令。
- **棉中中小学（Zhong Hua School）**：棉兰的历史性华文学校，禁令前时代的存续机构，也是离散文化项目的天然承办方。

# A Medan Summer Camp and Beijing's Patient Wager on the Indonesian Chinese Diaspora

## A summer camp with a longer shadow
On 16 September 2026, in the North Sumatran city of Medan, a two-week camp opened with dragon-and-lion dances and martial-arts demonstrations, under a banner reading "Chinese Culture Grand Park" (中华文化大乐园). To the casual eye it looked like any summer enrichment programme: ethnic Chinese teenagers from Medan's Zhong Hua School learning calligraphy, ink painting, paper-cutting and tie-dye from teachers flown in from Guangxi Normal University. Benign, even charming. But step back, and the camp is a small, tidy window onto something larger — Beijing's systematic, patient effort to keep cultural ties with one of the world's largest ethnic Chinese diasporas alive and affectionate.

## Why Medan, and why now
Medan matters. The capital of North Sumatra is a historic heartland of the Indonesian Chinese community, home to centuries-old schools, clan associations and a Cantonese-Hokkien-Hakka commercial culture. Choosing Zhong Hua School as host is no accident: it is a living institution from the era before Chinese-language education was criminalised. The 2026 camp arrives as Indonesia-China relations are warm but watchful — Jakarta courts Beijing's investment while guarding its non-aligned autonomy, and the ethnic Chinese minority remains a politically sensitive subject decades after the anti-Chinese violence of 1965–1998. A cultural camp is the softest possible entry point.

## After the bans: a diaspora rediscovering its tongue
For most of Suharto's New Order (1966–1998), Chinese language, schools and cultural expression were banned outright, and ethnic Chinese were pressed into assimilation. After the 1998 reformasi the bans lifted, and a generation of Indonesian Chinese began reclaiming a heritage their parents had been forced to hide. Demand for Chinese-language instruction now far outstrips the supply of trained teachers. Beijing's answer is a layered offering: Confucius Institutes at universities, partnerships with private Chinese schools, and short, high-affinity camps like this one. The camp does not manufacture the desire to learn — it meets a demand the diaspora rediscovered itself.

## Guangxi's quiet diplomatic footprint
Notice who is running it. The camp is organised by the Guangxi Overseas Friendship Association and hosted by Guangxi Normal University — not a Beijing ministry. This is decentralised soft power. Guangxi, bordering Vietnam and branding itself China's gateway to ASEAN, has turned cultural diplomacy into provincial business. Nanning permanently hosts the China-ASEAN Expo; now its universities run diaspora camps across the region. Stated plainly: cultural outreach to overseas Chinese is no longer the exclusive preserve of the central state — provincial governments compete to be the friendly face of China abroad.

## Culture the community already carries
The opening ceremony is revealing. Local students performed the dragon and lion dance themselves; the Chinese teachers supplied the songs, martial arts and folk music. In other words, the camp reinforces a culture the community already holds, rather than implanting a foreign one. For ethnic Chinese youth who are, first and foremost, Indonesian citizens, that distinction matters. The risk — and the reason host governments pay attention — is that "heritage" and "loyalty" can blur. Jakarta's toleration depends on the camp reading as cultural, not political.

## Counting the camp's real weight
Scale tempers the drama. The Chinese Culture Grand Park programme runs dozens of such camps across dozens of countries each year, yet each reaches only scores or hundreds of youngsters. Indonesia's ethnic Chinese number somewhere between two and three million. So this is not mass indoctrination; it is relational and symbolic — a banner, a friendship, a teacher remembered. Its value to Beijing is cumulative: a generation of diaspora youth with warm, personal bonds to Chinese culture is a durable asset in any future contest for influence.

## Soft power, or heritage — where Jakarta draws the line
Critics in the region warn of a "long-arm" cultural reach; defenders call it legitimate heritage for diaspora youth who want to know their roots. The truth is both, and the line is negotiated, not fixed. Indonesian governments have generally allowed such activities so long as they stay cultural and do not erode national identity. Beijing, for its part, is careful to frame the camp as "Chinese culture" rather than "Chinese politics" — a deliberate choice that keeps the door open.

## The bigger wager behind a small camp
Seen across Southeast Asia — similar camps run in Malaysia, Thailand and the Philippines — the Medan programme is one stitch in a wider tapestry. As great-power competition intensifies, Beijing is quietly recasting the region's ethnic Chinese communities from a liability to be disavowed into a durable, affinitive bridge. A two-week camp in a Medan schoolyard will not shift any balance of power. But multiply it by years, by provinces, by friendships, and you have a strategy: patient, inexpensive, and remarkably hard to counter.

## Further reading
- **Chinese Culture Grand Park (中华文化大乐园)**: Beijing's flagship short-term cultural camp programme for ethnic Chinese youth abroad, run through provincial overseas-friendship associations and partner universities.
- **Reformasi (改革时期)**: Indonesia's 1998 political transition that lifted the Suharto-era bans on Chinese language, schools and public cultural expression.
- **Zhong Hua School (棉中中小学)**: Medan's historic Chinese-medium school, a surviving institution from the pre-ban era and a natural host for diaspora cultural programmes.
