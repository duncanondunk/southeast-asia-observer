# 赤道上的发射场：印尼拟建首座航天港，为何牵动俄美澳神经？

## 比亚克岛：赤道边的"天然发射台"

印尼正计划在紧邻赤道的比亚克岛（Biak）建设国家首个航天发射场。赤道附近发射可借助地球自转最大线速度，节省运载火箭燃料、提升有效载荷，是航天大国青睐的选址。印尼国家研究创新署（BRIN）已与俄罗斯国家航天公司（Roscosmos）展开初步接触。对一个长期以资源与人口规模见长的群岛国家而言，拥有自主发射能力意味着跃入"航天国家"行列。

## 普拉博沃访俄，议程暗含航天合作

《雅加达邮报》8月31日报道，印尼总统普拉博沃本周将前往俄罗斯符拉迪沃斯托克与普京会晤，议程可能包含航天发射场建设。对印尼来说，自主发射能力既是科技声誉，也是战略资产——它意味着不再完全依赖外国提供商的搭载服务，并在区域太空竞赛中占据一席。

## 俄大使承诺：绝不在印尼设军事基地

俄驻印尼大使托尔切诺夫明确表态：即便俄方参与建设，也绝不会在印尼设立军事基地。他强调，印尼向来奉行不结盟外交政策，无论是美国、俄罗斯还是澳大利亚，都不会被允许在印尼领土驻军。"我相信以后也不会有。"这一表态意在消解外界对印俄潜在军事合作的担忧，也为合作划定了政治边界。

## 不结盟传统下的安全疑虑

2025年初，曾有传言称俄方拟在比亚克部署军机，引发澳大利亚担忧；托尔切诺夫斥其"极其荒谬"。但疑虑并未完全消散：一个由外国协助、紧邻赤道、可作卫星发射之用的设施，天然带有军民两用属性。印尼是否用它发射军用卫星，决定权在雅加达而非莫斯科，这恰是不结盟姿态下的主权宣示。

## 能源合作先行，西方"二级制裁"阴影

航天之外，印俄能源合作更令西方侧目。印尼能源部长巴赫利8月初称，政府正草拟进口俄罗斯石油的长期合同；受美伊战争引发的能源危机影响，印尼已决定增购约1.5亿桶俄油。托尔切诺夫直言，两国一举一动都受西方严格监视，随时可能对其实施"二级制裁"。雅加达则坚持：如何满足自身能源需求，"由不得他国干涉"。

## 小国的大国平衡术

把航天、能源与不选边站的姿态叠加观察，印尼正演练一套典型的中等强国平衡术：借俄方技术补齐能力短板，同时以不结盟原则划清红线；既不愿被西方制裁大棒左右，也不愿沦为任何大国的前沿基地。比亚克岛上空的火箭若真升空，映照的将是东南亚另一重地缘图景。

## 延伸阅读

**不结盟（Non-alignment）**：冷战时期兴起的外交原则，主张不与大国军事集团结盟；印尼是万隆会议与不结盟运动的奠基者之一，该传统至今影响其安全政策。

**商业航天（Commercial Space）**：以企业为主体、市场为导向的航天活动，涵盖发射服务、卫星制造与数据应用，正在重塑全球太空产业格局。

**二级制裁（Secondary Sanctions）**：制裁发起国对与受制裁对象进行正常交易的第三方施加的惩罚，常被用作延伸管辖权的工具。

# A launch site on the equator: Indonesia's first spaceport plan and why it alarms Russia-watchers in Washington and Canberra

## Biak Island: a 'natural launch pad' by the equator

Indonesia plans to build its first national space launch site on Biak Island, close to the equator. Launching near the equator exploits the earth's maximum rotational speed, saving rocket fuel and increasing payload — which is why equatorial sites are prized by spacefaring nations. Indonesia's National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN) has already opened preliminary talks with Russia's Roscosmos. For an archipelagic state long known for its resources and population, an indigenous launch capability would mean entry into the club of "space nations."

## Prabowo's Russia trip carries a space-cooperation agenda

The Jakarta Post reported on 31 August that President Prabowo Subianto will travel to Vladivostok this week to meet Vladimir Putin, and the agenda may include the launch-site project. For Indonesia, an autonomous launch capacity is both technological prestige and a strategic asset — it means no longer relying entirely on foreign providers for rides, and securing a seat in the regional space race.

## Russian envoy vows: no military base on Indonesian soil

Russia's ambassador to Indonesia, Tolchenov, made the position clear: even if Moscow participates in construction, it will never establish a military base in Indonesia. He stressed that Indonesia's long-standing non-aligned foreign policy means no foreign power — the United States, Russia or Australia — will be allowed to station troops on its territory. "I believe there will be none in the future," he said, aiming to defuse worries about potential Indo-Russian military cooperation and drawing a political boundary around the partnership.

## Security anxieties under a non-aligned tradition

Early in 2025, rumours that Russia might deploy military aircraft on Biak alarmed Australia; Tolchenov dismissed the claim as "extremely absurd." Yet the unease has not fully dissipated. A foreign-assisted facility near the equator, usable for satellite launches, carries inherent dual-use character. Whether Indonesia uses it to launch military satellites is Jakarta's decision, not Moscow's — itself a sovereign statement under the non-aligned banner.

## Energy cooperation first, with a Western 'secondary sanctions' shadow

Beyond space, Indo-Russian energy ties draw more Western attention. Energy minister Bahlil said in early August that the government is drafting a long-term contract to import Russian oil; prompted by the energy crisis from the US–Iran war, Indonesia has decided to buy an additional 150 million barrels of Russian crude. Tolchenov noted bluntly that every move by the two countries is under strict Western surveillance and could trigger "secondary sanctions." Jakarta insists how it meets its own energy needs "is no business of other countries."

## A small power's great-power balancing act

Stacking space, energy and a refusal to take sides, Indonesia is practising a classic middle-power balancing act: using Russian technology to close capability gaps while drawing red lines through non-alignment; neither letting Western sanctions dictate policy nor becoming any great power's forward base. If a rocket truly lifts off above Biak, it will illuminate another geopolitical contour of Southeast Asia.

## Further reading

**Non-alignment**: A diplomatic principle that emerged during the Cold War advocating non-membership in great-power military blocs; Indonesia was a founder of the Bandung Conference and the Non-Aligned Movement, a tradition still shaping its security policy.

**Commercial Space**: Space activity led by private firms and market demand, spanning launch services, satellite manufacturing and data applications, and reshaping the global space industry.

**Secondary Sanctions**: Penalties a sanctioning state imposes on third parties that conduct normal business with the sanctioned target, often used as a tool of extended jurisdiction.
