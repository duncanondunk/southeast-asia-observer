# 当高温成为"隐形工伤"：东南亚户外劳动者为何在保护网之外

据《南华早报》9月初的报道，一项研究指出，许多东南亚城市缺乏足够的高温风险评估，保护户外劳动者的法规相当薄弱。这句话读起来平平无奇，却戳破了一个被增长叙事长期遮蔽的真相：在号称"世界上最炎热地区之一"的东南亚，数以千万计的户外劳动者，正在没有任何防护网的情况下，独自承担气候变暖最原始、也最不被统计的那笔代价。

## 现场：当高温成为"隐形工伤"

在雅加达的工地、在胡志明市的快递站点、在湄南河畔的码头，高温从不是一起"事故"，而是一种缓慢、分散、不被计入台账的"工伤"。建筑工在正午的钢筋上烫出手印，农人在焦土般的稻田里弯着腰，平台骑手在柏油路上连轴转运——他们不会出现在任何一起"安全生产事故"的通报里，却实实在在地被温度啃噬着身体。与坍塌、火灾不同，热伤害没有现场、没有目击证人，它只是让人"慢慢撑不住"。这正是它最危险的地方：制度看不见它，统计也装不下它。

## 数字：被低估的致命温度

别被"气温三十八度"这种日常表述骗了。真正决定人能否扛住的，是"湿球温度"（人体在湿热环境中散热能力的指标），学界普遍认为约 35°C 湿球已逼近人类持续生存的生理上限。而东南亚近年正在把这条红线一次次逼到眼前：2023 年，老挝录得 44.2°C 的全国最高气温，泰国多地突破 45°C，越南达到 44.1°C，菲律宾的"体感温度"更一度超过 53°C。国际劳工组织（International Labour Organization）估算，到 2030 年，全球因高温造成的劳动生产力损失或达 2.4 万亿美元（约 17 万亿元人民币），其中亚太占比最大。换句话说，热不是"天气问题"，而是一本正在被悄悄透支的账。

## 缺口：城市有预案，工人没有

有意思的是，不少东南亚城市其实并不缺"高温应对"。气象部门会发高温预警，市政会提醒老人少出门，商场和写字楼早早开了冷气。可一旦把镜头移到脚手架上、田埂边、配送箱后，针对劳动者的"职业健康"规定就稀薄得可怜，执行更是近乎空白。前述研究点名的"缺乏足够的高温风险评估"，说的正是这道制度缺口：城市把自己保护得很好，却把最该保护的人留在了网外。预案写给市民看，代价留给工人扛——这是一种安静的不公平。

## 谁在裸奔：非正规就业的大军

为什么偏偏是这些人？答案藏在就业结构里。在东南亚，农业与非正规部门合计吸纳了约三成到一半的劳动力：他们是自雇的小贩、没有合同的日结工、缺了社保的农人。没有劳动合同，就没有"高温津贴"的索取权；没有社保，就连中暑进医院都要自己掏钱。当热浪来袭，正式部门的白领可以躲进空调房，而他们只能硬扛——不是因为更能耐热，而是因为退无可退。热风险从来不是均匀分布的，它精准地落在最没有议价能力的那群人身上。

## 政策悖论：增长叙事下的沉默成本

区域层面的故事很好看：越南制造业迁入、泰国旅游回暖、印尼基建狂奔，国内生产总值曲线漂亮得让外界眼热。可高温对生产力、对健康、对基层医疗系统的侵蚀，是一笔典型的"外部性"——它不进国内生产总值，也不进财政部的账本。政府算增长时把这笔成本外置，企业在报利润时把它忽略，于是"热"成了所有人都在承受、却没人认领的损失。当一座城市为数据中心、为工业园区争抢电力时，是否也该为在烈日下扛电的劳动者，预留一笔"耐热"的预算？

## 他山之石：从"高温津贴"到"湿球红线"

办法不是没有。泰国早在 2018 年就出台了涉及职业场所高温健康的部长级规定；卡塔尔在世界杯前后，把建筑工地的高温作息与"湿球温度"监测推到了全球视野；印度部分邦也已在极端高温时段强制停工。可借鉴的路径很清楚：把"湿球温度"设成一条不能碰的"红线"，超线即强制休息与补水，并把"高温津贴"从企业自愿变成法律强制。技术不复杂，难的是愿不愿意把人放在利润前面。

## 区域镜鉴：东南亚能否先立规矩

东盟擅长谈贸易、谈互联互通，却很少谈"人的耐热底线"。一个值得追问的方向是：既然高温是跨境的、统一的区域气候压力，能否由东盟牵头，推一份面向户外劳动者的热保护共同标准？尤其对快递、外卖等平台经济，算法把配送密度越压越紧，平台就该为"准时"之外的"安全温度"担起责任。区域一体化若只止于货物与资本，却漏掉了最普通的劳动者，那它终究是跛脚的。

## 结论：给高温一个价格

说到底，只要高温的成本不被"定价"、不被内部化，就不会有人真正去防暑。把热伤害写进职业健康、写进社保、写进劳动合同，不是给增长的"添乱"，而是让那笔一直被外置的账显形。当高温成为"隐形工伤"，我们要做的，是让它现形——先有网，才有安全感。

## 延伸阅读

**湿球温度（Wet-bulb temperature）**：衡量人体在湿热环境中散热能力的指标，约 35°C 被视为人类持续生存的生理上限，是评估热风险的核心变量。

**高温津贴（Heat allowance）**：部分国家向户外劳动者发放的季节性补贴，东南亚多数经济体尚未普遍立法强制，多停留在企业自愿层面。

**非正规就业（Informality）**：未签劳动合同、缺乏社会保障的就业形态，在东南亚农业与服务业中占比极高，是热风险中最脆弱的群体。

# When Heat Becomes an 'Invisible Injury': Why Southeast Asia's Outdoor Workers Fall Outside the Safety Net

A recent South China Morning Post report, citing a study, notes that many Southeast Asian cities lack adequate heat-risk assessments and that rules protecting outdoor workers are weak. The sentence sounds mundane, yet it punctures a truth long obscured by the region's growth narrative: in one of the hottest regions on earth, tens of millions of outdoor labourers are absorbing the most primal, and the least counted, cost of warming — with no safety net beneath them.

## The scene: when heat becomes an 'invisible injury'

On a Jakarta construction site, at a Ho Chi Minh City delivery hub, on the docks along the Chao Phraya — heat is never a single "accident." It is a slow, diffuse, unlogged "injury." A builder burns his palms on midday rebar; a farmer bends over cracked paddy soil; a rider runs deliveries across asphalt for hours. None of them appear in any workplace-safety bulletin, yet their bodies are quietly eroded by temperature. Unlike a collapse or a fire, heat leaves no scene and no witnesses — it simply makes people "gradually give out." That is precisely its danger: the system cannot see it, and the statistics cannot hold it.

## The numbers: the underestimated lethal temperature

Do not be lulled by the everyday phrase "38 degrees." What decides whether a body can cope is the wet-bulb temperature — a measure of how well a person can shed heat in hot, humid air — and around 35°C wet-bulb is widely treated as the physiological ceiling for sustained survival. Southeast Asia keeps pushing that line into view: in 2023 Laos recorded a national high of 44.2°C, Thailand topped 45°C in several provinces, Vietnam reached 44.1°C, and the Philippines reported a heat index above 53°C. The International Labour Organization estimates that by 2030, heat stress could cost the world some US$2.4 trillion (~S$3.1 billion equivalent in regional terms, roughly US$2.4tn globally) in lost labour productivity, with Asia-Pacific the largest share. Heat, in other words, is not "weather." It is a ledger being quietly overdrafted.

## The gap: cities have plans, workers do not

Tellingly, many Southeast Asian cities are not short of "heat response." Meteorological agencies issue warnings; municipalities urge the elderly to stay indoors; malls and offices switch on the air-conditioning early. But shift the camera to the scaffold, the rice field, the delivery box, and the occupational-health provisions for workers grow thin — and enforcement thinner still. The study's named failure, "insufficient heat-risk assessment," is exactly this institutional gap: the city protects itself well, yet leaves the people who most need protection outside the net. The plan is written for citizens to read; the cost is left for workers to carry. That is a quiet unfairness.

## Who is exposed: the army of informal workers

Why these people in particular? The answer sits in the employment structure. Across Southeast Asia, agriculture and the informal sector together absorb roughly a third to a half of the labour force: self-employed vendors, day-labourers without contracts, farmers without social security. No contract means no entitlement to a heat allowance; no social security means even a hospital visit for heatstroke comes out of their own pocket. When a heatwave hits, formal-sector employees retreat to air-conditioned rooms; the others simply endure — not because they tolerate heat better, but because they have nowhere to retreat. Heat risk is never evenly distributed; it lands, with precision, on those with the least bargaining power.

## The policy paradox: the silent cost beneath the growth story

The regional story looks good: manufacturers relocating to Vietnam, Thai tourism recovering, Indonesian infrastructure surging — GDP curves handsome enough to turn heads. Yet heat's erosion of productivity, health, and primary-care systems is a classic externality: it does not enter GDP, nor the finance ministry's books. Governments externalise the cost when they count growth; firms ignore it when they report profit. Heat becomes a loss everyone bears but no one claims. When a city competes for power for data centres and industrial parks, should it not also reserve a "heat-resilience" budget for the labourers who keep the current flowing under the sun?

## Lessons elsewhere: from 'heat allowances' to 'wet-bulb red lines'

The tools exist. Thailand introduced ministerial rules on heat in the workplace as early as 2018; after the World Cup, Qatar pushed construction heat schedules and wet-bulb monitoring into the global spotlight; several Indian states mandate stoppages during extreme-heat windows. The borrowable path is clear: set the wet-bulb temperature as a hard red line that triggers mandatory rest and hydration, and turn the heat allowance from corporate voluntarism into legal compulsion. The technology is trivial; the difficulty is whether one is willing to put people before profit.

## A regional mirror: can Southeast Asia set the rules first

ASEAN is adept at talking trade and connectivity, but rarely about "the human baseline for heat." A question worth asking: since heat is a cross-border, region-wide climate pressure, could ASEAN lead a common heat-protection standard for outdoor workers? Especially for platform economies — delivery and ride-hailing — where algorithms compress dispatch density ever tighter, the platforms should bear responsibility for a "safe temperature" beyond "on-time." If regional integration stops at goods and capital while omitting the most ordinary workers, it remains lame.

## Conclusion: put a price on heat

In the end, so long as the cost of heat is neither priced nor internalised, no one will truly prevent it. Writing heat injury into occupational health, into social security, into labour contracts is not "disrupting growth" — it makes a cost long externalised finally visible. When heat becomes an invisible injury, our job is to make it visible: only with a net comes security.

## Further reading

**Wet-bulb temperature**: A measure of the human body's ability to shed heat in hot, humid air; around 35°C is treated as the physiological ceiling for sustained survival and is the core variable in heat-risk assessment.

**Heat allowance**: A seasonal subsidy some countries pay outdoor workers; most Southeast Asian economies have not made it broadly mandatory by law and largely leave it to corporate voluntarism.

**Informality**: Employment without a labour contract and without social protection; it accounts for a very high share of agriculture and services in Southeast Asia and is the most heat-vulnerable group.
