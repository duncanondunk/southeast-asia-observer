# 东南亚抬高中国电动车门槛：从"欢迎"到"落地建厂"的第二阶段

9月4日前后，马来西亚、泰国等东南亚国家相继收紧中国产纯电动汽车的进口条件、提高整车进口门槛与税负，并要求车企增加本地生产与零部件采购。罗兰贝格的数据显示，2025年中国品牌电动车约占东南亚九成份额。政策拐点的含义很清楚：各国正从"欢迎消费"阶段，转向"招商引资、发展本土产业"的第二阶段。对中国车企而言，整车出口的逻辑必须让位于本地建厂与供应链深耕。

## 九成份额背后的"甜蜜负担"

中国电动车在东南亚的扩张速度超出许多人的预期。据观察者网等媒体援引罗兰贝格数据，2025年中国EV占东南亚约九成份额。规模优势带来价格竞争力，也带来"一家独大"的观感。对渴望发展本土汽车工业、又希望拥抱电动化转型的东南亚国家而言，这既是机遇，也是需要主动管理的结构性压力。

## 从"欢迎"到"设门槛"

政策转向并不突然。过去数年，东南亚多国以关税优惠、进口便利吸引中国电动车进入，培育了消费与市场。如今马来西亚、泰国等开始提高整车进口条件、加征税负，并把"本地生产、本地采购"设为硬约束。这标志着政策进入第二阶段：不再只求把车卖进来，而是要求产业落下来。

## 东盟内部的差异化节奏

各国步调并不一致。泰国长期是中国电动车出海重镇，本地化政策更系统；马来西亚则在保护本土组装与零部件体系上更积极。差异背后是各自的产业基础与谈判筹码：越早建立本地供应链的国家，越有底气提条件。对中国车企而言，这意味着"一国一策"的本地化方案将取代统一出口打法。

## 中国车企的"第二曲线"

门槛抬高并非终局，而是倒逼升级。此前中国品牌多以整车出口加贸易商渠道快速铺货，如今必须把产能、研发与供应链搬到当地。比亚迪、上汽、长城等已在泰国布局工厂，正从"卖车"转向"建生态"。谁先把本地化做成体系能力，谁就能在下一阶段锁定份额。

## 供应链的"在地化"考题

本地化不只是建厂。电池、电机、电控以及充电网络，都需要在地配套。对中国零部件企业而言，跟随整车厂出海、在东南亚设厂，既是为客户配套，也是规避贸易壁垒的现实选择。区域供应链由此从"中国生产、全球销售"向"区域生产、区域销售"迁移，这与全球产业链区域化的大趋势同频。

## 招商竞争的暗线

东南亚国家并非只对中国设限，也在向全球车企招商。美国、日本、韩国与欧洲品牌同样被邀请本地建厂。对中国车企而言，竞争对象不再只是其他中国品牌，而是所有寻求东南亚生产基地的国际玩家。谁能提供更完整的本地就业、技术与税收贡献，谁就能拿到更友好的政策。

## 不必过度解读为"脱钩"

把门槛升高读作"排华"或"脱钩"都失之简单。这本质上是后发国家工业化的标准动作：用市场换技术、用准入换投资。当年中国入世后也曾以合资与本地化要求培育本土汽车业。东南亚今天的做法，是从"消费国"向"生产国"跃迁的必经之坎，方向是互利而非对立。

## 给中国企业的长期提示

对中国电动车产业而言，真正的考验不是某一国关税高低，而是能否把"成本优势"升级为"体系优势"。整车出口的窗口期正在关闭，经营门槛在抬高，品牌需要从铺货逻辑转向长期主义：深耕本地供应链、培养本地人才、融入区域标准。谁能完成这一步，谁就能在东南亚电动化浪潮中真正留下来。

## 延伸阅读

**本地化率（Local Content Requirement）**：东道国要求外资企业在产品中达到一定比例的本地零部件或生产份额，是产业保护与发展常用的政策工具。

**中国加一（China Plus One）**：跨国企业为分散风险，在原有中国产能之外另设生产基地的策略，东南亚是主要承接方之一。

**东盟电网（ASEAN Power Grid）**：区域能源一体化倡议，与中国电动车在东南亚的充电网络布局存在协同空间。

# Southeast Asia Raises the Bar for Chinese EVs: From "Welcome" to "Build Here," Phase Two

Around 4 September, Southeast Asian countries including Malaysia and Thailand tightened import conditions on Chinese-made battery electric vehicles, raised barriers and taxes on fully built units, and required automakers to increase local production and parts procurement. Roland Berger data cited in Chinese media put Chinese-brand EVs at roughly 90% of Southeast Asia's market in 2025. The policy pivot is clear: countries are moving from a "welcome consumption" stage to a second stage of "attract investment, grow local industry." For Chinese automakers, the logic of finished-vehicle export must give way to local plants and deep supply chains.

## The "sweet burden" behind a 90% share

Chinese EVs expanded across Southeast Asia faster than many expected. Roland Berger figures cited by outlets including Guancha show Chinese EVs at about 90% of the regional market in 2025. Scale brings price competitiveness and also the perception of dominance. For Southeast Asian states eager to build their own auto industries while embracing electrification, this is both an opportunity and a structural pressure to be actively managed.

## From "welcome" to "set barriers"

The turn was not abrupt. For years, several Southeast Asian countries used tariff breaks and import facilitation to draw Chinese EVs in, cultivating consumption and markets. Now Malaysia and Thailand are raising fully built-unit conditions, adding taxes, and making "local production, local sourcing" a hard constraint. This marks phase two: no longer merely selling cars in, but demanding the industry land.

## Divergent rhythms within ASEAN

The pace is not uniform. Thailand, long a major destination for Chinese EV exports, has a more systematic localisation policy; Malaysia is more active in protecting its local assembly and components base. Behind the difference lie each country's industrial foundation and bargaining chips: the earlier a state builds local supply chains, the more confidence it has to set terms. For Chinese automakers, "one policy per country" localisation will replace a uniform export playbook.

## Chinese automakers' "second curve"

Higher barriers are not an endpoint but a forced upgrade. Previously, Chinese brands rushed volume through finished-vehicle export and trading channels; now they must move capacity, R&D and supply chains onshore. BYD, SAIC and Great Wall have already built plants in Thailand, shifting from "selling cars" to "building ecosystems." Whoever turns localisation into systemic capability first locks in share for the next phase.

## The "localisation" test for supply chains

Localisation is more than building a plant. Batteries, motors, electronic controls and charging networks all need local matching. For Chinese component firms, following OEMs offshore and setting up in Southeast Asia both serves customers and hedges trade barriers. Regional supply chains thus migrate from "China produces, world buys" toward "region produces, region buys," in tune with the broader trend of supply-chain regionalisation.

## The subtext of investment competition

Southeast Asian states are not restricting only China; they are courting global automakers too. American, Japanese, Korean and European brands are equally invited to build locally. For Chinese automakers, the competitor is no longer only other Chinese brands but all international players seeking Southeast Asian bases. Whoever offers fuller local employment, technology and tax contribution wins friendlier policy.

## Not "decoupling," read with care

Reading higher barriers as "anti-China" or "decoupling" is too simple. This is essentially the standard move of late-industrialising states: trading market for technology, access for investment. China itself, after WTO entry, used joint-venture and localisation requirements to nurture its auto sector. Southeast Asia's move today is the necessary threshold from "consumer" to "producer" — a direction of mutual benefit, not confrontation.

## A long-term note for Chinese firms

For China's EV industry, the real test is not any single country's tariff level but whether "cost advantage" can be upgraded to "system advantage." The window for finished-vehicle export is closing and the operating bar is rising; brands must shift from volume logic to long-termism: deepen local supply chains, cultivate local talent, fit regional standards. Whoever completes this step truly stays in Southeast Asia's electrification wave.

## Further reading

**Local Content Requirement**: A host-country policy requiring foreign investors to reach a certain share of local parts or production, a common tool for industrial protection and development.

**China Plus One**: A strategy where multinationals add production bases beyond China to diversify risk; Southeast Asia is a major recipient.

**ASEAN Power Grid**: A regional energy-integration initiative with synergy potential for Chinese EV charging-network layouts in Southeast Asia.
