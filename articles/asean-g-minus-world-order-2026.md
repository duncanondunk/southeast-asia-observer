# 没有霸权的世界如何运转？东盟（ASEAN）给出的「无霸权秩序」答案

据《南华早报》（South China Morning Post）报道，新加坡国立大学李光耀公共政策学院前院长、经济学教授丹尼·夸（Danny Quah）提出一个听起来很抽象、落点却极其务实的概念：这个世界需要一套「无霸权秩序（G-minus world）」——即便没有大国点头，甚至有大国阻挠，国际规则、规范与机构照样能够运转。他说，过去80年各国在主权框架下生活，靠的是一个由美国建立并长期充当「仁慈霸主」的架构；如今这种领导已经不再可指望。于是问题变成：能不能降低世界秩序对大国喜怒的敏感度，让其余国家在大国行为失常时仍能正常做生意、解决争端、应对共同挑战？

这是个经济学者的提问，答案却写在东盟的履历里。夸的判断是，东盟「几乎已经拥有」这样一套架构：10个、现在是11个差异极大的国家，60年来一直在没有谁发号施令的情况下合作。值得我们追问的是：这种「没有领导者」究竟是能力，还是只是尚未暴露的脆弱？东盟的经验能不能被当作区域秩序的模板，还是只能算一次特定的历史侥幸？

## 概念：没有守门人的规则

「无霸权秩序」的要点不是反美，也不是反华，而是反「许可依赖」。如果一个多边安排必须取得某个大国的同意才能启动、才能续命，那么这套安排的稳定性就绑在了大国的国内政治周期上：换一届政府，改一次选举结果，规则就可能转向。夸把它表述为降低「世界秩序的大国弹性」——大国的行为变化越小程度地传导到全球规则，其他国家就越有安全感。

这里有一个容易被忽略的区分：无霸权不等于无规则。恰恰相反，它要求更细、更可操作、更不依赖政治姿态的规则。东盟的方式是把议题拆小，先做能做的：关税减让、原产地认定、通关程序、灾害通报。每一步都不需要对「谁来领导亚洲」这个问题表态。据《南华早报》报道，夸把这套做法称为「无意中形成的合作」——不是谁画好了蓝图，而是各国在规则里发现彼此可以让利、可以对接，于是越做越深。

这种路径的优点是抗俘获能力强：没有一面共同的旗帜可以被外部力量一举拿下。缺点是慢，遇到需要一次性决断的危机时，共识机制往往显得迟钝。看清这一点，才不至于把东盟经验浪漫化。

## 样本：东盟为何接近这种架构

东盟的11个成员里有岛国、内陆国、海洋争议方、不同政治体制和差异极大的发展阶段。按常理，这样的组合最容易被大国分化：谁需要投资，谁需要安全保护，谁就有理由向一方倾斜。但东盟60年来形成的工作习惯是，把分歧留在议题之外，把可操作的部分放在桌面上。夸所说的「几乎拥有」，指的正是这种程序性资产，而不是东盟已经有一套成文的世界政府宪章。

值得注意的是，东盟的运作并不靠超国家机构强制命令。它缺少类似欧盟委员会那样的中枢，也缺少可执行的制裁工具。它的约束力来自反复开会、反复确认、反复把双边摩擦翻译成区域议程。这种弱制度在风平浪静时显得效率低下，在强权施压时反而有用：没有单一入口可供施压者锁定，每个成员都能用自己的方式维持对外接触。

《南华早报》的访谈把这一点讲得很清楚：东盟不是被迫在华盛顿与北京（Beijing）之间选边，而是把「不选边」变成一种可重复的生产方式。这个说法值得点评：不选边从来不是免费的，它需要各国持续投入外交成本，也需要外部大国暂时容忍模糊空间。一旦有人决意要把模糊变成明确，成本就会骤然上升。

## 原产地规则：意外长成的一体化

这套架构最实在的成果，藏在不起眼的技术条款里。夸举例说，中国的半导体技术进入马来西亚（Malaysia）、新加坡和泰国（Thailand），经过组装与再加工，变成更高价值的电子制成品出口全球。这不是转口贸易的障眼法，而是在多个成员国境内真刀真枪增加了价值。由于区域累积机制允许把不同环节计入原产地认定，最终产品可以被识别为「东盟制造」，而不是「中国绕道」。

这套链条让美国针对转运的关税工具变得难以施展：要指控转运，就得证明增值主要没有发生；但当零部件在泰国加工、再到马来西亚组装、最后从印尼（Indonesia）出口时，所谓「中国产地」的判定本身就失去抓手。据该报道，美国在2024年占东盟出口的16%，夸认为现在这个比例可能更高。美国仍是大市场，也是新加坡等地外国直接投资的重量级来源；正因如此，东盟供应链不是「脱钩」的产物，而是把相互依赖拆散到多个节点，让任何单一节点都不至于成为致命开关。

但技术条款也有边界。增值可以累积，治理能力不能自动累积。劳工标准、环保责任、数据合规仍然是各国各自执行；如果某一环出现强制劳动或污染丑闻，整条链的声誉会一起受损。原产地的账算得清，责任的账常常算不清。

## 谁在主导区域全面经济伙伴关系协定

访谈里有一句需要被反复咀嚼的话：许多人以为《区域全面经济伙伴关系协定》是中国主导的集团，其实不是。夸的判断是，它是东盟发起、碰巧把中国、日本与韩国都包含进来的安排。这个区分不只是面子问题。若由一个大国主导，那些与中国关系紧张的国家加入时会有完全不同的心理成本；正因为是东盟自己搭台，成员才更容易把参与解释成区域规则而非站队行为。

这正是「无霸权」在最具体意义上的价值：领导权的功能被分散给了程序。谁都可以提案，但没有人能够单方面定义协定的政治含义。协定里的原产地累积、版权与电子商务条款，也不是任何一方的专属战利品。

不过也要看到另一面：东盟主导不等于东盟说了算。协定之所以能谈成，同样因为主要经济体都判断参与比退出划算。一旦某个大国认定规则不再有利于己，所谓东盟中心性就会立刻受到压力测试。程序性权威能延缓冲击，却不能替代实力对比。

## 中国冲击：为什么在东南亚（Southeast Asia）听着不同

欧美关于「中国冲击」的叙事通常只有两个主角：受损的本国产业与受益的消费者。夸指出，亚洲还有第三个角色——下游生产者。它们用中国提供的、质量不低而价格可承受的投入品，把自己的产品做得更便宜、更好。非前沿半导体、电动汽车电池、太阳能板与风机，都嵌进了东南亚的电力与制造体系。在这个结构里，把中国投入品简单等同为威胁，等于否认本地厂商的竞争力来源。

他不否认反例。印尼产业界就有对中国冲击的抱怨，这类声音应当被认真对待，而不是用「东南亚整体受益」一句话抹平。与此同时，他也提醒西方：中国加入世界贸易组织后经历过自己的冲击，东北国有企业部门有大量工人失业；黑龙江的企业家讲出的入世故事，与外界想象的中国截然不同。这个对照让「竞争有益」的说教失去了单向道德优势。

真正的问题因而不是「要不要竞争」，而是「竞争之后如何安置失败者」。如果一套秩序只允许别人承受创造性破坏，却在本国用补贴和壁垒抵消同一逻辑，它就会失去说服力。夸的批评正落在这里。

## 双重标准：把竞争教义讲给自己听

据《南华早报》报道，夸认为美国的对华紧张是跨党派的：传统共和党人追求美国优先地位，民主党人反感中国的威权体制，特朗普阵营则从经济层面反对中国，因为其选民把失业归咎于外部。欧洲也面临类似的产业竞争压力。但在南美、非洲和亚洲大部分地区，中国商品意味着可负担的质量；这些国家并不富裕，没有能力让价格普遍上升，因此并不乐于接受「保护本国产业」的普世化说辞。

这段分析的价值在于把争论从道德标签拉回利益结构。东南亚国家并不是在「价值」与「利益」之间做选择，它们面对的是更朴素的问题：电费、零部件成本、工厂订单与就业。谁能让这些数字变得可控，谁就获得政策空间。无霸权秩序之所以有吸引力，正是因为它允许多重归属：可以在安全上靠近一方，在供应链上靠近另一方，而不必把每笔交易都升格为立场宣言。

但也请不要把务实误读为没有原则。多重归属需要最低限度的可预期性：规则不能今天允许、明天禁止。而恰恰在这一点上，大国竞争的当前阶段正在恶化。

## 天花板：无霸权架构做不到什么

夸自己也说，「无霸权秩序」目前仍是一种愿望，东盟只是「几乎」具备。这个限定词极其重要。东盟没有共同军队，没有统一财政，没有强制履约机制；南海（South China Sea）行为准则谈了多年仍卡在适用范围与法律约束力上；缅甸问题暴露出「不干涉内政」与共同底线之间的张力。一个靠共识运转的机构，能阻止最坏情况，却很难主动塑造最好结果。

此外，这套架构的外部条件正在变薄。美国市场若进一步收紧原产地审查，中国若对第三方转口施压，东盟的转圜空间都会缩小；美元体系仍然主导结算与流动性，人民币在本区域的使用虽在扩展，却远未改变格局。规则可以分散，清算与信用仍然集中。

因此，正确的结论不是「东盟模式胜利了」，而是「东盟模式仍值得守住」。在一个大国越来越倾向于把经济工具政治化的时代，保留一块不需要每笔交易都先获得许可的区域空间，本身就有价值。无霸权秩序不是终点，而是一道防止世界秩序彻底私有化的堤坝。堤坝不漂亮，也挡不住所有洪水，但拆掉它，代价会由最没有议价能力的国家先付。

## 延伸阅读

**无霸权秩序（G-minus world）**：指国际规则与机构在大国不授权、甚至阻挠时仍能运转的设想，核心是降低世界秩序对单一大国行为的敏感度。

**原产地累积规则（rules of origin cumulation）**：允许把多个成员境内的加工增值计入同一产品的原产地判定，使跨国分工形成的商品被识别为区域产品。

**创造性破坏（creative destruction）**：竞争迫使落后产能退出的市场逻辑；当同样逻辑落到本国产业时，发达经济体往往转而强调安全与补贴。

# How does the world work without a hegemon? ASEAN's "G-minus" answer

According to the *South China Morning Post*, Danny Quah, an economics professor at the National University of Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew School of Public Policy and its former dean, has given a name to an idea that sounds abstract but lands very concretely: the world needs a "G-minus" order, in which global rules, norms and institutions work properly even without great-power permission — perhaps even against great-power obstruction. For eight decades, he notes, countries maintained sovereignty under an architecture built and underwritten by the United States, a great power that was largely a benevolent hegemon. That leadership can no longer be assumed. The question now is whether the rest of the world can reduce the "great-power elasticity" of world order: whether ordinary states can keep trading, settling disputes and answering common challenges even when a major power behaves unpredictably.

This is an economist's question, but the most developed answer is written in ASEAN's biography. Quah argues that ASEAN "almost has" such an architecture: ten, now eleven, very different states cooperating for six decades without anyone telling them what to do. It is worth asking whether that absence of leadership is a capability or an untested vulnerability, and whether the ASEAN experience is a template for regional order or a piece of historical luck that should not be generalised too quickly.

## The idea: rules without a gatekeeper

The point of a G-minus order is not anti-Americanism or anti-China sentiment. It is opposition to dependency on permission. If a multilateral arrangement needs a great power's approval to start and to survive, its stability is tied to that power's domestic political cycle. One election, one change of administration, and the rules can turn. Quah frames the task as reducing the great-power elasticity of world order: the less that global rules move when a great power moves, the more secure everyone else becomes.

An easy distinction is missed here. G-minus does not mean no rules. It means more precise, more operational rules that do not depend on political posture. ASEAN's method is to break problems into manageable pieces: tariff reductions, origin certification, customs procedures, disaster notifications. None requires taking a position on who leads Asia. According to the SCMP interview, Quah describes this as inadvertent cooperation — no one drew the blueprint; countries simply discovered inside the rules that they could accommodate one another, and kept going.

The advantage is resistance to capture: there is no single banner for an outside power to seize. The disadvantage is slowness, and a consensus machine looks sluggish when a crisis demands one decisive act. Holding both facts at once keeps the ASEAN story from turning into romance.

## Why ASEAN resembles a G-minus system

ASEAN's eleven members include island states, a landlocked state, maritime claimants, divergent political systems and radically different levels of development. By ordinary logic this is a combination primed for great-power division: whoever needs investment or protection has a reason to lean one way. ASEAN's habit instead has been to leave disputes outside the room and put operational items on the table. What Quah calls "almost" having a G-minus architecture is a procedural asset, not a written constitution for a world government.

It is also worth noting that ASEAN does not run on supranational command. It has no commission with EU-style authority and no robust enforcement toolbox. Its binding force comes from meeting repeatedly, confirming repeatedly, and translating bilateral friction into a regional agenda repeatedly. Weak institutions look inefficient in calm weather; under pressure they have a different utility. With no single entry point, a coercer has difficulty locking down the whole grouping, and each member can maintain its own external contacts.

The SCMP interview puts it plainly: ASEAN is not being forced to choose between Washington and Beijing; it has turned non-alignment into a repeatable way of producing outcomes. A brief comment is in order. Non-alignment is never free. It requires sustained diplomatic investment and a degree of tolerance for ambiguity from great powers. Once someone decides that ambiguity must be resolved, the costs rise sharply.

## Rules of origin: integration by accident

The most tangible achievement of this architecture sits inside unglamorous technical clauses. Quah points to Chinese semiconductor technology entering Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand for assembly and further processing, then leaving as higher-value electronics. This is not sleight-of-hand transhipment; value is genuinely added inside several members. Because regional cumulation allows those stages to count toward origin, the final product is legibly "made in ASEAN" rather than "China, via somewhere else".

That chain makes American anti-transhipment tools awkward to deploy. To prove circumvention, investigators must show that little value was added; but when components are processed in Thailand, assembled in Malaysia and exported from Indonesia, the very category of "Chinese origin" loses its grip. According to the report, the United States accounted for 16 per cent of ASEAN exports in 2024, a share Quah suspects is higher now. America remains a large market and a major source of foreign direct investment in places such as Singapore. ASEAN supply chains are therefore not a product of decoupling; they distribute interdependence across nodes so that no single node becomes an off switch.

Technical clauses have limits, however. Value can be cumulated; governance capacity cannot be assumed to accumulate with it. Labour standards, environmental responsibility and data compliance remain national tasks. If one link is implicated in forced labour or pollution, the whole chain absorbs the reputational damage. Origin accounts are easier to balance than responsibility accounts.

## Who actually leads RCEP

One sentence in the interview deserves to be read twice. Many people, Quah says, think RCEP is a China-led grouping; it is not. It is an ASEAN-initiated arrangement that happens to include China, Japan and South Korea. This is not a matter of face. If a single great power had led the talks, countries with uneasy relations with China would have paid a very different domestic political price to join. Because ASEAN built the table, participation can be read as adherence to regional rules rather than as a declaration of alignment.

That is the concrete value of G-minus: the function of leadership is dispersed into procedure. Anyone can table a proposal, but no one can unilaterally define what the agreement politically means. Cumulation rules, intellectual-property provisions and e-commerce chapters are not the exclusive spoils of one side.

The reverse side deserves attention too. ASEAN leadership does not mean ASEAN dictates outcomes. The agreement was possible because major economies judged participation preferable to exit. Should a great power decide the rules no longer serve it, ASEAN centrality will face an immediate stress test. Procedural authority can delay an impact; it cannot substitute for material weight.

## Why the China shock sounds different here

Western accounts of the "China shock" usually have two characters: injured domestic industries and benefiting consumers. Quah identifies a third in Asia — downstream producers that use cheap, sufficiently good Chinese inputs to make their own output cheaper and better. Non-frontier semiconductors, electric batteries, solar panels and wind turbines are embedded in Southeast Asia's manufacturing and power systems. In that structure, treating Chinese inputs as a purely hostile force amounts to denying the source of local competitiveness.

He does not deny counter-examples. Indonesian industry has voiced complaints about the China shock, and those voices deserve engagement rather than being erased by a sentence about aggregate regional benefit. He also reminds Western readers that China experienced its own shock after joining the WTO, with large layoffs in the north-eastern state sector. Industrialists in Heilongjiang tell a story of accession unlike the one the outside world imagines. The comparison strips the advocacy of competition of its one-way moral advantage.

The real question is therefore not whether competition should exist, but how societies treat those who lose under it. An order that requires others to absorb creative destruction while shielding its own industries with subsidies and barriers will struggle to persuade anyone. That is precisely where Quah's critique lands.

## The double standard Western economists should hear

According to the SCMP, Quah sees American tensions with China as bipartisan: traditional Republicans want US primacy, Democrats object to Chinese authoritarianism, and the Trump administration opposes China economically because its base blames outsiders for job losses. Europe faces parallel industrial pressures. But in South America, Africa and much of Asia, Chinese goods mean affordable quality. These countries are not rich enough to welcome higher prices across the board, and they are unimpressed by pleas to protect domestic industry as a universal virtue.

The value of this argument is that it moves the debate from moral labels back to material structure. Southeast Asian governments are not choosing between "values" and "interests" in the abstract. They are asking plainer questions about electricity bills, component costs, factory orders and jobs. Whoever makes those numbers manageable gains policy space. The appeal of a G-minus order is that it permits multiple affiliations: a country can lean toward one partner on security and another in its supply chains without promoting every transaction into a statement of allegiance.

Pragmatism should not be mistaken for an absence of principle, though. Multiple affiliations need a minimum of predictability: rules cannot be permitted one day and prohibited the next. It is exactly here that the current phase of great-power competition is making matters worse.

## The ceiling: what G-minus cannot do

Quah himself says G-minus remains an aspiration and that ASEAN only "almost" has it. That qualifier matters enormously. ASEAN has no common army, no shared budget and no strong compliance mechanism. The South China Sea code of conduct has stalled for years over scope and legal bindingness. Myanmar exposes the tension between non-interference and any notion of shared red lines. A body that runs on consensus can prevent the worst outcome, but it cannot reliably produce the best one.

Nor are the external conditions improving. Tighter American scrutiny of origin claims or Chinese pressure on third-country processing would both shrink ASEAN's room to manoeuvre. Dollar settlement and liquidity still dominate; yuan use in the region is growing but nowhere near changing that picture. Rules can be dispersed; clearing and credit remain concentrated.

The right conclusion, then, is not "the ASEAN model has won" but "the ASEAN model is worth defending". At a moment when great powers increasingly politicise economic instruments, preserving a regional space where not every transaction needs prior permission has value in itself. G-minus is not a destination. It is a dyke against the privatisation of world order. It is not beautiful and it will not stop every flood — but if it is dismantled, the countries with the least bargaining power will be the first to pay.

## Further reading

**G-minus world**: the idea that global rules and institutions should function even without great-power authorisation, reducing world order's sensitivity to the behaviour of any single power.

**Rules of origin cumulation**: counting processing value added in several member economies toward a product's origin, so a cross-border division of labour is recognised as a regional good.

**Creative destruction**: the market logic by which competition forces outdated capacity out; when the same logic reaches their own industries, developed economies often shift to talking about security and subsidies.
