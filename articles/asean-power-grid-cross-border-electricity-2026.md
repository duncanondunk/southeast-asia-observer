# 东盟电网为何走得慢：跨境电力交易背后的搁浅资产与调度主权

把老挝的水电送到新加坡，技术上早已不是难题。据公开报道，东盟内部已有跨越数国、以双边购电协议串联的试点项目，证明高压输电与跨境结算可以被组织起来。但把零散的试点连成一张多边电网，进展却始终缓慢。东盟电网作为一个区域构想被讨论了数十年，落地形态仍以一条条双边线路为主。

常见的解释是技术标准与融资缺口，但更根本的症结在制度。电力不是普通商品：它要求实时平衡，依赖本国公用事业的调度，牵动已建电厂的回收周期，也直接关联供电安全这一政治责任。本文的核心判断是：电力互联的障碍不在技术，而在两个无人愿意先行认领的问题——谁承担搁浅资产（stranded asset），谁让渡调度主权。

## 从“东南亚电池”说起：资源与负荷的地理错位

老挝境内河流落差大，水电资源丰富，长期被称作“东南亚电池”；把电力输往邻国，也成为其获取外汇与吸引投资的重要路径。据公开报道，围绕湄公河支流的梯级电站与跨境线路已形成相当规模。与此同时，用电负荷却集中在泰国、越南、马来西亚与新加坡的城市与工业区。资源与需求在地理上并不重合，这正是互联最朴素的经济理由：一方有富余的水电，另一方有稳定且增长的负荷。

但水电并非恒定输出。丰水期与枯水期的落差、季风年份的波动，意味着卖方也需要煤电或燃气机组兜底，买方则必须保留足够的备用容量。于是“卖电”与“保供”从一开始就存在张力：跨境线路输送的不只是电量，还有一份关于枯水期谁来补足的默契。一般而言，水电占比越高的系统，对跨境互济的需求越迫切，对调度协调的要求也越苛刻。

## 双边购电协议与多边市场：两种治理逻辑

现阶段跨境交易主要靠购电协议（power purchase agreement）组织：两国、两家公用事业、一条线路、一份长期合同，约定电量、电价与期限，常含照付不议条款。它的好处是责任清晰——买方知道要付多少钱，卖方知道能收回多少投资，争议可以在双边框架内解决。据公开报道，区域试点也多采取这种点对点结构。双边模式之所以容易落地，是因为它只需要两国对账，不需要任何一方把裁量权交给第三方。

多边市场则是另一套逻辑。它要求共同的市场规则、独立的调度机构、跨境结算与争端机制，还要给容量、辅助服务与输电权定价。这些规则一旦确立，便意味着各国不能再单独决定本国机组何时发电、以什么价格卖给谁。多方观察认为，从双边迈向多边的关键不是再建几条线路，而是各国是否同意被同一套规则约束。前者是工程问题，后者是治理问题。

## 国有公用事业与监管碎片化：谁有权签字

东盟多数国家的电力体制以国有垂直一体化公用事业为骨架，本国公用事业往往同时是唯一或主要购电方，电价、补贴、许可与规划由各自监管框架决定。跨境交易一旦展开，本国公用事业便不再是唯一买家：它要面对来自邻国的报价、跨境的调度指令，以及本国政府并未单独批准的购电成本。这直接触及机构利益与监管权限。

更现实的障碍是规则不兼容。各国电价形成机制不同，有的含交叉补贴，有的与燃料价格挂钩；并网标准、计量口径、备用义务与环评要求也各行其是。据公开报道，区域层面虽有多份互联互通的规划研究，但落地仍需逐国逐项对齐。监管碎片化不是缺文件，而是缺一个能把不同制度翻译成同一套可结算条款的主体。

## 搁浅资产：已建电厂的账没人愿意先认

假定廉价跨境水电大规模进入，进口国本国已建或已签约的燃气、煤电机组利用率就会下降，回收期被拉长，部分机组可能在折旧完成前失去经济价值，成为搁浅资产。问题在于这份账由谁认：公用事业、政府财政、纳税人还是终端用户？长期购电协议与照付不议条款又使退出成本高企，即便机组不再满发，合同义务仍在。

因此进口国对放开市场的谨慎，本质上不是保护落后产能，而是保护既有投资与既有合同的信用。一般而言，任何会改变存量资产现金流的市场设计，都会遭遇来自存量持有者的阻力；电力行业资产重、周期长，这种阻力尤其强。跨境互联的收益是长期且分散的，而搁浅资产的损失是即时且集中的——收益与损失在时间上不对称，谈判自然难以推进。这是东盟电网走得慢最实在的原因。

## 数据中心带来的新增负荷：需求侧的突然加压

近年数据中心、算力设施与电气化在少数城市与园区集中形成新增负荷，且对供电可靠性要求极高。据公开报道，部分区域电网已因此重新评估扩容节奏与进口电力的可行性。新负荷同时给出两个方向相反的信号：一方面，它提供了互联的理由——需要更多电，也更需要低碳电源；另一方面，它加剧了顾虑——把一座数据中心的连续供电托付给跨境线路与邻国调度，是否可被接受。

需求侧的紧迫与制度侧的谨慎，由此形成反差。负荷增长是季度性的，规则协调却是年度性的；资本可以迅速决定建一座园区，却无法迅速决定由谁承担跨境故障时的断供责任。多方观察认为，新增负荷未必自动转化为互联的政治动力，反而可能让各国更倾向先在本国境内补齐可控容量。

## 调度主权：互联真正让渡的东西

把前述几层剥开，互联的终点并不是多几条线路，而是把一部分实时调度权交给跨国的协调机制。频率稳定、备用容量、故障切除、事故时的优先次序，都需要一个能够发号施令的主体；而这个主体要有效，就必然要求各国让渡部分裁量。对把供电安全视作国家责任的政府而言，这是最难签字的一项。技术难题可以外包给工程公司、融资机构与设备供应商，主权与存量资产却无法外包。

所以东盟电网的推进速度，最终取决于能否设计出一整套让搁浅资产有补偿、让调度权有边界、让风险可分担的制度安排，而不取决于输电技术本身。谁承担搁浅资产与调度主权，这两个问题若没有答案，互联互通就只会停留在一条条双边线路与一份份规划文本里。一条跨境线路改变的是电流方向，一套区域规则改变的则是各国之间长期形成的责任分配——后者远比前者更难谈成。
## 延伸阅读

**东盟电网（ASEAN Power Grid）**：以跨境输电线路把东盟各国电力系统逐步互联、形成区域电力市场的长期构想；目前落地形态仍以双边联网与试点项目为主。

**购电协议（Power purchase agreement）**：约定电量、电价与期限的长期售电合同，常含照付不议条款，是当前跨境电力交易最常见的组织方式。

**搁浅资产（Stranded asset）**：因市场或政策变化而提前丧失经济价值、投资无法回收的资产；在电力互联中，指可能受进口电力挤压的本国既有机组。

# Why the ASEAN Power Grid Moves Slowly: Stranded Assets and Dispatch Sovereignty Behind Cross-Border Electricity

Moving hydropower from Laos to Singapore ceased to be a technical problem long ago. Pilot projects already link several ASEAN countries through bilateral power purchase agreements, showing that cross-border transmission and settlement can be organised. Yet joining those scattered pilots into a multilateral grid has been slow. The ASEAN Power Grid has been discussed for decades, but what gets built is still a series of bilateral lines.

The usual explanations cite technical standards and financing gaps; the deeper obstacle is institutional. Electricity is not an ordinary commodity: it must be balanced in real time, depends on dispatch by national utilities, affects the payback of plants already built, and touches supply security, a political responsibility. The barrier to interconnection is not technical but lies in two questions no one wants to answer first: who absorbs the stranded assets, and who gives up dispatch sovereignty.

## The "battery of Southeast Asia": a geography of mismatched supply and demand

Laos has abundant hydropower and is long called the "battery of Southeast Asia"; exporting power has become a key source of foreign exchange and a magnet for investment. According to public reporting, cascaded plants on Mekong tributaries and their cross-border lines have reached considerable scale. Demand, meanwhile, concentrates in the cities and industrial zones of Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia and Singapore. Supply and load do not coincide geographically, which is the plainest case for interconnection: one side has surplus hydropower, the other a growing load.

But hydropower is not constant output. Wet and dry seasons and monsoon variation mean the seller needs coal or gas backup, while the buyer must hold reserve capacity. Selling power and guaranteeing supply are in tension from the start: a cross-border line carries not only electricity but an understanding about who covers a dry-year shortfall. The higher a system's hydropower share, the more it needs cross-border balancing — and the more demanding coordination becomes.

## Bilateral power purchase agreements versus a multilateral market: two logics of governance

Cross-border trade today is organised mainly through power purchase agreements: two countries, two utilities, one line, one long-term contract fixing volume, price and duration, often with take-or-pay clauses. Its virtue is clear responsibility — the buyer knows what it will pay, the seller what it will recover, and disputes stay bilateral. Bilateral deals close easily because only two parties settle accounts, with no need to hand discretion to a third.

A multilateral market follows a different logic. It needs common rules, an independent system operator, cross-border settlement and dispute mechanisms, and prices for capacity, ancillary services and transmission rights. Once such rules exist, no country alone decides when its plants run or at what price they sell. Many observers argue that the step from bilateral to multilateral is not about building more lines but about whether governments accept being bound by one set of rules. The first is an engineering problem; the second is governance.

## State utilities and regulatory fragmentation: who has the authority to sign

Most ASEAN power systems rest on vertically integrated state utilities that are often the sole domestic buyer; tariffs, subsidies, licensing and planning are set by separate national regulators. Once cross-border trade begins, the utility is no longer the only buyer: it faces quotes from neighbours, dispatch instructions from across the border, and costs its own government did not approve alone. That reaches directly into institutional interests and regulatory authority.

The more practical obstacle is incompatible rules. Tariff formation differs — some systems carry cross-subsidies, others index to fuel — and grid codes, metering, reserve obligations and environmental rules vary too. Regional interconnection studies exist, but implementation must be aligned country by country, item by item. Fragmentation is not a shortage of documents; it is the absence of a body able to translate different systems into one set of settleable terms.

## Stranded assets: no one wants to recognise the bill for plants already built

Suppose cheap cross-border hydropower arrives at scale. Domestic gas and coal plants already built or contracted in the importing country would run fewer hours, their payback would stretch, and some could lose economic value before depreciation ends — becoming stranded assets. Who recognises that bill: the utility, the treasury, taxpayers or end users? Long-term power purchase agreements and take-or-pay clauses make exit expensive; even an underused plant still carries contractual obligations.

Caution about opening the market, then, is less about protecting inefficient capacity than about protecting the credibility of existing investment and contracts. Any market design that changes the cash flow of incumbent assets meets resistance from their owners, and in an industry this capital-intensive and long-cycle, that resistance is strong. The gains from interconnection are long-term and diffuse; the losses from stranded assets are immediate and concentrated. With benefits and costs asymmetric in time, negotiation stalls. This is the most concrete reason the grid moves slowly.

## New load from data centres: sudden pressure on the demand side

In recent years data centres, computing facilities and electrification have created new load concentrated in a few cities and industrial parks, with extremely high reliability requirements. According to public reporting, some grids have reassessed the pace of expansion and the case for imported power. The new load sends two opposing signals. It supplies a reason to interconnect — more electricity is needed, preferably low-carbon. But it also sharpens the worry: is it acceptable to entrust a data centre's continuous supply to a cross-border line and a neighbour's dispatcher?

Urgency on the demand side thus meets caution on the institutional side. Load grows quarter by quarter; rules are harmonised year by year. Capital can decide quickly to build a campus, but cannot quickly decide who bears liability for a cross-border fault. Many observers note that new load does not automatically generate momentum for interconnection; it may instead push governments to add controllable capacity at home first.

## Dispatch sovereignty: what interconnection actually transfers

Peel back these layers and the endpoint of interconnection is not a few more lines but the transfer of part of real-time dispatch authority to a transnational coordinator. Frequency stability, reserve capacity, fault isolation and emergency priority all require a body able to give orders; for it to be effective, governments must cede some discretion. For a state that treats supply security as a national responsibility, this is the hardest item to sign. Technical problems can be outsourced to engineers, financiers and vendors; sovereignty and incumbent assets cannot.

The pace of the ASEAN Power Grid therefore depends on whether a settlement can be designed that compensates stranded assets, bounds dispatch authority and shares risk — not on transmission technology. Until the two questions are answered, interconnection remains a collection of bilateral lines and planning documents. A cross-border line changes the direction of current; a set of regional rules changes the long-standing allocation of responsibility among states — and the latter is far harder to negotiate.
## Further reading

**ASEAN Power Grid**: A long-term plan to link ASEAN power systems and gradually form a regional electricity market; today it is mostly bilateral interconnection and pilot projects.

**Power purchase agreement**: A long-term contract fixing volume, price and duration, often with take-or-pay clauses; the most common way cross-border electricity trade is organised.

**Stranded asset**: An asset that loses economic value before its investment is recovered because of market or policy change; here, domestic plants that imported power could displace.
