# 越南北部暴雨成灾：在洪灾与旱情之间，越南的气候脆弱性无处遁形

2026年9月17日，河内多处道路积水及腰，电单车骑士不得不下车推车涉水。据联合早报报道，越南北部自9月14日起持续遭遇暴雨，引发洪水与山体滑坡，截至发稿已造成至少三人死亡、逾1万6000间房屋被淹、2万2000余公顷农作物受损。这并非一场孤立的天灾，而是敲在红河三角洲北缘的一记气候警钟——更吊诡的是，就在洪水未退之际，厄尔尼诺已预告中南半岛另一端即将转入干旱。

## 灾难现场：红河三角洲北缘的暴雨

这次暴雨的主战场在越南北部。越南政府9月17日在官网声明，北部多地自14日起连续强降雨，部分地区累计降雨量高达653毫米。洪水与11起山体滑坡、30处道路积水交织，多条国道与省道中断，河内至胡志明市的南北铁路部分区段被约一米深的洪水淹没，多趟列车于17日、18日停驶。首都河内中心城区积水最深达一米，部分学校被迫改为线上授课或停课。

一幅幅画面透着熟悉的无奈：骑手推车涉水、商铺进水、农田汪洋。但对越南而言，这种"北部年年雨季受灾"的剧本，早已不是新闻，而是反复上演的结构性困境。

## 数字：653毫米与一个被淹没的首都

把数字摊开，才知分量。653毫米——这是部分地区短短数日的累计降雨量，已逼近许多城市一个月的降水总量。逾1万6000间房屋被淹，意味着数以万计家庭被迫离家或就地避险；2万2000余公顷农作物受损，直接威胁小农的口粮与现金流。

更值得玩味的是铁路中断。南北铁路是越南的客运大动脉，一米深的积水就能让这条动脉"休克"，说明关键基础设施的防洪标准，远跟不上极端降水的节奏。当一座首都的核心交通因一场暴雨停摆，所谓"发展中经济体的韧性"，便露出最朴素也最刺眼的一面。

## 气候鞭击：从北涝到中南旱

真正让这场灾害超越局部意义的，是它所处的气候节律。联合早报援引气象部门称，清化至顺化一带周末仍有大雨，部分地区雨量或超100毫米；而厄尔尼诺现象预计将使越南中部和南部今年稍后降雨减少10%至50%。

这意味着一种"气候鞭击"：北部的越南人刚从洪水中爬起，中南部就要面对干旱。湄公河三角洲约35万公顷稻米种植区可能受波及，中部高原——越南主要的咖啡产区——也将遭遇严重缺水。同一个国家，上半年抗涝、下半年抗旱，农业与民生被夹在两种极端之间反复拉扯。

## 结构性脆弱：为什么越南总是受伤

越南的受灾，不能全怪天气。红河三角洲与湄公河三角洲地势低平、河网密布，本就易涝；而近几十年的快速城市化，又把大量人口与资产堆到了排水能力本就薄弱的冲积平原上。非正式聚落、被侵占的泄洪道、年久失修的排水管网，共同放大了每一次极端降水的破坏力。

更深层的问题是投资错位。越南把大量资源投向增长型基建——工业园区、高速公路、港口——却长期轻视"看不见"的韧性基建：城市排水、蓄滞洪区、早期预警与社区应急。当灾害来临，缺的从来不是抢险的干劲，而是事先把风险摊薄的能力。

## 治理答卷：应急有声，短板犹在

平心而论，越南政府的应急并非毫无章法。此次洪水期间，官方及时发布预警、组织线上教学、调度铁路停运，避免了更大规模的群死群伤。相比十年前同类灾害的信息滞后，今天的响应已明显进步。

但进步不等于过关。越南的灾害治理仍偏"抢险型"而非"韧性型"：灾害发生后动员有力，灾前的风险敞口却年年照旧。排水标准落后于气候、城市规划缺乏韧性评估、跨部门与跨省市的防洪协调碎片化，这些短板不会因为一次成功的应急而被抹平。真正的考题，是能否把"救灾英雄叙事"换成"少灾少损"的硬投入。

## 经济损失账：减少84.9%的悖论

据联合早报援引彭博社，越南今年迄今因自然灾害死亡或失踪63人，经济损失约22万亿越南盾（约61亿元人民币；原报道另以约1亿零790万新元折算，约合5.8亿元人民币），但较去年同期经济损失减少了84.9%。

这个数字看似喜人，却暗藏悖论。84.9%的降幅，更多源于今年前三季度极端事件相对温和，而非防灾能力质的飞跃；一旦遇上类似2024年的超强台风或流域性大洪，损失曲线随时可能反弹。把"损失同比下降"当作安全感的来源，恰恰是最危险的自我安慰——它掩盖了结构性风险的存量。

## 区域镜像：东南亚的气候压力测试

把镜头拉远，越南的困境是整个东南亚的缩影。泰国雨季洪灾、印尼绵延数月的林火与跨境烟霾、菲律宾频仍的台风、湄公河水位的历史性波动——东南亚正集体承受气候压力的"压力测试"。这些灾难往往跨境传导：烟霾飘过国界，稻米减产推高区域粮价，台风路径牵动供应链。

一国单打独斗的防灾，注定有天花板。东盟虽有灾害人道救援协调中心等机制，但协调一致的跨境早期预警、联合蓄水调度与气候融资，仍停留在"倡议多、落地少"的阶段。越南的洪水，因此也是区域治理成色的一块试金石。

## 出路：从抢险到韧性

要跳出"年年受灾、年年抢险"的循环，越南需要一次治理重心的位移。其一，把城市排水与蓄滞洪区纳入基建优先项，对标极端降水而非历史均值；其二，织密面向社区的早期预警，让预警从"发得出"到"到得了、用得上"；其三，推动气候韧性农业，在湄公河与中部高原布局耐旱作物与灌溉缓冲；其四，把灾害数据纳入区域共享，借东盟框架做联合防洪与粮安预案。

说到底，气候鞭击不会等人。越南今天在红河三角洲北缘扛下的这场雨，既是警告，也是窗口——能否把抢险的劲头，转成提前十年铺排的韧性，将决定下一次极端天气来临时，付出的是可承受的代价，还是又一笔用生命与家园写下的账单。

## 延伸阅读

**厄尔尼诺（El Niño）**：赤道中东太平洋海温异常增暖的现象，每二至七年出现一次，会改写全球降水格局，使东南亚部分地区旱涝极端化，是越南"北涝南旱"节律的重要推手。

**红河三角洲（Red River Delta）**：越南北部以红河冲积而成的低平平原，集中了河内等核心城市与大量人口资产，地势低洼、河网密布，是该国最易遭受风暴潮与内涝的区域之一。

**气候鞭击（Climate Whiplash）**：指同一地区在短期内从极端湿润骤转为极端干旱的现象，近年因气候变化而更频繁，对农业灌溉、水库调度与粮食安全构成连续冲击。

# Between Flood and Drought: Vietnam's Climate Vulnerability Laid Bare by the Northern Deluge

On 17 September 2026, waist-deep water stranded motorbikes across Hanoi as riders abandoned their vehicles and waded through the streets. According to Lianhe Zaobao, sustained heavy rain since 14 September triggered floods and landslides across northern Vietnam, leaving at least three dead, more than 16,000 homes submerged, and over 22,000 hectares of crops damaged. This was no isolated act of nature. It was a climate alarm ringing on the northern rim of the Red River Delta — and, more jarring still, even as the water had not receded, El Niño was already signalling a swing to drought at the other end of the Indochina peninsula.

## The scene: the deluge on the northern rim of the Red River Delta

Northern Vietnam was the epicentre. In a statement on 17 September, the government said relentless rainfall since the 14th had hit the north, with local accumulations reaching as high as 653 millimetres. Flooding dovetailed with 11 landslides and 30 flooded road sections, severing national and provincial highways; stretches of the Hanoi–Ho Chi Minh City railway sat under roughly one metre of water, halting trains on the 17th and 18th. In central Hanoi, water reached one metre, and some schools shifted online or closed.

The images carried a weary familiarity: riders pushing bikes through floodwater, shops swamped, farmland turned to lakes. But for Vietnam, the script of "northern floods every rainy season" is not news. It is a structural predicament replayed year after year.

## The numbers: 653 mm and a submerged capital

The figures reveal the weight. 653 millimetres — the accumulation in parts of the north in just days — approaches a full month's rainfall for many cities. More than 16,000 homes flooded means tens of thousands of families displaced or sheltering in place; over 22,000 hectares of crops lost strike directly at smallholders' food and cash flow.

Telling, too, is the railway shutdown. The north–south line is Vietnam's passenger artery, yet a single metre of water could paralyse it — proof that the flood defences of critical infrastructure lag far behind extreme precipitation. When a capital's core transport stalls over one downpour, the "resilience of a developing economy" shows its plainest and most glaring face.

## Climate whiplash: from northern floods to central-southern drought

What lifts this disaster beyond the local is the climate rhythm it sits in. Zaobao, citing the meteorological agency, said heavy rain would persist from Thanh Hoa to Hue through the weekend, with some areas exceeding 100 millimetres; yet El Niño is expected to cut rainfall across central and southern Vietnam by 10 to 50 per cent later this year.

This is "climate whiplash": northerners barely climb out of the floods before central and southern provinces brace for drought. Around 350,000 hectares of rice land in the Mekong Delta could be hit, and the Central Highlands — Vietnam's main coffee belt — will face severe water stress. One country, flooding in the first half of the year and drought in the second; agriculture and livelihoods pulled back and forth between two extremes.

## Structural fragility: why Vietnam keeps taking the hit

Vietnam's losses cannot be blamed on weather alone. The Red River and Mekong deltas are low, flat and laced with rivers — naturally flood-prone; decades of rapid urbanisation have meanwhile piled population and assets onto alluvial plains whose drainage was never built for this. Informal settlements, encroached floodways and ageing drainage networks all amplify every extreme rainfall.

Deeper still is a misallocation of investment. Vietnam pours resources into growth infrastructure — industrial parks, expressways, ports — while neglecting the "invisible" resilience kind: urban drainage, retention basins, early warning and community response. When disaster strikes, what is missing is never the will to rescue, but the capacity to spread the risk beforehand.

## The governance report card: response heard, gaps remain

To be fair, the government's response was not without method. During this flood it issued timely warnings, moved classes online and suspended rail services, averting larger mass casualties. Compared with the information lags of a decade ago, today's response is visibly improved.

But improvement is not a pass. Vietnam's disaster governance remains "rescue-led" rather than "resilience-led": vigorous mobilisation after an event, yet the pre-disaster risk exposure recurs year after year. Drainage standards trail the climate, urban planning lacks resilience assessment, and cross-agency and cross-provincial flood coordination is fragmented. A successful emergency does not erase those gaps. The real test is whether "heroic rescue" can be swapped for hard investment that simply means fewer disasters and fewer losses.

## The economic ledger: the paradox of an 84.9% drop

Zaobao, citing Bloomberg, reported that Vietnam has seen 63 dead or missing from natural disasters this year, with economic losses of about 22 trillion dong (roughly S$108 million, ~US$84 million) — yet that loss is down 84.9 per cent from the same period last year.

The number looks reassuring, but hides a paradox. The 84.9 per cent fall owes more to a relatively mild run of extreme events in the first three quarters than to a qualitative leap in preparedness; once a super-typhoon or basin-wide flood like 2024 returns, the loss curve can snap back at any time. Treating "year-on-year loss reduction" as a source of security is the most dangerous complacency of all — it masks the stock of structural risk.

## Regional mirror: Southeast Asia's climate stress test

Zoom out, and Vietnam's plight is the region's mirror. Thailand's monsoon floods, Indonesia's months-long fires and transboundary haze, the Philippines' frequent typhoons, record swings in Mekong water levels — Southeast Asia is collectively sitting a "stress test" of climate pressure. These disasters transmit across borders: haze drifts across frontiers, rice losses lift regional food prices, and typhoon tracks shake supply chains.

Disaster defence fought alone has a ceiling. ASEAN has mechanisms such as the Coordinating Centre for Humanitarian Assistance, yet coordinated cross-border early warning, joint reservoir management and climate finance remain stuck at "many initiatives, little delivery." Vietnam's floods are thus also a touchstone for the region's governance.

## The way out: from rescue to resilience

To break the "disaster every year, rescue every year" loop, Vietnam needs a shift in the centre of gravity. First, put urban drainage and retention basins on the infrastructure priority list, built to extreme rainfall rather than historical averages. Second, weave community-level early warning that travels from "issued" to "received and usable." Third, advance climate-resilient agriculture — drought-tolerant crops and irrigation buffers in the Mekong and Central Highlands. Fourth, share disaster data regionally and use the ASEAN framework for joint flood control and food-security plans.

In the end, climate whiplash will not wait. The rain northern Vietnam absorbed on the rim of the Red River Delta is both a warning and a window — whether the energy of rescue can be converted into resilience planned ten years ahead will decide, next time extreme weather arrives, whether the price is bearable or another bill written in lives and homes.

## Further reading

**El Niño**: The anomalous warming of sea-surface temperatures in the equatorial central and eastern Pacific, recurring every two to seven years. It rewrites global rainfall patterns and pushes Southeast Asia toward flood–drought extremes, a key driver of Vietnam's "north-wet, south-dry" rhythm.

**Red River Delta**: The low alluvial plain in northern Vietnam formed by the Red River, home to Hanoi and large concentrations of people and assets. Flat and river-laced, it is among the country's most exposed zones to storm surges and inland flooding.

**Climate Whiplash**: The rapid swing of one area from extreme wet to extreme dry within a short span, made more frequent by climate change. It hits irrigation, reservoir management and food security with consecutive shocks.
