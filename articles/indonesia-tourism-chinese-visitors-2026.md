# 印尼旅游业持续向好，每十位入境客就有一位中国人

## 数据：九月外国游客同比增长2.31%

印度尼西亚中央统计局近日发布的最新旅游统计，描绘出一幅温和而稳固的复苏图景。今年9月，印尼接待外国游客143万人次，较去年同期增长2.31%；今年1至9月累计接待外国游客1201万人次，同比增长5%。在全球旅游业仍受地缘政治与汇率波动扰动的当下，印尼能维持个位数但持续的正增长，本身已说明其作为长线目的地的韧性。

不过也要看到，2.31%的月度增速并不算快。它更像「挤水分后的真实需求」，而非疫情后那种报复性反弹。对于一个人口超过2.8亿、坐拥巴厘岛与无数火山海岛的国家来说，旅游业的盘子还远未到天花板，距离疫情前每年1600万至1700万人次外国游客的水平也仍有回升空间。

## 格局：马来西亚居首，中国稳居第三

分国别看，9月入境游客中，马来西亚以16.76%的占比位居第一，澳大利亚以12.43%排第二，中国以10.76%位列第三。中国游客占比首次稳定站上「一成」关口，意味着每十位到访印尼的外国人中，就约有一位来自中国。

这个排名耐人寻味。马来西亚高居榜首，源于两国地理相近、文化相通与长期的跨境探亲流；澳大利亚则受益于短途度假与商务往来。中国排在第三，看似不靠前，但考虑到中国出境游市场整体仍在恢复通道、且印尼并非中国游客的「第一梯队」目的地（泰国、日本、韩国长期占据前列），能稳居前三已属难得。更关键的是，这一比例来之不易——它建立在中国游客对东南亚目的地重新排序的过程之上。

## 侨缘：华人血脉与民间往来的双重纽带

中国游客在印尼的特殊之处，在于它叠加了一层别的客源国没有的维度——华人血脉。印尼是海外华侨华人数量最多的国家之一，数以百万计、有中国血统的公民早已深度嵌入当地社会。每逢春节、清明，往返于中国与印尼之间的，既有观光客，也有寻根探亲的归侨与眷属，还有往返两地经商、参加宗亲会与同乡会活动的侨社成员。

这种「旅游加血缘」的复合流动，使得中印尼民间往来比普通旅游关系更厚、更韧。它不完全随双边关系的冷热而剧烈起伏，也不易被单一的外交事件打断。换句话说，10.76%这个数字背后，是一张用家族、方言、宗祠与故乡记忆织成的网，是任何短期波动都难以撕开的民间底盘。

## 战略：从巴厘岛到万隆，旅游版图多点开花

印尼显然不满足于「巴厘岛等于印尼」的刻板印象。近年从爪哇的日惹、万隆，到苏门答腊的棉兰、巴淡岛，再到东努沙登加拉的科莫多，印尼旅游局与地方政府合力把客源从单一海岛向多点分流。对中国市场而言，这与中国游客「从打卡式观光转向深度体验」的需求恰好同频——越来越多人不再满足于沙滩度假，而要走进火山、古城与市井烟火。

值得注意的是，基础设施正成为关键变量。雅万高铁的开通，让雅加达至万隆的通勤时间大幅压缩，沿线旅游带随之被盘活；而更多直航航线的恢复，则直接降低了中国游客的出行门槛。旅游从来不是孤立的产业，它跟轨道、航线、签证与支付便利绑在一起。当印尼的机场、码头与移动支付场景更友好，中国游客的脚步自然会走得更远。

## 纵深：旅游是双边关系的温度计

把视野拉得更开，旅游从来不只是生意。它是民间互信最朴素的载体，也是双边关系的「温度计」。当中国游客愿意把假期投向印尼，当印尼商家把中文菜单、支付宝与微信支付摆上柜台，这是一种自下而上的相互靠近，比任何宣言都更可感。

当然也要冷静。当前中印尼关系的压舱石仍是经贸与资源合作——镍矿、棕榈油、基建与能源占据大头，2026年两国还以「全面战略伙伴」定位彼此、双向语言学习热持续升温。旅游只是其中轻盈却不可或缺的一块拼图。它未必能决定风向，却能让风向变得温和。在南海议题时有波澜、大国博弈加剧的背景下，10.76%的游客占比，是一份值得珍惜的民间善意，也是两国关系最柔软的减震器。

## 延伸阅读

**东南亚（Southeast Asia）**：中南半岛与马来群岛十一个国家的统称，印尼是其中面积最大、人口最多的国家。
**华侨华人（Overseas Chinese）**：旅居海外、保留中华文化认同的华人群体；印尼的华人群体规模在东南亚位居前列。
**入境旅游（Inbound tourism）**：指外国居民到本国境内旅游的消费活动，是印尼外汇收入的重要来源之一。

# Indonesia's tourism keeps recovering — one in ten inbound visitors is Chinese

## The numbers: 2.31% year-on-year growth in September

The latest tourism statistics from Indonesia's Central Statistics Agency (BPS) paint a picture of mild but steady recovery. In September this year, Indonesia received 1.43 million foreign tourist arrivals, up 2.31% from the same month a year earlier. For the first nine months of 2026, cumulative foreign arrivals reached 12.01 million, a 5% increase year-on-year. At a time when global travel is still jolted by geopolitical tensions and currency swings, Indonesia's ability to sustain positive—if modest—growth says something about the resilience of a long-haul destination.

That said, a 2.31% monthly gain is not spectacular. It reads more like "real demand after the froth was squeezed out" than the revenge-travel rebound of the post-pandemic months. For a country of over 280 million people blessed with Bali and countless volcanic islands, the tourism pie is far from full—and it still sits below the 16 to 17 million annual foreign arrivals Indonesia drew before the pandemic, leaving meaningful room to climb back.

## The landscape: Malaysia leads, China holds steady third

By source market, Malaysia topped September arrivals with a 16.76% share, followed by Australia at 12.43%, with China third at 10.76%. China's share has now firmly crossed the "one in ten" threshold—roughly one of every ten foreigners visiting Indonesia comes from China.

The ranking is telling. Malaysia's lead reflects geographic proximity, cultural affinity, and long-standing cross-border family visits; Australia benefits from short-haul holidays and business travel. China sitting third may look modest, but consider the context: China's outbound market is still recovering, and Indonesia is not among the top-tier destinations for Chinese travelers, where Thailand, Japan, and South Korea have long held the front ranks. To hold a stable third place is no small feat—and it has been hard-won amid a broader reshuffling of how Chinese tourists rank Southeast Asian destinations.

## The diaspora tie: bloodlines and people-to-people bonds

What makes Chinese visitors to Indonesia special is a dimension no other source market has: the Chinese diaspora. Indonesia is home to one of the world's largest ethnic-Chinese communities, with millions of citizens of Chinese descent deeply embedded in local society. Around Lunar New Year and Qingming, the flows between China and Indonesia mix sightseers with overseas Chinese returning to ancestral hometowns, as well as community members shuttling back and forth for business and clan or hometown associations.

This "tourism-plus-bloodline" compound flow makes people-to-people ties thicker and more durable than ordinary tourism. It does not swing as wildly with the temperature of bilateral relations, nor is it easily severed by a single diplomatic incident. Behind the 10.76% figure lies a web woven from families, dialects, clan halls, and memories of home—a popular foundation that no short-term fluctuation can easily tear apart.

## The strategy: beyond Bali, a diversified map

Indonesia is plainly unwilling to let "Bali equals Indonesia" define its brand. In recent years, from Yogyakarta and Bandung in Java to Medan and Batam in Sumatra, and on to Komodo in East Nusa Tenggara, the tourism board and regional governments have worked to spread visitors beyond the single island. For the Chinese market, this aligns neatly with a shift among Chinese travelers from checklist sightseeing toward deeper experiences—more and more now want volcanoes, heritage towns, and street life rather than just beach resorts.

Infrastructure is the decisive variable. The Jakarta–Bandung high-speed railway has slashed travel time between the two cities and energized the tourism belt along the line; the restoration of more direct flights has directly lowered the bar for Chinese visitors. Tourism is never an isolated industry—it is bound up with rails, routes, visas, and payment convenience. The friendlier Indonesia's airports, ports, and mobile-payment scenes become, the farther Chinese tourists' footsteps will naturally reach.

## The bigger picture: tourism as a barometer of bilateral ties

Zoom out, and tourism is never just business. It is the most plainspoken carrier of mutual trust between peoples, and a "thermometer" of the bilateral relationship. When Chinese tourists choose to spend their holidays in Indonesia, when Indonesian vendors put up Chinese-language menus and Alipay and WeChat Pay at the counter, that is a bottom-up convergence more tangible than any communiqué.

Yet a dose of sobriety is warranted. The ballast of China–Indonesia relations remains trade, resources, and investment—nickel, palm oil, infrastructure, and energy dominate, and in 2026 the two countries frame each other as comprehensive strategic partners while two-way language-learning enthusiasm keeps rising. Tourism is only a light yet indispensable tile in that mosaic. It may not set the wind's direction, but it can soften it. Amid occasional South China Sea ripples and intensifying great-power competition, a 10.76% share of visitors is a piece of popular goodwill worth cherishing—and the gentlest shock absorber in the relationship between the two nations.
