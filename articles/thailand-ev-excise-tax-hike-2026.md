# 泰国将进口纯电汽车消费税提至35%：逼出本地化，还是日企的助攻？

11月3日，泰国内阁批准了一项针对纯电动汽车的税收调整：自2027年1月1日起，整车进口、不含泰国本地制造零部件的纯电动乘用车（BEV）消费税从10%大幅上调至35%；增程式电动车（REEV）从15%调升至35%。泰国副首相兼财政部长埃尼提在会后表示，此举意在把泰国推向区域电动汽车制造与出口枢纽，同时保住汽车供应链及其支撑的大量就业岗位。说白了，曼谷想要的不是更多的进口整车，而是把组装线和零部件工厂留在本土。

## 税改落地：10%到35%的一纸令

这不是一次温和的调整，而是近乎翻倍的加税。泰国2026年1月1日才修订过车辆消费税结构，不到一年又出新政，节奏之快本身就耐人寻味。按新方案，进口纯电动车消费税由10%跳到35%，增程式电动车由15%跳到35%；而本地组装车的税率仍维持在2%的低位。一升一降之间，政策导向再清楚不过——用税差把整车进口的红利，逼成在泰设厂的诚意。

## 数字：中国品牌占据九成市场

数字最能说明问题。泰国工业联合会的数据透露，今年9月泰国纯电乘用车销量同比增长80%，至1.6404万辆；前9个月销量同比近乎翻番，达到16.1532万辆，占汽车总销量的约31%。更刺眼的是来源结构：2026年前9个月，泰国电动车进口额约为23.6亿美元（约170亿元人民币），其中96%来自中国；今年1至6月，泰国纯电乘用车新注册量同比增长84.8%，中国品牌份额高达90.0%。一句话，泰国电动车市场的增量，几乎由中国品牌定义。

## "日企施压"之说从何而来

那么，为何仅隔大半年又来一次调整？一些观察把原因指向日本车企的游说。据《日经亚洲》报道，长期以混合动力和内燃机见长的日本品牌，一直抱怨泰国现行税制"不公平"——在泰组装的混合动力车消费税率仅6%至15%，而内燃机车却要25%至35%。日本车企在东南亚的份额正被快速蚕食：绿色和平组织的一份报告显示，日本车企全球乘用车合并份额从2021年的32.7%降至2026年上半年的25.9%，而在东南亚，这一数字从73.9%一路滑落至50.9%。把税率这把刀，重新递到日系手里，听起来像是迟到的补偿。

## 税制背后的真实算计

不过，把一切归结为"日企施压"未免简单。泰国政府自己的账本也有压力。泰国经济近年增长乏力，消费者需求本就疲弱，政府既要保住汽车这一支柱产业的就业，又要吸引新的投资落地。埃尼提强调，新税制并非只为日本投资者开绿灯，而是为所有在泰投资生产的车企创造"公平竞争环境"，并已指示消费税局与有意设厂的车企磋商特别优惠税率。说到底，政府想用"进口加税—本地建厂—免税或低税"的杠杆，把整车进口的红利，转化为本土制造与就业。这是一道经典产业政策算术题。

## 并非逆转，而是"逼"出本地化

关键问题在于：加税能否真的逆转中国品牌的既有优势？多数分析给出的答案是否定的。道理很直白：在中国—东盟自贸区框架下，满足原产地规则的电动车整车出口泰国关税本就为零，泰国无法用关税挡住进口，于是退而用消费税"变相"抬高整车进口成本，引导车企本地化。但对已经或正准备在泰建厂的中国车企而言，这道税更像一记"催产针"，而非拦路石。比亚迪、长城等早已重金押注泰国本地生产，岚图、小鹏、零跑也表达了建线意愿。英国《汽车新闻》的判断颇具代表性：新政更可能加速行业整合，而非扭转中国品牌的优势。

## 日本车企的护城河还剩多少

那么日本车企真能借机上位吗？《民族报》的分析倒也清醒：日本制造商在泰国有现成的工厂、供应商网络和熟练工人，"可以带着优势起步"。但"带着优势起步"不等于"守住优势"。电动化转型迟缓，才是日系真正的软肋。更现实的是，即便消费税向混动倾斜，日本车企要满足泰国对发动机、电动机、驱动系统零部件本地化以及本土研发的更高要求，仍非易事。这场棋，日系拿到的是一手"存量好牌"，却未必是能赢的下半场。

## 中国车企的"泰国答案"

对中国车企而言，这道税暴露的与其说是风险，不如说是路径的确认。行业人士的判断很实在：中国电动汽车在泰国拥有稳定的竞争优势，企业既可以依托在泰基地以本地化方式供货，也可保留整车进口渠道，两种路径预计都会用上。消费税上调会抬高进口整车成本，但依托"贸易+投资"的组合拳，竞争力与经营灵活性仍在。换句话说，泰国想用税制把工厂"请"进来，中国车企本来就在"走进来"的路上——双方其实想到了一处，只是节奏被税改提前了。

## 抢购潮与年底车展的变量

短期变量同样值得玩味。泰国《曼谷邮报》提醒，新税率生效前，电动车需求、进口量和预订量可能在年底出现一波跳涨。毕竟，赶在35%落地前以10%的税率囤车，是再正常不过的套利逻辑。这种活跃，大概率会在12月2日开幕、为期12天的泰国国际车展上集中显现。对消费者是捡漏，对车企是清库存，对财政则是一次"提前征税"。政策还没生效，市场的应激反应已经先到。

## 规则变了，棋局未终

把视线拉长，这场税改的真正意义，或许不在"谁赢一时"，而在东南亚汽车产业规则的重写。泰国想从"进口终端"升级为"制造中枢"，日本想守住份额，中国车企则在"贸易+投资"间寻找最大公约数。三种逻辑同台，注定不会是零和。对曼谷而言，真正的考题从来不是把关税或消费税调到多高，而是本土供应链能否真正长出附加值、工人能否拿到更高技能的岗位。规则变了，棋局远未终了——而这恰恰是观察东南亚产业变迁最有意思的切口。

## 延伸阅读

**中国—东盟自贸区（China–ASEAN FTA）**：双方自贸框架下的原产地规则，使满足条件的电动车整车出口泰国关税为零，成为泰国改用消费税调节进口的根本前提。
**消费税法（Excise Tax）**：泰国对特定商品（含汽车）按类型与排放量征消费税，本地组装与整车进口税率不同，是产业政策常用的"隐形之手"。
**中国车企出海（Chinese Carmakers Going Global）**：以比亚迪、长城为代表的中国电动车品牌在东南亚的本地化布局，是观察中国制造从"产品出口"走向"产能出海"的窗口。

# Thailand's 35% EV Import Tax: Pushing Localisation, or a Lifeline for Japan's Carmakers?

On 3 November, the Thai cabinet approved an excise-tax overhaul for battery electric vehicles: from 1 January 2027, fully imported BEVs that contain no Thai-made parts will be taxed at 35%, up from 10%; range-extended EVs (REEVs) will rise from 15% to 35%. Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister Ekniti Nitithanprapas said the move is meant to turn Thailand into a regional EV manufacturing and export hub while protecting the supply-chain jobs the sector supports. Stripped of the diplomatic phrasing, Bangkok is signalling that it wants assembly lines and component plants on its soil, not just finished cars rolling off foreign docks.

## The tax order: from 10% to 35%

This is no gentle tweak but a near-doubling. Thailand only revised its vehicle excise structure on 1 January 2026, and a fresh policy within the year is itself telling. Under the new plan, the rate on imported BEVs jumps from 10% to 35% and REEVs from 15% to 35%, while locally assembled cars keep their low 2% rate. The signal could not be clearer: use the tax gap to convert the spoils of imported cars into a commitment to build plants at home.

## The numbers: Chinese brands hold a 90% share

The numbers tell the story. Figures from the Federation of Thai Industries show BEV passenger-car sales in September rose 80% year on year to 16,404 units, and the first nine months nearly doubled to 161,532 units — about 31% of all car sales. The sourcing is more striking: in the first nine months of 2026 Thailand imported roughly US$2.36 billion of EVs, 96% of them from China; in the first half, newly registered BEVs were up 84.8% and Chinese brands held a 90.0% share. In one sentence, China's brands have come to define the growth of Thailand's EV market.

## Where the "Japanese pressure" narrative comes from

Why, then, rework the tax barely ten months later? Some observers point to lobbying by Japanese automakers. Nikkei Asia reported that the Japanese brands built on hybrids and combustion engines have long complained the Thai regime is "unfair" — locally assembled hybrids pay just 6%–15%, while pure combustion cars face 25%–35%. Japanese carmakers are losing ground fast: a Greenpeace report puts their global passenger-car share at 32.7% in 2021 falling to 25.9% in the first half of 2026, and in Southeast Asia the drop is steeper, from 73.9% to 50.9%. Handing Tokyo a sharper tax tool looks, to many, like overdue compensation.

## The real calculus behind the tax redesign

Yet reducing it all to "Japanese pressure" is too simple. Bangkok has its own balance sheet to worry about. With growth sluggish and consumer demand weak, the government must protect jobs in its automotive backbone while still pulling in fresh investment. Ekniti stressed the redesign is not a handout to Japanese investors but a "level playing field" for every maker that builds in Thailand, and he has instructed the Excise Department to negotiate special rates with firms ready to set up plants. At bottom, the state wants to convert the spoils of imported cars into local manufacturing and employment through a classic lever: tax imports, subsidise local assembly. It is textbook industrial-policy arithmetic.

## Not a reversal, but a push toward localisation

The crux is whether the tax can actually reverse the Chinese brands' lead. Most analyses say no. The logic is plain: under the China–ASEAN FTA, EVs meeting rules of origin enter Thailand tariff-free, so Bangkok cannot use customs duties to block imports; instead it reaches for excise tax to indirectly raise the cost of finished cars and steer makers toward localisation. But for Chinese automakers already building or planning plants in Thailand, the tax is more a midwife than a roadblock. BYD and Great Wall have already bet heavily on local production; Voyah, Xpeng and Leapmotor have signalled their own intentions. Automotive News summed it up well: the policy is more likely to accelerate consolidation than to reverse Chinese dominance.

## How much of Japan's moat is left

Can Japanese carmakers really capitalise, then? The Nation's analysis is clear-eyed: Japanese makers have ready factories, supplier networks and skilled labour in Thailand and "can start with an advantage." But starting ahead is not the same as staying ahead. Slow electrification is the genuine weak spot. More concretely, even with the tax tilting toward hybrids, meeting Thailand's rising demands for localised engines, motors and drivetrain parts — plus domestic R&D — will be no small feat. Tokyo holds a strong "incumbent hand," but it is far from a guaranteed win in the second half.

## Chinese automakers' "Thailand answer"

For Chinese automakers, the tax reveals less a risk than a confirmation of path. Industry voices put it plainly: Chinese EVs hold a stable competitive edge in Thailand, and firms can supply both through local bases and via imports — and will likely use both. A higher excise tax raises the cost of finished cars, but the "trade plus investment" combo preserves competitiveness and flexibility. In other words, while Thailand uses tax to invite factories in, Chinese makers were already walking that road; the reform merely pulled forward the timetable both sides had quietly agreed on.

## The pre-buy rush and the year-end motor show variable

The short-term variable is worth watching too. The Bangkok Post warns that demand, imports and bookings could jump in the run-up to the new rate. After all, stocking cars at the old 10% before the 35% bites is the most natural arbitrage there is. That burst will likely converge on the 12-day Bangkok International Motor Show opening on 2 December. For buyers it is bargain-hunting, for makers it is inventory clearance, and for the treasury it is a round of "tax collected early." The policy has not even taken effect, yet the market's reflex has already arrived.

## The rules have changed, but the game is not over

Zoom out, and the tax's real significance may lie less in who wins the moment than in the rewriting of Southeast Asia's automotive rules. Thailand wants to upgrade from an import terminal to a manufacturing hub; Japan wants to hold its share; Chinese makers seek the greatest common divisor between trade and investment. Three logics on one stage are rarely zero-sum. For Bangkok, the true test was never how high it sets tariffs or excise rates, but whether local supply chains genuinely grow value-added and workers land higher-skilled jobs. The rules have changed, but the game is far from over — and that is precisely the most interesting vantage point from which to watch the region's industrial shift.

## Further reading

**China–ASEAN FTA**: Under the free-trade framework, EVs meeting rules of origin enter Thailand tariff-free — the very premise that forced Bangkok to reach for excise tax instead of customs duties.
**Excise Tax**: Thailand levies excise on specific goods including cars by type and emissions, with different rates for local assembly and fully imported vehicles — a classic "hidden hand" of industrial policy.
**Chinese Carmakers Going Global**: The localisation of BYD, Great Wall and peers across Southeast Asia is a window into China's shift from exporting products to exporting production capacity.
