## 从“抢项目”到“算总账”：人工智能数据中心热潮遭遇现实电网

过去一年，东南亚多国把吸引人工智能（AI）数据中心当作新的增长抓手：廉价土地、优惠电价、宽松审批，一度让这片土地成为全球云厂商扩张的热门选址。然而，当项目批量落地后，监管者才发现规则手册跟不上建设速度。最近几周，泰国、印尼和马来西亚接连“踩刹车”，区域投资叙事正从“来者不拒”转向“代价几何”。

泰国商业部9月4日宣布，暂停全国166个数据中心项目——其中49个已在施工，117个尚在审批——以便制定全国性标准，规范资源消耗、选址、安全与经济回报。与此同时，马来西亚能源委员会主席西蒂·萨菲娜·萨利赫指出，该国电力需求年增速因数据中心从1.5%至2%骤升至近10%，电网压力陡增。

## 印尼西爪哇：640兆瓦园区被叫停

在印度尼西亚西爪哇省，省长下令立即停止新加坡BDx（BDx Data Centres）数据中心公司在贾蒂卢胡尔建设的640兆瓦人工智能数据中心园区。检查人员发现项目缺少关键许可，包括环境影响评估。该项目9月22日才破土动工，不到一个月便被按下暂停键。

这一案例凸显了审批能力与投资热情之间的落差。政治经济学家阿尔文·坎巴指出，政府起初只看到数据中心带来的大额投资和云基础设施升级，“生怕落后”，却低估了土地、电力、水资源和监管协调等下游成本。

## 马来西亚与菲律宾：从土地红利到社区反弹

马来西亚柔佛州曾是数据中心的磁石，正是因为土地、电力和水资源相对便宜。但当项目储备累积到足够规模，这些资源便开始稀缺。吉隆坡附近哥打白沙罗的一个拟议站点在收到近300宗反对意见后，开发商撤回了申请。

在菲律宾伊洛伊洛省，一个50兆瓦人工智能数据中心项目遭遇农民强烈抗议，挪威投资方数日内撤回申请。这些社区反弹未必是政府决策的主因，却说明大规模数字基础设施同样需要“社会许可证”。

## 规则收紧不等于竞争力下降

政策研究者马克·马南坦认为，“投资者能够接受规则，但不能接受不可预期性”。更严格的监管未必削弱东南亚吸引力；相反，电力、水资源和许可流程的可靠性，可能比一味放宽审批更重要。新加坡和正向其看齐的柔佛，走的就是“规则可预期”路线。

这场降温也赋予区域政府更多谈判筹码：在电力和水资源紧张的情况下，它们可以要求数据中心项目附带本地采购、可再生能源、技术转让或本土人工智能能力提升。狂热之后，东南亚正在学习如何把“算力”变成真正的“发展红利”。

## 延伸阅读

**Data centre（数据中心）**：集中存放服务器、存储设备和网络设备的设施，是云计算和人工智能算力的物理底座，耗电量与耗水量巨大。

**AI boom（人工智能热潮）**：全球范围内围绕生成式人工智能和大模型展开的算力、资本与基础设施竞赛，正在重塑能源、半导体与云服务的供需格局。

**Grid pressure（电网压力）**：数据中心大规模并网后，对输配电系统造成的负荷骤增，可能导致供电紧张、电价波动和可再生能源配比要求提高。

## From a project rush to a cost audit: the AI data-centre boom meets the power grid

For the past year, Southeast Asian governments treated AI data centres as a new growth lever: cheap land, subsidised power and permissive approvals made the region a favoured destination for global cloud builders. Now that projects are arriving in bulk, regulators are discovering that the rule book has not kept up with the build-out. In recent weeks Thailand, Indonesia and Malaysia have all hit the brakes, and the regional investment narrative is shifting from "open arms" to "at what cost?"

On 4 September Thailand's Ministry of Commerce suspended 166 data-centre projects nationwide — 49 under construction and 117 awaiting approval — while it drafts national standards covering resource consumption, site selection, safety and economic benefit. At the same time, Siti Safinah Salleh, chair of Malaysia's Energy Commission, said electricity demand growth had jumped from 1.5-2% annually to nearly 10% because of data centres, straining the grid.

## West Java: a 640-megawatt campus halted

In Indonesia's West Java province, the governor ordered an immediate halt to construction of a 640-megawatt AI data-centre campus being built by Singapore-based BDx Data Centres in Jatiluhur. Inspectors found that key permits, including an environmental impact assessment, had not been issued. The project had broken ground only on 22 September, yet was paused less than a month later.

The case highlights the gap between investment enthusiasm and regulatory capacity. Political economist Alvin Camba notes that governments initially saw only the large capital inflows and cloud infrastructure upgrades, and feared being left behind, while underestimating the downstream costs of land, power, water and regulatory coordination.

## Malaysia and the Philippines: from land advantage to community pushback

Johor in Malaysia was once a magnet for data centres precisely because land, power and water were cheap. As the project pipeline grew large enough, those inputs began to run short. A proposed site in Kota Damansara near Kuala Lumpur was withdrawn after the developer received nearly 300 objections.

In Iloilo province in the Philippines, a proposed 50-megawatt AI data centre faced fierce protests from farmers, prompting the Norwegian investor to pull the application within days. Such community backlash is not necessarily the main driver of government decisions, but it shows that large-scale digital infrastructure also needs a social licence.

## Tighter rules do not automatically mean weaker competitiveness

Policy researcher Mark Manantan argues that "investors can live with rules, but not with unpredictability." Stricter regulation may not make Southeast Asia less attractive; what matters more is the reliability of power, water, permits and connectivity. Singapore, and Johor as it moves in the same direction, are betting on rule predictability rather than permissiveness.

The cooling also gives regional governments more bargaining power. With electricity and water becoming scarce, they can ask data-centre projects to deliver local procurement, renewable power, technology transfer or domestic AI capacity. After the frenzy, Southeast Asia is learning how to turn computing power into genuine development dividend.

## Further reading

**Data centre**: A facility that houses servers, storage and networking equipment; the physical base of cloud computing and AI computing power, and a heavy consumer of electricity and water.

**AI boom**: The global race for computing power, capital and infrastructure around generative AI and large language models, reshaping demand for energy, semiconductors and cloud services.

**Grid pressure**: The sudden load placed on power transmission and distribution systems when large numbers of data centres connect, which can cause supply tightness, price volatility and higher renewable-energy requirements.
