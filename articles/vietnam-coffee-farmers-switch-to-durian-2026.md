# 一条田埂上的两种赌注：越南咖啡农改种榴梿之后

## 八月的多乐省：卡车、果王与一条公路的欲望

每年8月到10月，越南中部多乐省的公路会被同一种货物塞满。满载榴梿的大卡车排成长队，从高原的果园驶向边境。据法新社记者在当地的描述，果园里农民正一颗一颗检查果实，确保这些被称为"果王"的榴梿能达到出口标准——差一点，就进不了那辆车。

多乐省所在的越南中部高原，每年生产着全球六分之一的咖啡。这片红土地盛产罗布斯塔咖啡豆，把越南送上了世界第二大咖啡生产国的位置，仅次于巴西。但今天，让这片土地躁动起来的不是咖啡，是榴梿。

当地农民把话说得很直白：种咖啡能养家糊口，种榴梿能让人发家致富。一句大白话，胜过一堆产业报告。

## 七倍的差距，勤劳补不上

把这个"直白"换算成数字会更刺眼。56岁的范春麟是多乐省农民，20年前就开始尝试多元种植，如今拥有400棵榴梿树，每年产出约60公吨榴梿。他估计今年能靠榴梿赚到7.6万美元（约51万元人民币）。

如果他只种咖啡呢？年收入约1万美元（约6.7万元人民币）。

七倍多的差距，不是靠多起几个早、多施几遍肥能追上的。当一个农户面对这样的算术题，任何关于"保持作物多样性""保护咖啡产业传统"的劝说都会显得苍白。市场的价格信号已经替他做了决定，而且声音大到足以盖过一切产业政策。

## 一个提前二十年下注的人

但范春麟的故事里有一个容易被略过的细节：他不是这两年才转种的，而是20年前就开始了。

这意味着什么？意味着他在榴梿价格起飞之前，已经用二十年时间摸清了土壤、湿度、肥料比例和病虫害的脾气，也积累下了改种所需的资本。他的400棵树不是一次冲动的结果，而是一条走了二十年的路。

而今天挤进这条路上的人，没有这二十年。他们看到的是范春麟的别墅，看不到他前二十年缴的学费。这是所有"致富故事"共同的盲区：媒体报道的是已经跑出来的那一个，制度却要为还在路上、随时可能摔倒的大多数负责。

## 阿雄的那一半故事

联合早报的报道里出现了另一个人，只用了两段——42岁的农民阿雄，不愿透露全名。

五年前，他把原本的咖啡园全部改种榴梿。两年前，他又改回了咖啡树。他说，要达到榴梿的出口质量要求很困难。"我做不到。我失败了，我再也不种榴梿了。"

这段话值得反复读。榴梿不是咖啡，它对湿度、水分和肥料比例的要求严苛得多，果实品质不达标的农户，面对的不是"便宜点卖"，而是根本没有买家。范春麟的成功与阿雄的失败，其实遵循的是同一套规则：这行有门槛，而门槛是用钱、技术和时间垫起来的。

问题在于，政策讨论往往只围绕成功者展开。当一个地方政府为榴梿种植面积的增长而兴奋时，它有没有为阿雄这样的人准备退出通道？

## 十年五倍：一场被单一市场牵引的转换

越南农业部的统计显示，过去十年，全国专门种植榴梿的土地面积激增超过五倍，达到20万公顷。这是一场规模可观的土地用途重构。

时间线很清楚。越南在2022年签署贸易议定书后才正式进军中国榴梿市场，短短几年就取代泰国，结束了后者近20年的霸主地位，成为中国最大的榴梿供应国。去年，中国进口的新鲜榴梿价值近75亿美元（约504亿元人民币），比2015年增长近12倍。越南今年的榴梿出口预计达到40亿美元（约269亿元人民币），远高于2021年的1.8亿美元（约12.1亿元人民币），其中九成出口到中国。

把这串数字摊开看，结论并不复杂：这不是一场由技术进步或品种改良驱动的农业升级，而是需求外溢——越南农民在替另一个国家消费者的口味改种自己的土地。中国市场的胃口有多大，越南的榴梿地就有多大。

## 政府的警告与"榴梿周期"

值得肯定的是，越南政府并没有被出口数字冲昏头。今年8月初，官方就公开警告：种植面积的快速增加，提高了榴梿供应过多和质量下降的风险。农民自己也在担忧两件事——过度依赖中国市场，以及更多人加入后出现供过于求。

这种担忧有充分的历史依据。小农经济的价格周期几乎是同一套剧本：价格信号出现→农户集体扩种→供给过剩→价格崩塌→砍树改种。越南的咖啡、胡椒、橡胶都演过这出戏，东南亚其他国家的水果、木薯、棕榈也没能免俗。

但这一次，剧本多了一个变量。以往的需求端是分散的全球市场，价格波动有对冲空间；这一次，九成的出口指向同一个买家。而这个买家不仅体量巨大，还掌握着一套可以随时调节的检疫与质量标准——它不需要发布任何政策声明，只要把抽检频率提高一档，通关节奏就会立刻改变。

## 单一买主的风险算术

所以，真正需要算的不是"榴梿还能赚几年"，而是"九成"这个比例本身。

九成出口到中国，意味着议价权、标准权、通关节奏，很大程度上都不在越南农户手里。他们的收入不仅取决于自己种得好不好，还取决于一个他们完全没有影响力的市场监管系统怎么动作。这是一种不对称的依赖：收益是个人的、即时的，风险是集体的、延后的。

要降低这种依赖，靠劝农民少种榴梿显然不现实，也不公平。能做的在三处：一是把"九成"这个数字慢慢降下来，向其他市场要增量；二是把加工能力留在国内，让不达鲜果出口标准的果实还有去处，而不是烂在地里；三是尽快建立可追溯的质量分级体系，把"达标"从一个玄学变成一套农户能照着做的流程。

对东南亚观察者而言，多乐省这条公路上的卡车，运送的不只是水果。它运送的是一个更普遍的问题：当一个发展中经济体的农业越来越紧密地挂上邻国消费者的餐桌时，增长的来源与风险的来源，就变成了同一件事。区别只在于，增长的账单当月就能收到，风险的账单要等几年。

## 延伸阅读

**罗布斯塔（Robusta）**：咖啡两大主要栽培品种之一，抗病性强、产量高、咖啡因含量较高，多用于即溶咖啡与拼配豆；越南中部高原是全球最主要的罗布斯塔产区。

**果王（Durian）**：榴梿在东南亚的俗称，因其气味浓烈、价格高昂而得名；中国市场近年成为全球榴梿需求增长最快的来源，直接改写了泰国、越南、马来西亚的种植结构。

**单一作物依赖（Monocrop dependence）**：一个地区或农户的收入高度集中于单一经济作物的状态，能带来短期高收益，但会放大价格波动、病虫害与买方集中度带来的风险。

# Two gambles on one field: what happens after Vietnam's coffee farmers switch to durian

## Dak Lak in August: trucks, the "king of fruits," and a road full of appetite

Every year from August to October, the roads of Dak Lak province in Vietnam's central highlands fill with a single cargo. Trucks piled high with durian queue up from the highland orchards towards the border. Agence France-Presse correspondents on the ground describe farmers inspecting fruit one by one, making sure these "kings of fruits" meet export standards — fall short by a little, and the fruit never makes it onto the lorry.

The central highlands around Dak Lak produce roughly one sixth of the world's coffee. The red soil here grows Robusta beans that have made Vietnam the world's second-largest coffee producer, after Brazil. Yet what sets this land stirring today is not coffee. It is durian.

Local farmers put it bluntly: coffee lets you feed a family; durian lets you get rich. One plain sentence beats a shelf of industry reports.

## A sevenfold gap that hard work cannot close

Translate that bluntness into numbers and it stings. Pham Xuan Lan, a 56-year-old farmer in Dak Lak, began diversifying two decades ago and now owns 400 durian trees yielding around 60 tonnes of fruit a year. He expects to earn as much as US$76,000 from durian this year.

Had he stuck to coffee? Annual income of about US$10,000.

A gap of more than seven times is not something you close by rising earlier or fertilising more often. Faced with that arithmetic, any appeal to "preserve crop diversity" or "protect the coffee heritage" sounds hollow. The price signal has already decided for him, and it is shouting loud enough to drown out industrial policy.

## A man who placed his bet twenty years early

One detail in Pham Xuan Lan's story is easy to skip: he did not switch in the last two years. He started twenty years ago.

That matters. It means he spent two decades learning his soil, humidity, fertiliser ratios and pest cycles, and accumulating the capital a switch requires. His 400 trees are not the fruit of an impulse; they are the end of a twenty-year road.

The people now crowding onto that road do not have twenty years. They see the villa; they do not see the tuition fees paid in the first two decades. This is the blind spot in every success story: the media reports the one who made it out, while policy has to answer for the many still on the road who may fall at any moment.

## The other half of the story, told by Ah Hung

Lianhe Zaobao's report gives another man two paragraphs: a 42-year-old farmer who gave only the name Ah Hung.

Five years ago he converted his entire coffee plot to durian. Two years ago he converted it back to coffee. Meeting export quality standards, he says, proved too hard. "I couldn't do it. I failed. I will never grow durian again."

That passage deserves re-reading. Durian is not coffee. It is far more demanding about humidity, water and fertiliser ratios, and a grower whose fruit misses the standard is not offered "a lower price" — he is offered nothing. Pham Xuan Lan's success and Ah Hung's failure obey the same rule: this trade has a threshold, and the threshold is built out of money, technique and time.

The trouble is that policy discussion tends to organise itself around the winners. When a local government grows excited about expanding durian acreage, has it built an exit route for people like Ah Hung?

## Fivefold in a decade: a conversion pulled by one market

Vietnam's Ministry of Agriculture reckons that land devoted specifically to durian has grown more than fivefold over the past decade, reaching 200,000 hectares. That is a substantial restructuring of land use.

The timeline is clear. Vietnam signed a trade protocol in 2022 and only then formally entered the Chinese durian market; within a few years it displaced Thailand, ending nearly two decades of Thai dominance to become China's largest durian supplier. Last year China imported close to US$7.5 billion of fresh durian, nearly twelve times the 2015 figure. Vietnam's durian exports are expected to reach US$4 billion this year, far above the US$180 million of 2021, with nine tenths of that going to China.

Laid out, the conclusion is not complicated. This is not an agricultural upgrade driven by technological progress or improved varieties. It is demand spillover: Vietnamese farmers are replanting their own land to suit the palate of another country's consumers. As big as China's appetite is, so big is Vietnam's durian acreage.

## The government's warning and the durian cycle

Credit where it is due: Hanoi has not been intoxicated by the export figures. In early August officials warned publicly that the rapid expansion of acreage raises the risk of oversupply and falling quality. Farmers themselves worry about two things — over-reliance on the Chinese market, and oversupply as more growers pile in.

History supports the worry. The price cycle in smallholder agriculture follows almost the same script everywhere: a price signal appears, growers plant en masse, supply outruns demand, prices collapse, trees are cut down. Vietnam has played it out with coffee, pepper and rubber; elsewhere in Southeast Asia, fruit, cassava and palm have not been exempt.

This time, though, the script has an extra variable. Previously the demand side was a diffuse global market where price swings could be hedged. This time nine tenths of exports point at the same buyer — a buyer that is not merely huge but also holds a set of quarantine and quality standards it can tighten at will. It need issue no policy statement; turn the inspection dial up one notch and the clearance tempo changes immediately.

## The arithmetic of a single buyer

So the real calculation is not "how many more years can durian earn." It is the ratio itself: nine tenths.

Nine tenths going to China means bargaining power, standard-setting power and clearance tempo largely sit outside Vietnamese farmers' hands. Their income depends not only on how well they grow, but on how a regulatory system they have zero influence over decides to act. It is an asymmetrical dependence: the gains are individual and immediate, the risks collective and deferred.

Cutting that dependence by persuading farmers to plant less durian is neither realistic nor fair. Three things can be done instead. Bring the nine-tenths figure down over time by finding increment in other markets. Keep processing capacity at home so that fruit which misses fresh-export grade still has somewhere to go other than rotting in the field. Build a traceable quality-grading system quickly, turning "meeting the standard" from an occult art into a procedure a grower can follow.

For anyone watching Southeast Asia, the trucks on that Dak Lak road are not merely carrying fruit. They are carrying a broader question: when a developing economy's agriculture is ever more tightly wired to its neighbour's dinner table, the source of growth and the source of risk become the same thing. The only difference is that growth is billed monthly, while risk is billed years later.

## Further reading

**Robusta**: One of the two main cultivated coffee species — disease-resistant, high-yielding and higher in caffeine, used largely for instant coffee and blends. Vietnam's central highlands are the world's leading Robusta-growing region.

**Durian**: A Southeast Asian fruit nicknamed the "king of fruits" for its pungent aroma and high price. Chinese demand has been the fastest-growing source of global durian consumption in recent years, directly reshaping planting patterns in Thailand, Vietnam and Malaysia.

**Monocrop dependence**: A condition in which a region's or household's income is heavily concentrated in a single cash crop. It delivers high short-term returns but amplifies exposure to price swings, pests and buyer concentration.
