# 泰国能否在2037年跨入高收入国家行列？世界银行的一份路线图与三重隐忧

据《日经亚洲》报道，世界银行9月3日在曼谷商业峰会发布旗舰报告《建设泰国未来》（Building Thailand's Future Today），为泰国规划了一条通往"高收入国家"的路线图：若能在2037年前将人均GDP年均增速提升到5.4%，泰国便可跨入高收入门槛。这份报告来得正是时候——10月12日至18日，国际货币基金组织与世界银行的年会将在曼谷举行，这是1991年以来该会议首度重回泰国。但雄心背后，泰国经济正卡在"中等收入陷阱"里：潜在增速已回落至2.7%，人口加速老龄化，产业升级屡屡雷声大雨点小。一份漂亮的蓝图，能否真正落地？

## 一份"未来之书"的雄心

世界银行在这份报告里为泰国列了一张"未来产业"清单：先进制造、可持续与健康旅游、数字服务、农业食品、创意产业。逻辑并不复杂——泰国不能再靠低成本劳动力和代工续命，必须向高附加值、高生产率的活动爬升。报告同时提出两条主线："升级"与"活力"。升级意味着更快采用技术、强化创新与本土附加值；活力则要求让更多企业进入、成长并竞争，让劳动力技能跟上，让二线城市也长出增长极。世行东亚与太平洋地区副行长卡洛斯·菲利普·哈拉米略说得直白：泰国的雄心"触手可及"，但前提是进入"更高价值增长"的新阶段。

## 数字：5.4%与2.7%的鸿沟

最刺眼的是那道数字鸿沟。世界银行测算，泰国要在2037年（距1932年君主立宪百年仅一步之遥）成为高收入国家，需要人均GDP年均增长5.4%。而现实是：2024年泰国经济增速仅2.5%，潜在增速更在2022至2030年间回落约0.5个百分点至2.7%。按当前轨迹，泰国"到2037年将无法实现高收入抱负"。换句话说，泰国需要用十一年时间，把增速整整抬升近一倍——这不是微调，而是换挡。泰国并非没有高光时刻：它曾用一代人的时间完成从农业国到出口导向工业国的跃迁，世行也援引这一点为其打气。但上一次奇迹的条件，今天未必还在。

## 中等收入陷阱：东南亚的老问题

"中等收入陷阱"从来不是泰国一家的心病。拉美国家困了数十年，东南亚亦如此——马来西亚自2011年前后跻身中高收入行列后，始终差临门一脚；越南近年刚跨入中高收入门槛，正拼命向价值链上游爬。泰国恰恰卡在最尴尬的位置：比越南富，却比马来西亚和老牌高收入经济体慢。世界银行的报告本质上是给泰国一剂"避免躺平"的清醒药——若不主动升级，人口红利消退后的泰国，很可能沦为区域内"增长平庸"的样本。这正是观察东南亚发展竞赛时最值得盯紧的一条线：谁先跳出陷阱，谁就握住下一轮产业链重构的话语权。

## 五大赛道：泰国的"比较优势"清单

报告挑出的五个赛道，几乎都踩在泰国的既有长板上。先进制造接续了泰国作为区域汽车与电子组装基地的底子；可持续与健康旅游则放大了它"医疗旅游+度假"的独特组合；农业食品呼应其全球主要粮食与橡胶出口国身份；创意产业押注软实力与文化输出；数字服务则要补上长期偏弱的短板。问题在于，这些赛道没有一条是泰国独享。越南在制造业与半导体上猛追，马来西亚抢半导体封装与数据中心，印尼握资源与人口。泰国的"比较优势"若只停留在报告里，就会被邻居们一点点蚕食。

## 中国因素：电动车与供应链的暗线

这条线索外界往往轻描淡写，却是2026年泰国经济最实在的变量之一。此前本站的观察已指出，2026年中国在泰国的电动车直接投资首次超过日本——比亚迪、长城、埃安等中国车企把泰国当作东南亚的整车与电池枢纽。这一趋势恰好与世行看好的"先进制造"和"可持续旅游"耦合：电动车产业链既是高端制造，又是绿色转型。但硬币的另一面是，泰国会不会从"日系车厂的后院"变成"中系供应链的组装间"？技术、品牌与附加值究竟有多少留在本地，决定这份蓝图是自主升级，还是又一次外部依附。

## 人口悬崖：最硬的约束

在所有约束里，人口是最冷酷的一条。泰国生育率已跌至更替水平以下多年，劳动年龄人口见顶回落，老龄化速度在东南亚仅次于新加坡。世行报告把"让更多人更长地参与生产"列为劳动力未来的核心，本身就说明问题：当被抚养比攀升，储蓄率、财政与增长都会被拖累。对比越南——后者仍享有人口红利尾巴，且通过加大对华榴莲贸易与制造业承接持续攒劲。泰国若不能在人口结构彻底恶化前完成产业升级，等到"未富先老"坐实，5.4%的增速目标将不再是雄心，而是奢望。

## 蓝图易画，落地难行

泰国从不缺路线图。从"泰国4.0"到东部经济走廊（EEC），从生物—循环—绿色（BCG）经济到各式产业升级计划，文件叠文件，口号换口号，落地的成色却参差。世行的报告再精彩，也只是"咨询平台"上的建议，真正的关卡在芭提雅与曼谷的官僚体系、在地方利益与中央目标的撕扯、在选举周期对短期刺激（如1万泰铢（约2000元人民币）的现金发放）的偏好。2037年的高收入目标，与其说是一份经济预测，不如说是一张政治承诺书——而泰国政治，恰恰以易变著称。蓝图易画，行路难。

## 延伸阅读

**中等收入陷阱（Middle-Income Trap）**：指一国人均收入达到中等水平后，因成本上升、创新不足而难以向高收入跃升的发展停滞现象。

**东部经济走廊（Eastern Economic Corridor, EEC）**：泰国面向高端制造与数字产业的国家级经济特区，是"泰国4.0"战略的核心抓手。

**人均国民总收入（GNI per Capita）**：世界银行划分高/中/低收入的核心指标，2025年高收入门槛约为1.4万美元（约10万元人民币）。

# Can Thailand Reach High-Income Status by 2037?

The World Bank's flagship report "Building Thailand's Future Today," launched on 3 September at the Bangkok Business Summit, sets out a roadmap: if Thailand can lift its per-capita GDP growth to 5.4% a year through 2037, it will cross the high-income threshold. The timing is deliberate — the IMF–World Bank Annual Meetings land in Bangkok on 12–18 October, the first time since 1991. Yet behind the ambition sits an uncomfortable truth. Thailand's potential growth has slipped to 2.7%, its population is ageing fast, and its track record of turning industrial roadmaps into results is mixed. A handsome blueprint is one thing; delivery is another.

## A "book of the future" with grand ambitions

In its report, the World Bank hands Thailand a checklist of "industries of the future": advanced manufacturing, sustainable and wellness tourism, digital services, agrifood, and creative industries. The logic is straightforward — Thailand can no longer live on cheap labour and contract assembly; it must climb toward higher-value, higher-productivity activity. The report pairs this with two pillars: "upgrading" and "dynamism." Upgrading means faster technology adoption, stronger innovation, and deeper local value addition. Dynamism means letting more firms enter, scale, and compete, lifting workforce skills, and turning secondary cities into growth poles. Carlos Felipe Jaramillo, the World Bank's vice president for East Asia and the Pacific, put it plainly: Thailand's high-income ambition is "within reach," but only if it enters a new phase of higher-value growth.

## The numbers: a gap between 5.4% and 2.7%

The starkest line is the arithmetic gap. The World Bank estimates Thailand needs 5.4% annual per-capita GDP growth to become high-income by 2037. The reality: the economy grew just 2.5% in 2024, and potential growth has eased about half a percentage point to 2.7% for 2022–30. At the current trajectory, Thailand "will not achieve its high-income aspirations by 2037." In other words, it must nearly double its pace over eleven years — not a tweak, but a gear change. Thailand has form here: within a single generation it leapt from an agrarian economy to an export-led industrial one, and the Bank cites exactly that as reason for confidence. But the conditions behind the last miracle are not guaranteed to repeat.

## The middle-income trap: an old Southeast Asian problem

The middle-income trap is hardly Thailand's alone. Latin America has been stuck for decades, and Southeast Asia is no stranger to it — Malaysia has hovered in the upper-middle-income band since around 2011, while Vietnam only recently crossed into it and is scrambling up the value chain. Thailand sits in the most awkward spot: richer than Vietnam, yet slower than Malaysia and the established high-income economies. The World Bank's report is essentially a "don't settle" jolt — without proactive upgrading, an ageing Thailand risks becoming the region's poster child for "mediocre growth." This is the thread worth watching in the Southeast Asian development race: whoever escapes the trap first seizes the voice in the next round of supply-chain reshuffling.

## Five tracks: Thailand's comparative-advantage checklist

The five chosen sectors largely rest on Thailand's existing strengths. Advanced manufacturing extends its base as a regional auto and electronics assembly hub; sustainable and wellness tourism amplifies its one-of-a-kind "medical care plus resort" mix; agrifood fits its role as a major global exporter of rice and rubber; creative industries bet on soft power; digital services must fix a chronic weakness. The catch: none of these lanes is Thailand's alone. Vietnam is charging in manufacturing and semiconductors, Malaysia is grabbing chip packaging and data centres, Indonesia holds resources and people. If Thailand's "comparative advantages" stay on paper, neighbours will quietly eat its lunch.

## The China factor: the EV and supply-chain undercurrent

This thread is often understated, yet it is one of the most concrete variables in Thailand's 2026 economy. As this site has noted, Chinese direct investment in Thailand's electric-vehicle sector overtook Japan's for the first time in 2026 — BYD, GWM, and Aion are treating the country as a Southeast Asian hub for vehicles and batteries. That trend dovetails neatly with the Bank's favoured "advanced manufacturing" and "sustainable tourism": the EV chain is both high-end industry and green transition. But the flip side is real. Will Thailand move from "Japan's backyard assembly line" to "China's supply-chain workshop"? How much technology, branding, and value-added stays local will decide whether this blueprint means autonomous upgrading or a fresh round of external dependence.

## The demographic cliff: the hardest constraint

Of all the constraints, demographics are the cruellest. Thailand's fertility has fallen below replacement for years; its working-age population has peaked and is shrinking, and its ageing pace in Southeast Asia is second only to Singapore's. The report lists "enabling more people to participate in productive employment for longer" as the core of the workforce agenda — telling in itself. As the dependency ratio rises, savings, public finances, and growth all take a hit. Contrast Vietnam, still enjoying the tail of its demographic dividend and pressing advantage through durian exports to China and manufacturing uptake. If Thailand fails to upgrade before its structure deteriorates, the 5.4% target stops being ambition and becomes fantasy.

## Blueprints are easy, implementation is hard

Thailand is no stranger to roadmaps. From "Thailand 4.0" to the Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC), from the bio-circular-green (BCG) economy to assorted industrial plans, documents stack on documents and slogans replace slogans, yet the delivery record is uneven. The World Bank's report, however polished, is advice on a "consultation platform"; the real bottlenecks sit in the bureaucracy of Bangkok and Pattaya, in the tug between local interests and central goals, and in the political preference for short-term stimulus — such as a THB 10,000 cash handout (about US$300). The 2037 high-income goal is less an economic forecast than a political promissory note — and Thai politics, famously, is prone to reversal. Blueprints draw easily; the road is hard.

## Further reading

**Middle-Income Trap**: A development stagnation where a country, after reaching middle-income status, struggles to advance to high income because costs rise while innovation lags.

**Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC)**: Thailand's flagship special economic zone for high-end manufacturing and digital industries, the centrepiece of the "Thailand 4.0" strategy.

**GNI per Capita**: The World Bank's core metric for classifying economies; the 2025 high-income threshold is around US$14,000.
