# 升格中高收入，增速却垫底：菲律宾的“里程碑”为何没能撑起增长

世界银行今年7月将菲律宾上调为“中高收入国家”，这本该是东南亚最受关注的新兴经济体之一迈过的一道门槛。然而据《南华早报（South China Morning Post）》9月21日报道，这一身份并未让马尼拉更有底气应对眼前的增长压力。公共支出疲软、基建进度滞后、生活成本高企，再加上防洪腐败丑闻调查的外溢冲击，正把菲律宾推到区域“掉队者”的位置。评级上调的喜讯，与增速垫底的冷现实，形成了刺眼的对照。

## 里程碑的讽刺

升格中高收入，意味着按人均国民总收入（GNI）计算，菲律宾跨过了世界银行划定的门槛。对历届政府而言，这是一份可以用来向选民交差的“成绩单”。但《南华早报》援引的分析师直言，这道门槛“几乎没能缓冲短期增长压力”。换句话说，标签变了，口袋没鼓，增长反而更吃力。这种“名义升级、实际承压”的反差，恰恰说明中等收入门槛从来不是增长的保证书，而只是一张阶段性体检单。

## 数字：区域最慢的增长

数字摆在桌面上。美国银行（Bank of America）预测菲律宾2026年国内生产总值（GDP）增速仅为2.5%，与泰国并列东南亚最慢；与此同时，该行把东盟整体增长预期上调至5%，较第二季度末的4.7%进一步走高。美国银行在9月报告中称，菲律宾相对2025年“将出现明显放缓”，并给出一组刺眼的对照：新加坡预计增长5.1%、马来西亚5.2%、印度尼西亚5.3%、越南8.2%。当邻居们都在加速，菲律宾的“掉队”就越发显眼。需要指出的是，看淡菲律宾的并非美国银行一家——此前亚洲开发银行把其2026年增长预期下调至3.3%，标普全球降至2.9%，国际货币基金组织降至4.1%，罕见的一致下修，说明市场对马尼拉近期动能的悲观已相当普遍。

## 消费引擎为何熄火

菲律宾长期被视作“消费驱动型”经济体，庞大的年轻人口和侨汇撑起的消费需求，一直是增长的主引擎。但《南华早报》采访的菲律宾经济学家、市场策略师乔纳森·拉维拉斯指出，与东盟同伴相比，菲律宾眼下正面临“相对放缓”——越南、印尼和马来西亚从制造业扩张、供应链搬迁和更强的外国直接投资（FDI）中获益更多，而菲律宾经济“高度依赖消费，而这台引擎已被更高的借贷成本、攀升的生活成本和放缓的公共支出所抑制”。一句话：别人在搞生产，菲律宾还在靠花钱；当钱不好借、物价又高，消费这台引擎自然就哑了火。

## 基建欠账：被反腐调查拖住的钱

更棘手的是公共投资。菲律宾理察商业银行首席经济学家迈克尔·里卡福特认为，政府补上基建“追赶性支出”，是重新拉起经济增长的“缺失环节”。但自2025年底以来，围绕防洪工程腐败丑闻的调查，让相关支出明显放缓。讽刺的是，多数投资者担心的不是反腐本身，而是反腐拖出来的延误。拉维拉斯说得直白：“透明和问责有利于投资信心，但项目必须继续推进。政策制定者的挑战，是在不拖慢执行的前提下整顿体制。”钱不是没有，是被调查卡在了半路——这比腐败本身更伤增长。

## 邻国的答卷：制造与搬迁

为什么同样是东盟成员，差距越拉越大？答案写在产业结构上。越南凭电子、半导体零部件出口吃到了全球人工智能需求的红利；印尼、马来西亚则借供应链搬迁和更强的外资流入扩大制造基本盘。菲律宾并非没有制造底子，却在上一轮产业转移中没能像邻居那样把外资和工厂牢牢接住。当区域竞争从“谁更便宜”转向“谁更能造”，偏重消费的菲律宾就显得被动。这背后，是长期政策重心与区域大势的一次错位。

## 气候与油价：双重外部冲击

内部尚未理顺，外部又来添乱。《南华早报》提到，预测机构警告厄尔尼诺驱动的干旱可能延续到2027年初，威胁粮价和农户收入——而这恰恰发生在家庭已不堪生活成本之重的时候。更宏观的地缘变量是中东战事推高的油价：能源价格通过食品、燃油、运输层层传导，既抬升通胀，也迫使央行维持偏紧的货币政策，反过来继续压制内需。菲律宾这种能源净进口、消费主导的小型开放经济体，对外部冲击的承受力本就薄弱，两记重拳叠加，缓冲空间所剩无几。

## 菲律宾不是没有底牌

当然，把美国银行的预测读成“菲律宾失去长期增长故事”，也言过其实。拉维拉斯就提醒，不应因此否定其长期基本盘：年轻的人口、稳健的消费基础和不断扩张的服务业，仍是真实存在的优势。里卡福特也指出，有针对性的非现金纾困、面向最困难群体的补助与就业计划，能缓解价格压力；而技术叠加教育，是把更多人带离贫困、提升农业与制造生产率的关键杠杆。换言之，牌还在手里，问题是出牌的节奏与秩序。

## 结论：里程碑之后是考题

菲律宾升格中高收入，是一件值得记下的事，但它更像一份“入场券”而非“毕业证书”。真正的考题在后面：能否把消费独大转向制造与投资的多元驱动，能否在反腐与保进度之间找到平衡，能否在气候与油价的逆风中稳住民生。世界银行的那道门槛跨过去容易，跨过去之后怎么跑，才是马尼拉真正的难关。对东南亚而言，菲律宾的处境也是一记提醒——中等收入不是终点，增长的质量与韧性，才是下半场的胜负手。

## 延伸阅读

**中高收入国家（Upper-Middle-Income）**：世界银行按人均国民总收入划分的国家收入组别之一，2026财年门槛约为人均国民总收入4500美元（约3.2万元人民币）左右；跨过该线意味发展阶段跃升，但也可能逐步失去优惠贷款与官方发展援助的资格。

**供应链搬迁（Supply-Chain Relocation）**：指企业因成本、关税或地缘风险，将生产基地从一地转移至另一地的过程；近年部分电子与半导体产能在中国与东南亚之间重新分布，越南、马来西亚受益明显。

**厄尔尼诺（El Niño）**：赤道中东太平洋海温异常偏暖的气候现象，常导致东南亚部分地区干旱、减产并推高粮价，对消费主导型经济体的民生冲击尤为直接。

# Upgraded but underperforming: why the Philippines' growth still lags its ASEAN peers

The World Bank's upgrade of the Philippines to upper-middle-income status this July was meant to mark a new chapter for one of Southeast Asia's most closely watched emerging economies. Yet as the South China Morning Post reported on 21 September, the milestone has done little to shield Manila from the short-term pressures now weighing on growth. Sluggish public spending, delayed infrastructure, high living costs and the fallout from a probe into alleged irregularities in flood-control projects have left the Philippines looking like a regional laggard. The cheer of a rating upgrade sits awkwardly next to the cold reality of growth at the bottom of the regional table.

## The irony of the milestone

Crossing into upper-middle-income means, by gross national income per capita, the Philippines has cleared the World Bank's threshold. For successive governments, it is a "report card" they can wave at the electorate. But as SCMP-cited analysts put it bluntly, the threshold "has done little to cushion short-term growth pressures." In other words, the label changed, the pockets did not, and growth became harder. This gap between a nominal upgrade and real strain shows precisely why an income threshold is never a guarantee of growth — merely a progress report along the way.

## The numbers: the region's slowest growth

The figures are on the table. Bank of America forecasts Philippine GDP growth of just 2.5 per cent for 2026, tying with Thailand for the slowest pace in the region; at the same time, the bank raised its overall ASEAN growth forecast to 5 per cent, up from 4.7 per cent at the end of the second quarter. In its September report, the bank said the Philippines is set for "a sharp slowdown relative to 2025," and laid out a stark contrast: Singapore is projected to grow 5.1 per cent, Malaysia 5.2 per cent, Indonesia 5.3 per cent and Vietnam 8.2 per cent. As neighbours accelerate, the Philippines' slip toward the back of the pack becomes all the more conspicuous. The caution is not Bank of America's alone. Earlier this year the Asian Development Bank trimmed its Philippine forecast to 3.3 per cent, S&P Global to 2.9 per cent and the IMF to 4.1 per cent — a rare convergence of downgrades that underscores how broadly the consensus has soured on Manila's near-term momentum.

## Why the consumption engine stalled

The Philippines has long been cast as a consumption-driven economy, with a large young population and remittance-backed demand as the main engine of growth. But Jonathan Ravelas, a Filipino economist and market strategist quoted by SCMP, notes that against its ASEAN peers the country is facing a "relative slowdown" — Vietnam, Indonesia and Malaysia are benefiting far more from manufacturing expansion, supply-chain relocation and stronger foreign direct investment, while the Philippine economy "has been heavily dependent on consumption, and that engine has been tempered by higher borrowing costs, elevated living costs and slower public spending." Put simply: others are making things, the Philippines is still relying on spending; when credit is tight and prices are high, that spending engine sputters.

## The infrastructure gap: money stalled by a corruption probe

The trickier problem is public investment. Michael Ricafort, chief economist at Rizal Commercial Banking Corporation, argues that stepped-up government "catch-up spending" on infrastructure is the "missing link" that could re-accelerate growth. Yet since late 2025, investigations into the flood-control corruption scandal have visibly slowed related outlays. The irony is that most investors are less troubled by the anti-graft drive itself than by the delays it has caused. Ravelas is blunt: "Transparency and accountability are positive for investor confidence, but projects must continue moving forward. The challenge for policymakers is to clean up the system without slowing down execution." The money exists; it is stuck halfway by the probe — and that hurts growth more than the corruption alone.

## Neighbours' answer: manufacturing and relocation

Why, among ASEAN members, is the gap widening? The answer is written in industrial structure. Vietnam has ridden the global AI demand boom through exports of electronics and semiconductor components; Indonesia and Malaysia have enlarged their manufacturing base through supply-chain relocation and stronger inflows of foreign capital. The Philippines is not without manufacturing roots, yet in the last round of industrial shift it failed to capture foreign investment and factories the way its neighbours did. As regional competition shifts from "who is cheaper" to "who can make more," a consumption-tilted Philippines looks exposed. Beneath this lies a misalignment between long-standing policy priorities and the region's prevailing tide.

## Climate and oil: a double external shock

Before the domestic house is in order, external shocks pile on. SCMP notes that forecasters have warned El Niño-driven drought conditions could stretch into early 2027, threatening food prices and farm incomes — precisely as households are already strained by the cost of living. The broader geopolitical variable is the oil-price spike from the Middle East conflict: energy costs transmit through food, fuel and transport, lifting inflation and forcing the central bank to keep monetary policy tight, which in turn keeps pressing down domestic demand. As an energy-importing, consumption-led small open economy, the Philippines has thin resilience to external shocks; two punches at once leave little cushion.

## The Philippines is not without cards

Of course, reading the Bank of America forecast as the Philippines "losing its long-term growth story" overstates the case. Ravelas cautions against reading it that way, pointing to genuine long-term strengths: a young population, a robust consumer base and an expanding services sector. Ricafort, too, argues that targeted non-cash relief, aid for the poorest and job programmes can ease price pressures, while technology paired with education is the key lever to lift more people out of poverty and raise productivity in agriculture and manufacturing. In short, the cards are still in hand; the problem is the tempo and the order of play.

## Conclusion: the test comes after the milestone

The Philippines' rise to upper-middle-income status is worth noting, but it reads more like an "entry ticket" than a "graduation certificate." The real test lies ahead: can it shift from consumption-dependence toward a diversified drive built on manufacturing and investment, can it balance anti-corruption with project delivery, and can it stabilise living standards against the headwinds of climate and oil? Clearing the World Bank threshold was the easy part; how it runs afterward is Manila's true ordeal. For Southeast Asia, the Philippines' predicament is also a reminder — middle income is not the finish line, and the quality and resilience of growth decide the second half.

## Further reading

**Upper-Middle-Income**: A World Bank country-income group classified by gross national income per capita; around the 2026 fiscal-year threshold of roughly US$4,500 per capita. Crossing it signals a development leap but may gradually cost access to concessional loans and official development assistance.

**Supply-Chain Relocation**: The process by which firms move production bases from one place to another over costs, tariffs or geopolitical risk. Recent redistribution of some electronics and semiconductor capacity between China and Southeast Asia has notably benefited Vietnam and Malaysia.

**El Niño**: A climate phenomenon of anomalously warm sea-surface temperatures in the equatorial central and eastern Pacific, often bringing drought and reduced harvests to parts of Southeast Asia and pushing up food prices — hitting consumption-led economies' household welfare most directly.
