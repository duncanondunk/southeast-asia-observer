# 麻将馆开进雅加达（Jakarta）：中华文化不再只由华人自己打

## 数字：10张桌与每三人一名非华裔

据《联合早报》（Lianhe Zaobao）11月26日报道，印度尼西亚（Indonesia）雅加达和泗水今年年中兴起一股麻将热，麻将馆一间接一间地开。形态五花八门：有独立门店，有开在板式网球馆里的，有和咖啡座联手办活动的，也有酒店、餐馆划出几桌专供爱好者切磋。牌友还通过通信应用组织社群，谁家牌局“三缺一”，群里喊一声就有人补位。

9月开张的“瓷砖屋”（House of Tiles）麻将馆位于雅加达北区椰风新城，摆了10张从海外购入的自动洗牌桌，目前约有60名活跃牌友。合伙人克里斯26岁，半年前开始接触麻将，看准潮流商机与朋友合伙开店。他说投入近10亿印尼盾（约7万新元，约合38万元人民币），预计一年半至两年回本。非繁忙时段每小时收费7万5000印尼盾（约5新元，约合27元人民币），繁忙时段和周末约11万印尼盾（约7新元，约合40元人民币）。

据店里观察，来打牌的有带家人来的，也有年轻人相约来的，各占一半；年轻顾客中，每三人就有一名非华裔。这组数字不像“文化复兴”的宏大叙事，倒像一门仔细算过客单价的生意。真正值得追问的是：谁愿意为一张桌子付这个价，他们又为什么来。

## 谁在打：从家族记忆到周末约会

30多岁的上班族拉曼沙（Ramansyah）每周末都留时间打麻将，学了两个多月就被玩法和文化底蕴吸引。他是非华裔印尼（Indonesia）人，刚开始学中文，认为麻将是中华文化“不可或缺的一部分”。他的解释很实在：“很多人觉得计分很复杂，但我不觉得，一旦弄懂不同的牌色组合，剩下的就只是凑够台数和争取高分。”

16岁的高中生瓦伦西雅（Valencia）在雅加达西部格罗戈区的“鲁玛琼”（Rumajong）麻将馆学牌。这家店是她堂哥新开的。两个月前她在社交媒体上看到麻将越来越火，就请已经上手的教她；起初觉得难，多听几遍讲解就慢慢清楚了。她说家里没人打过麻将，父母知道她来玩，也说想学。

过去谈海外华人文化传承，默认主角是华人自己：华校、侨团、节庆，都是族群内部的事。现在的主角变了——是外人主动进门，愿意为一门源自中国的游戏腾出周末。这不是“华人把自己的东西传下去”，而是“别人愿意把它拿过去用”。两件事的方向完全相反。

## “老师”这个工种：把文化拆成一两周能学会的规则

为了降低门槛，一些麻将馆设了被称为“老师”的指导员。19岁的加布里埃尔·雷耶斯·穆利亚（Gabriel Reyes Mulya）在“瓷砖屋”带新手。他说，新玩家一般一两周内就能熟悉牌色和“碰”“杠”等概念，再往后才学难度更高的搓牌组合。起步时靠熟背平和、碰、吃等和牌组合，上手之后就不再死记，而是看自己的手牌和别人的弃牌，思考要不要做十三幺、七对子、大三元；年轻玩家有些一周就能上手。

有意思的是，加布里埃尔自己也不是从小会打。小时候在家庭聚会上看长辈打过，但没学；到麻将馆工作后才真正入门，因为接触过其他桌面游戏，学起来没有想象中难。

这个细节比“麻将很受欢迎”更重要。“老师”是一个可复制的工种：它把含混的“传统”拆成牌色、组合、台数、策略四个步骤，让任何人都能在两周内取得可感知的进步。文化不再靠血缘自动传递，而是靠一套可教授、可付费、可复制的教学产品。这才是麻将馆和节庆展演的根本区别。

## 群组里的“三缺一”：社媒把人拉回同一张桌

克里斯说，印尼人喜欢跟风赶潮流，所以麻将热起来得很快。但他的解释不止于潮流：“现在的人把时间花在人工智能和社交媒体上，人与人之间的互动少了实实在在的联系。打麻将让我们找回这种人与人的实际接触。”

这话说得直白，也带着一点讽刺。重建面对面的办法，靠的恰恰是通信应用里的群组通知：谁“三缺一”，谁报名；店里再用社媒群组发布活动和比赛，维持牌友兴趣。这不是社交媒体与线下生活的对抗，而是把社交媒体的组织能力导流到一张实体桌前。

对研究者来说，这一点值得记下。社群不是麻将馆的附属宣传，而是留存机制。牌局是一次性的，群组让下一次牌局变得可召集；桌子租金是成本，群里的熟面孔才是资产。过去谈离散社群靠节庆维系，现在看来，一个能随时喊到人的群组可能更管用。

## 汉字牌面：不用进课堂的文化接触

拉曼沙说，他喜欢学习不同的文化，刚开始学中文，而麻将作为中华文化的代表，勾起了他的兴趣。虽然部分麻将牌带有汉字，他并不觉得难学。瓦伦西雅的堂哥开店、她跟着学，走的也是同一条路径：先被社交场景吸引，再顺手接触牌面上的汉字和中、发、白这类符号。

这是一种低门槛的文化接触——不要求你会说汉语，不要求你填表报名“文化课程”，只要你能认出牌、愿意凑齐四人坐下。牌面上的汉字成了日常物件，而不是课本上的生词。

但要克制结论。会打麻将不等于认同中国，更不等于理解中国。把牌桌上的好奇直接说成“文化认同”，是偷懒。真正的变化在于：中华文化从被展示的物件，变成了被使用的工具。人们不是为了“了解中华文化”才来打牌，而是为了打牌才顺带接触了中华文化——这个顺序，恰恰是文化传播最不容易被设计出来的部分。

## 代际：家里没人打，反而到馆里学

“瓷砖屋”的另一位合伙人、23岁的黄小琳（Prisca）说，打麻将能增进不同世代的交流。年轻一代对这门传统游戏可能只是略有耳闻，却从没接触过，因此感到好奇；年长一代则能一边教年轻人，一边分享自己对游戏的记忆。

瓦伦西雅的情况正好是这句话的注脚：家里没有人打过麻将，她是在商业场馆里第一次摸牌；父母听说后，也说想学。传统的家庭传承链条断了，补位的是按小时收费的桌子和愿意讲解的“老师”。

这对侨务工作有启发。我们习惯把传承想到“原汁原味”：祖辈怎么打，后代就该怎么打；可现实是，很多印尼家庭里这条线早就断了。与其追问年轻人还记不记得长辈的打法，不如问他们愿不愿意在周末花几十分钟、按小时付钱坐下来。愿意坐下，才有下一步。

## 潮流账本：比板式网球便宜，但仍是一笔生意

雅加达对“潮流型休闲”并不陌生。2025年，板式网球（padel）在这里迅速走红，一块球场造价约6亿至16亿印尼盾（约25万至67万元人民币），每小时租金40万至60万印尼盾（约150至230元人民币），约一年就能回本。但热潮也激化了阶层观感：雅加达科多亚南区居民曾反对把唯一的足球场改建为板式网球馆，在墙上写下“体育不是专属于富人”的标语，最终政府取消改建。

相比之下，麻将馆每小时7万5000至11万印尼盾的收费只是板式网球的一个零头，而且大多挤在咖啡馆、餐馆和板式网球馆的夹层里，不必占用公共操场。这种“寄生式”扩张降低了空间冲突，也降低了参与门槛。但11万印尼盾的周末价对普通印尼人仍不是随手可得的小钱，说它已经完全平民化，还为时过早。

黄小琳对潮流的判断倒是清醒：就像其他棋盘游戏，玩家人数不会像最热时那么多，但也不会完全消失，真正喜欢的人还会继续玩。这话听着像老板的客气话，却符合休闲市场的常识。热潮退去后留下来的是社群还是空桌，取决于那60名活跃牌友是否变成数百名常客，也取决于“老师”能否持续把新手送进牌局。

## 谁在输出：市场比仪式更能把人留下

把这件事放到“中华文化走出去”的框架里看，有点反常识。我们熟悉的路径是孔子学院、文化节、展演、讲座——它们都有一个隐含前提：对方要同意“这是文化”，愿意以学习者的身份坐进教室。麻将馆不一样，它不要求任何身份表态，只要求凑齐四个人，并且愿意为一小时付钱。

市场把文化变成了可消费的日常，反而可能走得更远。一个非华裔青年为了“三缺一”打开群组，为一局牌记住几个汉字，听年长牌友讲起旧时家族聚会——这些接触没有仪式感，却重复发生。交往理论里有个朴素道理：持续接触比一次性展示更能改变认知。牌桌恰好提供了持续接触的理由。

但也别把生意浪漫化。商业逻辑只挑最容易上手、最有社交回报的那部分文化，历史、语境和更复杂的意义不一定被带上桌。麻将热可能留下一个跨族群的休闲社群，也可能在下一波潮流来临时散去。衡量文化传播成效，不该只看办了多少场活动，而要看有多少非华裔愿意为它安排一个周六下午。

## 延伸阅读

**麻将（Mahjong）**：四人骨牌游戏，牌面含汉字与花色组合，各地规则差异很大；本文关注它作为社交与文化载体的功能。

**跨族群互动（Cross-ethnic Interaction）**：不同族群在日常休闲场景中形成的持续接触，区别于以制度、仪式或课堂为媒介的交流。

**文化传承（Cultural Inheritance）**：技艺与记忆在代际之间的传递；本文关注家庭链条断裂后，商业空间如何补位。

# Mahjong parlours in Jakarta: Chinese culture no longer played only by Chinese people

## The numbers: ten tables and one non-Chinese player in three

According to Lianhe Zaobao's 26 November report, a mahjong craze took hold in Jakarta and Surabaya in the middle of this year, with parlours opening one after another. The formats vary: standalone venues, tables tucked inside padel clubs, regular events run with cafés, and corners of hotels and restaurants set aside for players. Players organise themselves through messaging apps; when a game is one short, a call in the group usually finds a fourth.

House of Tiles, which opened in September in the Kelapa Gading area of north Jakarta, has ten automatically shuffling tables bought overseas and around 60 active players. One of its partners, 26-year-old Chris, took up mahjong about six months earlier and saw a business in the trend. He says the setup cost nearly Rp1 billion (about S$70,000, ~US$55,000), with a projected payback of 18 months to two years. Off-peak play costs Rp75,000 an hour (about S$5, ~US$4); peak hours and weekends are around Rp110,000 (about S$7, ~US$5.5).

By the house's own observation, half the players come with family and half with friends, and one in three young customers is not ethnic Chinese. These are not the numbers of a "cultural renaissance". They are the numbers of a business that has worked out its average revenue per seat. The more useful question is who is willing to pay for that seat, and why they come.

## Who plays: from family memory to a weekend date

Ramansyah, a white-collar worker in his thirties, gives up his weekends for a hobby he picked up only two months ago. He is not ethnic Chinese, has just started learning Chinese, and describes mahjong as an "indispensable part of Chinese culture". His reasoning is practical rather than reverent: "Many people find the scoring complicated, but I don't. Once you understand the tile combinations, the rest is about assembling hands and chasing points."

Valencia, a 16-year-old high-school student, learns at Rumajong in Grogol, west Jakarta, a parlour her cousin recently opened. She saw mahjong becoming popular on social media two months ago and asked a friend who already played to teach her. It seemed hard at first; after hearing the rules explained a few times, it began to make sense. No one in her family had ever played. When her parents heard where she was going, they said they wanted to learn too.

When overseas Chinese cultural work is discussed, the default protagonist is Chinese people themselves: language schools, associations, festivals, all matters internal to the community. Here the protagonist has changed. Outsiders walk in on their own initiative and are willing to spend a weekend on a game of Chinese origin. This is not a community passing its own heritage down; it is other people picking it up for use. The direction of travel is the opposite.

## The "teacher": turning culture into rules you can learn in a fortnight

To lower the barrier, some parlours employ instructors known as "teachers". Gabriel Reyes Mulya, 19, coaches beginners at House of Tiles. He says new players generally grasp the suits and concepts such as pung and kong within one or two weeks, before moving on to more demanding combinations. At the start they memorise winning patterns — a basic hand, pungs and chows; once they are comfortable they stop reciting and start reading their own tiles and other players' discards to decide whether to aim for Thirteen Orphans, Seven Pairs or Three Great Dragons. Some younger players manage it in a week.

Gabriel was not born knowing the game either. He watched relatives play at family gatherings as a child but never learned. He picked it up only after working at the parlour, and his experience with other tabletop games made it less daunting than expected.

That detail matters more than the headline claim that mahjong is popular. The "teacher" is a reproducible job: it breaks an amorphous "tradition" into four teachable steps — suits, combinations, scoring hands, strategy — and gives anyone a tangible sense of progress within a fortnight. Culture is no longer transmitted automatically by blood; it is delivered as a teachable, paid, replicable product. That is the fundamental difference between a parlour and a festival performance.

## "One more player" in the group chat: social media bringing people back to the table

Chris says Indonesians like following trends, which is why mahjong took off so quickly. But his explanation goes further: "People now spend their time on AI and social media, and face-to-face interaction has lost substance. Mahjong helps us recover that real contact."

That is both plain-spoken and faintly ironic. The way to rebuild face-to-face contact runs precisely through messaging-app groups: someone posts that a game is one short, someone else signs up, and the house uses its own group to announce events and competitions that keep players engaged. This is not social media fighting offline life; it is social media's organisational capacity being channelled back into a physical table.

For anyone studying this, the point is worth recording. The group is not a publicity accessory to the parlour; it is the retention mechanism. A single game is a one-off; the group makes the next game callable. Table rental is a cost; familiar faces in the chat are an asset. Diaspora communities used to be described as held together by festivals. A group that can summon four players on short notice may do more work.

## Chinese characters on the tiles: culture without a classroom

Ramansyah says he enjoys learning about different cultures, has begun studying Chinese, and found mahjong compelling as a representative of Chinese culture. Some tiles carry Chinese characters, but he does not find them difficult. Valencia's path was similar: her cousin opened a parlour, she followed along, and the encounter with the characters on the tiles — zhong, fa, bai — came after she was already drawn in by the social setting.

This is low-threshold cultural contact. It does not require you to speak Chinese, or to register for a "culture class". It requires you to recognise a few tiles and agree to sit down with three other people. The characters on the tiles become everyday objects rather than vocabulary-list entries.

The conclusion should nevertheless be kept in check. Knowing how to play mahjong is not the same as identifying with China, still less understanding it. To call the curiosity at a gaming table "cultural identity" is laziness. The real change is that Chinese culture has moved from being an object on display to a tool in use. People do not play in order to learn about Chinese culture; they encounter Chinese culture because they play. That sequence is precisely the part cultural promotion cannot easily design.

## Generations: nobody at home plays, so they learn in a parlour

Prisca, 23, the other partner at House of Tiles, says mahjong creates exchange between generations. Younger people may have heard of the traditional game but never touched it, and are curious; older players can teach them while sharing memories of the game from their own lives.

Valencia is a footnote to that sentence. Nobody in her family played; she first handled tiles in a commercial venue. When her parents heard about it, they said they wanted to learn as well. Where the family chain of transmission has broken, a table charged by the hour and an instructor willing to explain have stepped into the gap.

That carries a lesson for diaspora work. We tend to imagine inheritance as authenticity: whatever the grandparents did, the descendants should do the same. In reality that line broke long ago in many Indonesian families. Rather than asking whether young people still remember how their elders played, it is more useful to ask whether they are willing to sit down on a weekend and pay by the hour. Only once they sit down does anything else become possible.

## The trend ledger: cheaper than padel, still a business

Jakarta is no stranger to trend-driven leisure. Padel took off in 2025: a court cost roughly Rp600 million to Rp1.6 billion (about US$33,000–89,000), renting for Rp400,000 to Rp600,000 an hour (~US$25–40), with payback in about a year. The craze also sharpened perceptions of class. Residents of South Kedoya opposed converting the only local football pitch into padel courts and painted "sport is not only for the rich" on the wall; the city eventually cancelled the plan.

By comparison, Rp75,000–110,000 an hour for mahjong is a fraction of padel's price, and most parlours slot into cafés, restaurants and padel clubs rather than consuming public playing fields. This parasitic pattern of expansion lowers both spatial conflict and the barrier to entry. But Rp110,000 on a weekend is still not loose change for an ordinary Indonesian, so it is too early to call the craze fully democratic.

Prisca is clear-eyed about the cycle: like other board games, the player base will not stay at its peak, but neither will it vanish; those who genuinely enjoy it will keep playing. That sounds like a shopkeeper's polite remark, yet it matches how leisure markets behave. What survives the trend — a cross-ethnic community or empty tables — depends on whether those 60 active players become several hundred regulars, and whether the "teachers" keep feeding beginners into games.

## Who is doing the exporting: the market keeps people longer than ceremonies

Seen through the lens of "Chinese culture going global", there is something counter-intuitive here. The familiar route runs through Confucius Institutes, cultural festivals, performances and lectures, all of which carry an implicit condition: the other party must agree that "this is culture" and accept the role of learner in a classroom. A mahjong parlour asks no such declaration of identity. It asks only for four players willing to pay for an hour.

The market turns culture into consumable everyday life, and that may carry it further. A non-Chinese young person opens a chat because a game is one short, memorises a few characters for the sake of a hand, and hears an older player recall family gatherings from decades ago. None of this feels ceremonial, but it repeats. The plainest insight from contact theory is that sustained interaction changes perceptions more than one-off displays. The gaming table supplies a reason for sustained interaction.

Nor should the business be romanticised. Commercial logic selects the easiest and most socially rewarding parts of a culture; its history, context and harder meanings do not necessarily come to the table. The craze may leave behind a cross-ethnic leisure community, or dissolve with the next trend. The measure of cultural reach should not be the number of events staged, but the number of non-Chinese people willing to give it a Saturday afternoon.
