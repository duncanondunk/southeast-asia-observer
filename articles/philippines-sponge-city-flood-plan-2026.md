# 菲律宾（Philippines）「海绵城市」治水：一场迟到了数十年的防洪自救

2026 年夏末，西南季风裹挟连场暴雨横扫吕宋岛，大马尼拉多家医院地下室进水、轻轨停运、中小学校停课数周。当积水退去，菲律宾政府抛出一个听起来颇为「未来感」的方案——把首都变成一座能吸水、能储水、能缓释雨水的「海绵城市」。据《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》报道，大马尼拉发展署（MMDA）已圈定 39 个蓄滞洪点位，其中 5 个优先工程要在 2027 年 6 月前完工，亚洲基础设施投资银行（亚投行，AIIB）为之承诺 2 亿美元（约14.4亿元人民币）的初期资金。表面看这是一场技术升级，骨子里却是一份迟到了数十年的治水欠账。

## 从「对抗洪水」到「管理雨水」

传统的菲律宾防洪，靠的是混凝土防洪墙、深挖的排水渠和一座座抽水站——思路是「把水尽快赶走」。但 2026 年的暴雨告诉马尼拉（Manila），水已经快得赶不走了。大马尼拉发展署主席唐·阿特斯把新思路讲得很直白：过去我们总想「控制水、对抗水」，但气候变化来了，极端暴雨不该再被对抗，而该被「管理」。所谓海绵城市，就是用地下蓄水池、透水人行道、下沉式花园和人工湖，让雨水降落后先在原地「歇一歇」，别一股脑儿同时灌进本就脆弱的排水系统。新加坡、中国、荷兰、英国早已把这套基于自然的方案落地，菲律宾算是后进生。

## 39 个蓄滞点与一个 2 亿美元赌注

这笔账算得并不含糊。大马尼拉发展署最初筛出 107 个候选点位，经过近三年与地方政府、土地所有者和专家的磋商，砍到 39 个。5 个优先工程分别是亚银那洛营地、米里亚姆学院、黎刹纪念体育场、拉贾苏莱曼公园和菲律宾大学迪里曼校区下沉花园，目标 2027 年 6 月前完工。亚投行承诺的 2 亿美元（约14.4亿元人民币）来自一项为期三年、由亚投行与世界银行共同资助、荷兰皇家哈斯康宁德和（Royal HaskoningDHV）执行的可行性研究。最大的单体工程藏在甲米地一处营区：一座 5000 平方米、深约 10 米的蓄滞设施，可临时吞下约 5 万立方米雨水。阿特斯也坦白，39 个点位大概率要跨过小马科斯任期，「我们无法全部做完」。

## 灾难是催化剂：2026 年季风之夏

没有哪场灾难比 2026 年的季风之夏更能逼出这份方案。根据菲律宾国家减灾委（NDRRMC）9 月初的通报，西南季风叠加「路易斯」「玛梅」「能能」等热带气旋，已造成 39 人死亡、20 人受伤、3 人失踪，约 940 万人（270 万户家庭）受灾；基础设施损失 62.7 亿比索（约7.9亿元人民币）、农业损失 28.4 亿比索（约3.6亿元人民币），政府已拨出 18.5 亿比索（约2.3亿元人民币）援助。更早的 8 月，中吕宋八省连下暴雨，800 万人受灾、基础设施与农业损失超 84 亿比索（约10.6亿元人民币）。总统小马科斯的一句话点破了旧账：「我们几十年前就有总体规划，但什么都没做，直到现在才开始执行。」

## 腐败的阴影：同一批管道正在被调查

更刺眼的是，承接海绵城市建设的，恰恰是近年深陷贪腐丑闻的那套防洪工程管道。菲律宾监察署（Ombudsman）已就异常防洪项目对多名国会议员和公共工程官员提起渎职与掠夺罪指控；前邦板牙省众议员冈萨雷斯因 6.11 亿比索（约0.77亿元人民币）防洪合同被控三项渎职；公共工程和公路部（DPWH）更以「一次出局」政策撤换了达沃的防洪官员。换言之，要建海绵城市的部门，和正被查的，是同一批人、同一套招标链条。公众愤怒尚未平息，蓝图就先背上了信任赤字。

## 无法承诺「无洪城市」的诚实

阿特斯在公布方案时，罕见地给公众降了预期：「我们无法保证大马尼拉从此无洪。」他说，你看看越来越强的降雨，哪怕别国砸了钱建蓄滞设施也仍会淹。能担保的，只是这些工程「能有效地缓解洪水」。这种坦率反倒戳破了发展中国家抗旱排涝叙事里常见的浮夸：把海绵城市当成万能药，既不符合气候科学的现实，也会透支本就脆弱的公共信任。承认局限，或许才是这套方案最靠谱的起点。

## 中国方案、中国钱：地缘政治里的技术倒灌

最耐人寻味的，是这笔买卖里的「中国痕迹」。海绵城市这一概念，本就是中国在国内城镇化中大规模铺开、并向「一带一路」（Belt and Road）伙伴推广的治水范式；而出钱的亚投行，总部在北京（Beijing）、由中方倡议发起。于是出现了一幕微妙的倒挂：马尼拉与北京在南海（South China Sea）剑拔弩张、相互召见大使、海上对峙频传，但在首都治水这件民生大事上，菲律宾却接过了中国原创的城市理念，又接下了中国主导的多边银行支票。技术合作与地缘对抗，竟在同一座城市里并行不悖。

## 蓝图能否浇成混凝土

回到根本问题：纸面规划能不能真的浇成混凝土？菲律宾不缺蓝图——上世纪三四十年代就有防洪框架，2012 年阿基诺政府那份 3510 亿比索（约443亿元人民币）的总体规划，后来被杜特尔特政府接手，同样束之高阁。如今旧规划重启，叠加海绵城市新概念与亚投行资金，算是三届政府欠账的一次总清算。但土地权属冲突、私有地块难征、路权（right-of-way）纠纷，以及那桩尚未了结的贪腐案，都可能让 39 个点位长期停留在「研究报告、点位清单和预算草案」阶段。下一次季风来的时候，马尼拉人想看的，不是又一份漂亮的演示文稿，而是真的不再淹到膝盖的水。

## 延伸阅读

**海绵城市（Sponge City）**：以透水铺装、下沉绿地、蓄水池等基于自然的设施，让城市就地吸纳、蓄滞和缓释雨水，替代单纯「快排」的传统排水思路。

**亚洲基础设施投资银行（Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, AIIB）**：总部位于北京、由中国倡议发起的多边开发银行，重点为亚洲基建与互联互通提供融资，菲律宾防洪项目即其一例。

**西南季风（Southwest Monsoon / Habagat）**：每年下半年影响菲律宾的季节性环流，带来持续强降雨，是该国城市洪涝的主要气候成因。

# The Philippines turns to 'sponge cities' to tame its floods

In late summer 2026, the southwest monsoon swept wave after wave of torrential rain across Luzon. In Metro Manila, hospital basements flooded, the light rail halted, and schools suspended classes for weeks. When the water receded, the Philippine government floated a deceptively futuristic fix: turn the capital into a "sponge city" that can absorb, store, and slowly release rainwater. According to Nikkei Asia, the Metropolitan Manila Development Authority (MMDA) has identified 39 flood-catchment sites, with five priority projects targeted for completion by June 2027, backed by an initial $200 million commitment from the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB). On the surface this is a technical upgrade; underneath, it is a flood-control debt decades in the arrears.

## From fighting water to managing rain

Traditional Philippine flood control relies on concrete seawalls, deep drainage canals, and rows of pumping stations — the logic being to flush water away as fast as possible. But the 2026 deluge showed Manila that water now moves faster than the drains. MMDA chair Romando "Don" Artes put the new thinking plainly: in the past we wanted to "control and fight" water, but with climate change, extreme downpours should no longer be fought — they should be managed. A sponge city uses underground cisterns, permeable sidewalks, sunken gardens and lagoons so that rain first "pauses" where it lands, instead of rushing all at once into an already fragile drainage system. Singapore, China, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom have long deployed such nature-based solutions; the Philippines is a latecomer.

## 39 catchments and a $200 million bet

The arithmetic is specific. MMDA started with 107 candidate sites and, after nearly three years of consultation with local governments, landowners and experts, trimmed them to 39. The five priorities — Camp Aguinaldo, Miriam College, Rizal Memorial Stadium, Rajah Sulayman Park and the UP Diliman sunken garden — are due before June 2027. The $200 million AIIB commitment stems from a three-year feasibility study co-funded by AIIB and the World Bank and executed by the Dutch firm Royal HaskoningDHV. The largest single structure hides inside a Cavite camp: a 5,000-square-metre, roughly 10-metre-deep catchment able to temporarily hold about 50,000 cubic metres of rainwater. Artes also conceded that the 39-site programme will almost certainly spill beyond President Marcos's term — "we cannot possibly finish all of them."

## Disaster as catalyst: the monsoon summer of 2026

No disaster pressed this plan into being like the monsoon summer of 2026. Per the National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Council in early September, the southwest monsoon compounded by typhoons Luis, Maymay and Neneng had left 39 dead, 20 injured and three missing, and affected some 9.4 million people (2.7 million families). Infrastructure damage reached ₱6.27 billion (about US$110 million) and agricultural losses ₱2.84 billion (about US$50 million), with ₱1.85 billion (about US$32 million) already released in aid. Earlier in August, monsoon rains across eight Central Luzon provinces hit 8 million people, with infrastructure and farm losses exceeding ₱8.4 billion (about US$147 million). President Marcos laid the old failure bare: "We've had master plans for decades, but we never did anything — until now."

## The shadow of corruption: the same pipeline under investigation

What makes this awkward is that the very pipeline building the sponge city is the one mired in a flood-control graft scandal. The Ombudsman has filed graft and plunder charges against lawmakers and public-works officials over anomalous projects; former Pampanga representative Aurelio Gonzales Jr. was charged with three counts of graft over ₱611 million (about US$10.7 million) in flood contracts, and the Department of Public Works and Highways sacked its Davao flood-control officials under a one-strike policy. In other words, the agency rolling out the sponge city is the same one under probe, on the same procurement chain. Public anger has not subsided, and the blueprint already carries a credibility deficit.

## The honesty of not promising a flood-free city

When unveiling the plan, Artes unusually lowered expectations: "We cannot guarantee flood-free Metro Manila." He noted that with ever-stronger rainfall, even countries that spent heavily on catchments still flood. What he could promise was that the projects "will be efficient in mitigating the flood." That candour punctures the usual developing-world bravado about drought and drainage: touting sponge cities as a panacea neither matches climate science nor survives contact with already-fragile public trust. Admitting limits may be the most credible starting point the programme has.

## A Chinese idea, Chinese money: technology flows against the geopolitical tide

The most intriguing thread is the "Chinese fingerprint" on the deal. The sponge-city concept itself is something China scaled up domestically and pushed to Belt and Road partners as a flood-management paradigm. The bank writing the cheque, AIIB, is headquartered in Beijing and was initiated by China. The result is a telling inversion: Manila and Beijing trade summonses of ambassadors and stare each other down in the South China Sea, yet on the mundane business of keeping the capital dry, the Philippines has taken up China's home-grown urban idea and accepted a cheque from China-led multilateral bank. Technical cooperation and geopolitical rivalry run in parallel, in the same city.

## Will the blueprint become concrete

The fundamental question remains: can the paper plan actually become concrete? The Philippines is no stranger to blueprints — a flood framework existed as far back as the 1930s and 1940s, and the ₱351 billion (about US$6.2 billion) master plan approved under Aquino in 2012 was inherited by Duterte and likewise left on the shelf. Now the old plan is revived, bolted onto the sponge-city concept and financed by AIIB money — a settling of accounts three administrations owed. But land-ownership conflicts, privately held parcels that are hard to acquire, right-of-way disputes, and an unresolved graft case could keep the 39 sites stuck at "study, site list and draft budget." The next time the monsoon arrives, what Manileños want to see is not another slick PowerPoint, but water that no longer reaches their knees.

## Further reading

**Sponge City**: An urban flood-management paradigm using permeable paving, sunken green space and storage tanks to absorb, retain and slowly release rainwater on-site, replacing the traditional "drain it fast" approach.

**Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB)**: A Beijing-headquartered, China-initiated multilateral development bank that finances Asian infrastructure and connectivity; the Philippine flood project is one example.

**Southwest Monsoon (Habagat)**: The seasonal circulation that drenches the Philippines in the second half of each year, the principal climatic driver of the country's urban flooding.
