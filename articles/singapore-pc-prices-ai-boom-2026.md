# AI 热潮推高新加坡 PC 价格：当算力抢走消费者的内存

2026 年第二季度，新加坡的电脑买家遭遇了一场“价格休克”。研究公司 IDC 的数据显示，受 AI 数据中心扩张挤占关键零部件供给影响，当地个人电脑平均售价同比最高上涨约四成。普通笔记本均价从约 1300 新元升至 1650 新元（约 8900 元人民币），台式机涨 28%，游戏主机涨幅更达 39%。一台笔记本的涨价，背后是一条从硅谷算力到东南亚卖场的漫长链条，也折射出 AI 红利在社会化分配中的隐秘倾斜。

## 价格曲线：一台笔记本半年涨了四分之一

涨幅并非均匀分布。据 IDC 亚太分析师何锦伟（Ho Jin Wei）分析，本轮涨价主因是记忆体（RAM）短缺，业内甚至戏称为“RAMageddon”。普通 Windows 笔记本（16GB 内存、512GB 固态硬盘）均价升至 1650 新元；普通台式机从约 1100 新元涨至 1410 新元（约 7600 元人民币）；游戏台式机从 1330 新元跃至近 1860 新元（约 1 万元人民币），游戏笔记本也涨 17% 至 2890 新元。苹果则宣布 Mac 与 iPad 全线提价 15% 至 25%。在森林茂广场（Sim Lim Square）的电子卖场，经销商反映单条 32GB DDR5 内存价格从 2025 年 8 月的 195 新元涨至 2026 年的 763 新元，近乎四倍；1TB 固态硬盘从 149 新元翻倍至 328 新元（约 1770 元人民币）。

## 资本开支的溢出：AI 基建如何推高整机价格

价格上涨的根源不在消费端，而在算力端。全球企业正以前所未有的规模扩建 AI 数据中心，对 DRAM、NAND 与高端显卡的需求呈指数级膨胀。对记忆体厂商而言，卖给 AI 基建的利润率远高于卖给 PC 厂商，产能因此大幅倾斜。IDC 预计，这种高价位将延续至 2027 年，真正缓解恐怕要等到 2028 年及以后。换句话说，AI 的资本开支正透过零部件市场，悄悄外溢为普通消费者的账单。当科技巨头以数十亿美元抢购 GPU 与内存，终端用户并不在谈判桌旁，却被计入了最终的价格。

## “RAMageddon”：存储芯片周期的结构性转折

存储芯片素以强周期著称，但这一轮有所不同。过去价格起落多由供需错配驱动，如今则叠加了“需求的结构性迁移”——产能被长期、刚性地绑定到 AI 基础设施。英伟达 5060 系列显卡涨 44%，从 572 新元升至 824 新元（约 4450 元人民币）。当产线为更高利润的客户让路，消费级市场的“余量”被持续压缩。这已不是普通周期，而是一次分配次序的改写：算力优先，消费次之。对依赖进口整机的市场而言，这种优先级一旦固化，价格的刚性也会随之增强。

## 矿井里的金丝雀：新加坡为何最敏感

新加坡像是全球电子价格“矿井里的金丝雀”。它几乎不从事整机制造，极度依赖进口，市场价格高度透明且传导迅速。任何上游供给的风吹草动，都会在新加坡的标价牌上第一时间显现。正因如此，新加坡的涨价曲线，实则是全球存储与算力供需的一面镜子——它最先“感冒”，也最先预示其他地区即将到来的价格压力。对研究者而言，新加坡是一块天然的观测屏：读懂这里的 PC 标价，便大致读到了全球供应链的体温。

## 成本转嫁：企业与消费者的两本账

成本如何分摊，取决于议价能力。大企业能以批量采购与长期合约对冲，甚至借机升级 AI 设备；零售小店则被迫承受库存耗尽后的高价补货。森林茂广场四家经销商告诉《海峡时报》，2026 年销量较 2025 年下滑两成至八成，二手笔记本转售商反而因企业淘汰设备而受益。消费者的应对是推迟换机、转向二手；企业则继续采购。两本账之间，是同一轮涨价的冷暖不均。价格信号在此分化：一端是被迫节衣缩食的家庭，一端是逆势扩张的算力资本。

## 双重电子市场：制造繁荣与消费承压

放在东南亚版图中，这幅图景更显分裂。一边是槟城、柔佛等地借 AI 超级周期涌入的制造业投资与数据中心建设，电子产业资本开支火热；另一边是终端消费者面对更贵的笔记本与更长的换机周期。同一个“电子东南亚”，制造端与消费端走了两条相反的斜率。算力的繁荣未必等同民生的宽裕，产业红利与货架价格之间，横着一道分配鸿沟。区域内的电子制造越强，消费端为此付出的隐性成本，反而越难被叙事所看见。

## 红利失衡：算力所有者与终端用户

更深层的张力在于红利归属。AI 浪潮的赢家是掌握算力与数据中心的所有者，他们以更高价格锁定了稀缺的记忆体产能；而埋单的，是被迫接受涨价的普通用户与学生。当稀缺资源向高利润环节集中，终端消费者的选择权被悄然稀释。技术愈是普惠，其上游成本却愈可能以隐蔽方式回流到使用者身上——这恰是“数字经济”叙事常被忽略的背面。算力所有者享受效率红利，终端用户却承担供给稀缺的溢价，二者并不对称。

## 技术红利的社会化账单：谁来买单

市场会自我调节吗？短期内恐怕不会。IDC 判断显著缓和要等到 2028 年之后，而新加坡政府并无直接干预零售定价的政策工具，更可能的是通过补贴、数字普惠或技能培训缓冲冲击。真正值得追问的是：当一项技术的红利高度集中于少数人，其成本却社会化地分散给全体用户，我们该如何衡量它的“普惠”成色？新加坡的 PC 涨价，是一记微小的、却清晰的提醒——技术红利与成本，从来都不是对称分配的。

## 延伸阅读

**DRAM（动态随机存取存储器）**：即电脑记忆体（RAM），用于临时存取运行数据；AI 数据中心大规模采购使其供应趋紧，是本轮 PC 涨价的核心推手。

**AI 数据中心（AI Data Centre）**：部署大量 GPU 与服务器、提供 AI 算力的基础设施；其扩张挤占了消费级存储与显卡产能。

**森林茂广场（Sim Lim Square）**：新加坡知名电子卖场，本地经销商与价格变化的观测点。

# AI Boom Sends Singapore PC Prices Soaring: When Compute Steals Consumers' Memory

In the second quarter of 2026, PC buyers in Singapore got a sticker shock. According to research firm IDC, average personal-computer prices rose by as much as 40 per cent year-on-year, as the expansion of AI data centres squeezed supplies of key components. A typical laptop's average price climbed from about S$1,300 to S$1,650 (about US$1,220); desktops rose 28 per cent, and gaming rigs jumped 39 per cent. Behind one laptop's price tag lies a long chain running from Silicon Valley compute to a Southeast Asian shop floor—and a hidden tilt in how the AI dividend is distributed across society.

## The price curve: a quarter added in half a year

The increases are not evenly spread. IDC Asia Pacific analyst Ho Jin Wei attributes the surge mainly to a memory (RAM) shortage so acute that the industry has nicknamed it "RAMageddon." A standard Windows laptop (16GB RAM, 512GB SSD) now averages S$1,650; a regular desktop moved from about S$1,100 to S$1,410 (about US$1,040); gaming desktops leapt from S$1,330 to nearly S$1,860 (about US$1,380), and gaming laptops rose 17 per cent to S$2,890. Apple, too, raised Mac and iPad prices by 15 to 25 per cent. At Sim Lim Square, retailers report a single 32GB DDR5 RAM kit rose from S$195 in August 2025 to S$763 (about US$144 to US$565) in 2026—nearly fourfold—while a 1TB SSD doubled from S$149 to S$328 (about US$240).

## The spillover of capex: how AI infrastructure lifts device prices

The root cause sits not in consumption but in compute. Companies worldwide are building AI data centres at an unprecedented scale, driving exponential demand for DRAM, NAND and high-end graphics cards. For memory makers, selling to AI infrastructure yields far higher margins than selling to PC vendors, so capacity has tilted sharply. IDC expects elevated prices through 2027, with meaningful relief only in 2028 or beyond. In short, AI capital expenditure is quietly spilling over, through the component market, onto ordinary consumers' bills. When tech giants spend billions securing GPUs and memory, end users are not at the negotiating table—yet they are quietly written into the final price.

## "RAMageddon": a structural turn in the memory cycle

Memory chips are famously cyclical, yet this round differs. Past swings were driven by supply–demand mismatches; now a "structural migration of demand" is layered on top—capacity is bound, long-term and rigidly, to AI infrastructure. Nvidia's 5060 series rose 44 per cent, from S$572 to S$824 (about US$610). As lines yield to higher-margin customers, the consumer segment's surplus keeps shrinking. This is no ordinary cycle but a reordering of priority: compute first, consumers second. For markets that import finished machines, once this priority hardens, the rigidity of prices hardens with it.

## The canary in the mine: why Singapore feels it first

Singapore is the "canary in the mine" for global electronics pricing. It barely assembles finished machines and depends heavily on imports; its market is transparent and transmits shocks fast. Any upstream tremor shows up on Singapore's price tags almost immediately. Precisely for this reason, Singapore's price curve is a mirror of global memory and compute supply—it catches cold first, and foreshadows the pressure other markets will soon face. For researchers, Singapore is a natural observation screen: read its PC price tags and you have read, roughly, the temperature of the global supply chain.

## Cost pass-through: two ledgers for firms and consumers

How costs are shared depends on bargaining power. Large firms hedge with volume buys and long contracts, even upgrading AI gear; small retailers absorb expensive restocking once inventories drain. Four Sim Lim Square dealers told The Straits Times their 2026 sales fell 20 to 80 per cent from 2025, while second-hand laptop resellers benefited from corporates offloading equipment. Consumers delay upgrades or turn to used machines; firms keep buying. Between the two ledgers lies the uneven warmth of the same price wave. The price signal splits here: on one side households forced to tighten belts, on the other compute capital expanding against the trend.

## A dual electronics market: booming manufacturing, squeezed consumers

In the Southeast Asian frame, the picture splits further. On one side, Penang and Johor draw AI-supercycle manufacturing investment and data-centre builds, with electronics capital expenditure on fire. On the other, end consumers face pricier laptops and longer replacement cycles. The same "electronics Southeast Asia" runs two opposite slopes. Compute prosperity need not mean household comfort; between industrial dividend and shelf prices sits a distribution gap. The stronger the region's electronics manufacturing, the less visible the hidden cost paid by consumers at the far end.

## The dividend imbalance: compute owners versus end users

The deeper tension is who keeps the dividend. AI's winners are those who own compute and data centres; they lock up scarce memory capacity at higher prices. Those who pay are ordinary users and students forced to accept the increase. As scarce resources concentrate in high-margin segments, end consumers' choice is quietly diluted. The more "inclusive" a technology claims to be, the more its upstream cost may flow back to users in hidden ways—the underside a "digital economy" narrative often omits. Compute owners enjoy the efficiency dividend; end users carry the scarcity premium. The two are not symmetric.

## The socialised bill of tech dividend: who pays

Will the market self-correct? Not soon. IDC sees meaningful easing only after 2028, and Singapore's government has no direct tool to set retail prices; more likely are subsidies, digital inclusion or skills training to cushion the shock. The real question: when a technology's dividend concentrates in few hands while its cost disperses socially across all users, how do we weigh its "inclusive" credentials? Singapore's PC price rise is a small but clear reminder—tech dividends and costs are never symmetrically distributed.

**AI Data Centre (人工智能数据中心)**: Infrastructure deploying large numbers of GPUs and servers to provide AI compute; its expansion has crowded out consumer-grade memory and graphics-card capacity.

**Sim Lim Square (森林茂广场)**: Singapore's well-known electronics mall and an observation point for local retailers and price shifts.
