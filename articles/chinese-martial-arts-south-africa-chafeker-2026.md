# 中国功夫扎根南非：一种"润物无声"的文化出海

南非武术名家查飞葛习武三十年，将中国功夫从银幕符号变为南非青少年可习练的本土技艺，并以武为桥连接中南人文交流。这提示我们：文化出海最耐久之物，往往是功夫、中医、中文这类"润物无声"的文明载体。

## 从爱好到志业：三十年的功夫路

南非武术名家查飞葛与中国功夫的缘分，始于二十世纪九十年代。那时他还是个五六岁的孩子，祖父从中国带回的礼物——李小龙的电影、功夫裤、老式功夫鞋——给了他最初的震撼。三十年后，他已是南非精武青年功夫队总教练，也是首位入选世界动作武术名人堂的南非人。

真正令人侧目的不只是时间之长，更是方向之变。上世纪九十年代，"功夫"对多数南非人而言是个陌生词，常被人误认作空手道。而今天，查飞葛在西开普省带出一百三十多名学生，在豪登省也有三十余名，并计划向夸祖鲁—纳塔尔省拓展。他每年在开普敦举办的赛事，能吸引来自八个非洲国家的一百五十多名运动员。一个银幕上的猎奇符号，已长成南非青少年可习练的本土技艺。

## 功夫为何能在非洲扎根

查飞葛自己的答案朴素得近乎老套：是功夫教会了他坚持。马步站得腿疼背疼，山路要一步步爬。但更深层的理由在于结构。南非并不缺少需要方向、纪律与归属感的青年；功夫提供的恰是这些，且不是靠说教，而是靠训练——尊师、耐性、对他人的责任。"真正的功夫，"他说，"不是打败对手，而是让你获得掌控自己的力。"这句话之所以能跨越山海，正因为它回应了一种本地需求。

这一点值得驻足。我们太习惯用国家工程来解释中国文化"走出去"：孔子学院、外宣媒体、峰会头条。它们当然重要。但查飞葛现象提示我们，最持久的传递往往由个体而非机构完成。开普敦的一个男孩因一部电影入门，被一位用中医治好他伤病的中国师父点醒，此后余生都在把这门技艺传给下一代。文化出海，是因为有人爱上了它。

## 不止拳脚：武术、中医与中文的连缀

对查飞葛而言，功夫从来不只是打斗。2009年备赛受伤，一位中国师父用中医为他调理，这扇门后，是气功、太极与疗愈的整片文明世界。他后来前往江西南昌学习中医，如今仍在攻读中医方向的硕士学位。他的协会每月还请中文老师来武馆授课，好让学生在赴中国训练时能开口与人交流。

这正是文化本地化的静默智慧。技艺不是作为密封包裹抵达，而是不断分叉：武学通向医道，医道通向语言，语言通向历史——少林、霍元甲、黄飞鸿。从一部电影入门的南非学生，最后会明白中国"不是一个抽象的名字，而是有着数千年历史和文明传统的国家"。这才是最高效的软实力：不是强加，而是被自己发现。

## 文化出海的另一种范本

查飞葛也做了反向的事——把南非人带去中国。他的访问团走过北京（Beijing）、广州、西安、香港，也到过兰州、喀什、乌鲁木齐。他说："当人与人真正见面，许多误解就会减少。亲身体验比单纯听别人讲更有力量。"在中非关系常被公众简化为贷款、港口与矿物的时代，这座功夫之桥提醒我们：最稳固的交流层，始终是文明与人的层面。

对华侨华人研究者而言，这一课尤为尖锐。我们习惯用"量"来衡量文化出海——开了多少中心、播了多少节目。查飞葛的三十年却指向另一种尺度：本地扎根的深度。一门由非洲人教非洲人、送出八国一百五十名运动员的技艺，胜过十数次剪彩。中国文化的非洲未来，或许较少取决于北京"送"出什么，而更多取决于它无意间"造"出了多少个查飞葛。

## 延伸阅读

- **精武（Jingwu, Chin Woo）**：1910年创立于上海的武术团体，后走向海外，成为全球华人身体文化的重要载体。
- **民心相通（People-to-people bond）**：中国"一带一路（Belt and Road Initiative）"框架中除设施联通外的文明交流支柱，强调人与人的纽带。
- **中医（Traditional Chinese Medicine）**：以"气"与平衡为核心的传统医学体系，正随华侨网络在海外被研习与实践。

# How Chinese Martial Arts Took Root in South Africa: A Quiet Model of Cultural Going-Out

South African wushu master Chafeker has practised kung fu for three decades, turning a cinematic symbol into a locally rooted practice for South African youth and a bridge between China and Africa. His story suggests the most durable form of cultural "going-out" is the quiet, person-to-person transmission of art, medicine and language.

## From hobby to calling: three decades of kung fu

Chafeker, a South African wushu master, first met kung fu as a five- or six-year-old boy in the 1990s, through gifts his grandfather brought back from China: Bruce Lee films, kung fu trousers, old-style slippers. The shock stayed with him. Three decades later, he is head coach of the South Africa Jingwu Youth Kung Fu Team and the first South African inducted into the World Action Martial Arts Hall of Fame.

What is striking is not merely longevity but direction. In the 1990s, "kung fu" meant little to most South Africans; people confused it with karate. Today Chafeker runs more than 130 students in the Western Cape and over 30 in Gauteng, with plans to expand into KwaZulu-Natal. Every year his Cape Town tournament draws more than 150 athletes from eight African countries. A cinematic curiosity has become a lived, local practice.

## Why kung fu takes root in Africa

Chafeker's own answer is deceptively simple: kung fu taught him persistence. The horse stance is painful; the climb is slow. But the deeper reason it resonates in a society like South Africa is structural. The country has no shortage of youth who need direction, discipline and a sense of belonging. Kung fu supplies these not as sermon but as training — respect for the teacher, patience with the body, responsibility toward others. "Real kung fu," he says, "is not about defeating opponents, but about gaining control of yourself." That message travels precisely because it answers a local need.

This is the part worth pausing on. Too often we explain Chinese culture's global rise through state-led initiatives — Confucius Institutes, broadcast networks, headline summits. Those matter. But the Chafeker phenomenon suggests the most durable transmission is carried by individuals, not institutions. A boy in Cape Town learns from a film, meets a Chinese master who cures his injury with traditional medicine, and spends the rest of his life repaying the gift by teaching the next generation. Culture goes out because someone falls in love with it.

## Beyond the fists: wushu, medicine and language intertwined

Kung fu, for Chafeker, was never only about fighting. After a 2009 injury, a Chinese teacher treated him with traditional Chinese medicine; the experience opened a door to an entire civilizational world of qigong, tai chi and healing. He later studied TCM in Nanchang, Jiangxi, and is now pursuing a master's degree in the field. His association now also invites a Mandarin teacher to the gym every month, so students can speak with locals when they train in China.

Here lies the quiet genius of cultural localization. The practice does not arrive as a sealed package; it branches. Martial art leads to medicine, medicine to language, language to history — Shaolin, Huo Yuanjia, Huang Feihong. The South African student who begins with a film ends by understanding that China "is not an abstract name but a civilisation with thousands of years of history." That is soft power at its most effective: not imposed, but discovered.

## A different model of cultural going-out

Chafeker has also done the reverse — bringing South Africans to China. His groups have visited Beijing, Guangzhou, Xi'an, Hong Kong, Lanzhou, Kashgar and Urumqi. "When people actually meet," he notes, "many misunderstandings fade. Personal experience is stronger than hearsay." In an era when China–Africa relations are often reduced in public debate to loans, ports and minerals, the wushu bridge is a reminder that the steadiest layer of exchange is civilizational and human.

For observers of the Chinese diaspora, the lesson is pointed. We tend to measure "cultural going-out" by volume — how many centres opened, how many broadcasts aired. Chafeker's three decades argue for a different metric: depth of local rootedness. A practice that produces 150 athletes from eight countries, taught by Africans to Africans, is worth more than a dozen ribbon-cuttings. The future of China's cultural presence in Africa may depend less on what Beijing sends than on how many Chafekers it unwittingly creates.

## Further reading

- **Jingwu (精武, Chin Woo)**: A martial arts association founded in Shanghai in 1910, later spread overseas and became a global vehicle for Chinese physical culture.
- **People-to-people bond (民心相通)**: A stated pillar of China's Belt and Road framework, emphasising cultural and civilizational exchange over infrastructure alone.
- **Traditional Chinese Medicine (中医)**: A system of medicine rooted in qi and balance, increasingly studied and practised abroad through diaspora networks.
