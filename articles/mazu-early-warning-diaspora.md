# 从妈祖庙到气象预警：当"中国方案"遇上东南亚的最后一公里

2026年7月，上海世界人工智能大会（WAIC）上，中国国家主席习近平宣布了一项看似寻常却意味深长的承诺：未来五年内，推动中国气象智能预警方案"妈祖（MAZU）"在30个国家落地应用。这套以中国沿海民间海神命名的预警系统，其英文缩写被赋予了严谨的技术含义——M代表多灾种（Multi-hazard），A代表预警（Alert），Z代表零差距（Zero-gap），U代表普惠（Universal）。

然而，一个耐人寻味的现实是：截至2026年8月，"妈祖"已在巴基斯坦、埃塞俄比亚、所罗门群岛、约旦、斯里兰卡、蒙古国和吉布提等7个国家完成落地部署，全球40余国通过云端使用——**但东南亚十国中，尚无一国正式接入。**

这并非疏忽，而是一个值得深究的结构性悖论：东南亚恰恰是全球妈祖信俗最深厚的地区，新加坡天福宫、吉隆坡天后宫、槟城琼州会馆、胡志明市天后宫、马尼拉岷伦洛区的华人寺庙群……据不完全统计，仅马来西亚一国的妈祖宫庙就超过200座。2009年联合国教科文组织将"妈祖信俗"列入人类非物质文化遗产代表作名录时，评审报告特别强调了其在东南亚华人社群中的活态传承。

**换句话说，品牌认知早已就位，技术落地却绕道而行。为什么？**

## 数字：预警缺失的代价有多高

先看一组冷峻的数据。联合国秘书长古特雷斯在2022年发起"全民早期预警"（Early Warnings for All）倡议，目标是在2027年底前让地球上每一个人都受到灾害预警系统的保护。该倡议的行动计划估算，2023至2027年间需要31亿美元（约224亿元人民币）的新增投资——相当于每人每年0.5美元（约3.58元人民币）。这笔钱能换来什么？世界气象组织（WMO）前秘书长佩泰里·塔拉斯给出的答案是：提前24小时发出预警，可将灾害损失降低30%。

更残酷的是不作为的代价。联合国数据显示，预警覆盖率低的国家，灾害相关死亡率是覆盖率高的国家的至少六倍（部分统计口径下高达八倍）。过去五十年间，与天气、水和气候相关的灾害已造成超过200万人死亡，其中90%来自发展中国家。

东南亚是全球气候风险最集中的区域之一。菲律宾年均遭遇约20个热带气旋；印尼地处环太平洋地震带与火山带的交汇处，同时面临洪水、干旱和跨境烟霾的多重威胁；湄公河流域的越南、柬埔寨、老挝则深受旱涝交替之苦。东盟灾害管理人道主义援助协调中心（AHA Centre）自2011年成立以来，平均每年响应超过40起灾害事件。

然而，预警能力的短板并不在卫星层面——日本向东盟国家提供Himawari卫星数据，美国NOAA共享GOES卫星产品，欧洲EUMETSAT也有覆盖。真正的瓶颈在于**最后一公里**：预警信息如何从国家气象局传到偏远村庄的手机里、渔民的收音机里、菜市场小贩的耳朵里？

## 悖论：文化最浓的地方，技术反而缺席

"妈祖"方案的架构设计本身是务实的。它不是一套"交钥匙"后甩手不管的成品系统，而是基于云端的气象早期预警业务平台——各国预报员注册登录后，可获取监测、预报和预警产品，还能对比查看中国CMA模型与欧洲中期天气预报中心（ECMWF）模型的不同输出。系统搭载"风雷""风清""风顺"等系列AI气象预报模型，覆盖30大类200余种气象灾害，并能基于大语言模型自动生成分角色、分灾种的防御指引。

据中国媒体报道，"妈祖"在阿富汗成功提前捕捉到强对流信号并发布冰雹预警；在乌干达协助应对两场突发强降雨；在蒙古国接入风云2H气象卫星积雪数据后，用当地语言生成避险指引。2026年4月发布的云端公共版增设了海外云服务节点，平台加载速度提升6倍，显著改善了非洲和南亚用户的访问体验。

这些案例全部发生在南亚、中东和非洲。东南亚呢？

一个关键原因是**竞争格局不同**。东南亚各国的国家气象水文机构——菲律宾PAGASA、印尼BMKG、泰国TMD、马来西亚MetMalaysia、越南NCHMF——大多已有相对成熟的业务体系，且长期与日本JMA、美国NOAA、澳大利亚BoM及欧盟Copernicus保持合作关系。在这些市场，"妈祖"面对的不是空白，而是存量替代或增量补充，政治敏感度和谈判复杂度远高于吉布提或蒙古国。

另一个原因或许更为微妙：**宗教与文化敏感性**。"妈祖"作为中国民间信仰的神祇，在佛教占主导的泰国、越南和天主教为主的菲律宾问题不大，但在穆斯林人口占多数的印尼（近2.3亿穆斯林）和马来西亚（约61%穆斯林），以神祇命名的公共产品可能引发不必要的联想。这也是为何中方始终强调MAZU首先是一个技术缩写——多灾种、预警、零差距、普惠——而非宗教符号。

不过，转机已经出现。2026年WAIC期间，中国气象局宣布启动**中泰气象灾害智能预测预警联合实验室**建设，并启动"风和"气象大模型全球开源计划。这标志着"妈祖"正式迈出进入东南亚的第一步——而且选择泰国作为桥头堡，既有地缘考量（泰国是东盟核心成员），也有务实逻辑：泰国气象局（TMD）技术基础较好，合作阻力相对较小。

## 华侨华人：被忽视的最后一公里基础设施

如果说国家层面的对接需要外交斡旋和技术谈判来慢慢推进，那么在社区层面，东南亚华侨华人的传统网络其实构成了一个现成的、尚未被充分调动的"最后一公里"基础设施。

妈祖宫庙在东南亚的功能远超宗教场所。新加坡天福宫始建于1842年，曾是福建移民的登陆点、同乡会和福利中心；吉隆坡天后宫不仅举办祭典，还运营教育项目和社区服务；印尼泗水的兴安会馆、缅甸仰光的福建会馆，都在历次自然灾害中扮演过物资募集和灾民安置的角色。这些组织拥有三样东西：**基层信任网络**、**多语言能力**（闽南语、普通话、当地语言）、**灾害响应经验**。

据中新社报道，中国气象局在世界气象组织执行理事会第80次届会上（2026年6月）宣布设立"妈祖"国际奖学金，面向共建"一带一路"国家气象访问学者和发展中国家一线工作者。自2023年以来，已有来自100多个国家和地区的近千名学员参与中国气象早期预警技术培训。这是能力建设的顶层设计。

但顶层设计再完善，也替代不了社区层面的触达。2018年印尼巽他海峡海啸造成430人死亡，部分原因是火山引发的海啸不在当时预警系统的覆盖范围内，且多个深海预警浮标因缺乏维护而失效。2008年纳尔吉斯气旋在缅甸造成超过13.8万人死亡，一个关键因素是预警信息未能及时传递到伊洛瓦底江三角洲的农村社区。2013年台风"海燕"在菲律宾造成逾6000人死亡，许多遇难者死于风暴潮——而"storm surge"这个术语对普通民众来说太过陌生，翻译和本地化传播严重滞后。

这三个惨痛案例指向同一个结论：**预警技术的价值，最终取决于它能否以人们听得懂的语言、通过他们信任的渠道、在他们需要的时间到达手中。**

东南亚的妈祖宫庙网络恰好具备这三要素。它们不需要从零搭建信任——信任已在几代人的香火中积累；它们天然具备多语言环境——闽南语、普通话、英语、马来语、泰语、越南语在宫庙日常中交织流转；它们有灾害响应的组织记忆——从2004年印度洋大海啸到2020年新冠疫情，宫庙和会馆多次参与募捐和救助。

当然，这并不意味着宫庙应该成为官方预警发布渠道——根据WMO的"单一权威声音"原则，只有国家气象机构才有法律权限发布权威预警。但宫庙可以作为**辅助传播节点**，在官方预警发出后，通过微信群、广播、敲锣打鼓等传统方式确保信息覆盖到那些不常看智能手机的老人、渔民和外籍劳工。

## 冷静审视：机遇之外的风险

对"妈祖"方案进入东南亚，有必要保持清醒的判断。

第一，**主权敏感性**。任何外国提供的气象数据和AI模型都可能被视为对国家主权的潜在侵蚀，尤其在地缘政治紧张的当下。东南亚国家普遍奉行大国平衡外交，接受中国的预警方案不意味着排斥美日欧的现有合作，但如何平衡多方关系考验着各国决策者的智慧。

第二，**数字依赖风险**。如果"妈祖"云平台成为某国气象部门的主要工具，那么平台方的服务连续性、数据安全性和算法透明度就成了关键问题。开源化（如"风和"大模型的全球开源计划）可以在一定程度上缓解这一担忧，但开源不等于无门槛——许多发展中国家仍缺乏运行和维护复杂AI模型的人才。

第三，**效果验证**。"妈祖"在南亚和非洲的成功案例多由中国媒体单方面报道，独立的第三方评估仍然缺失。东南亚的科研机构（如新加坡地球观测研究所EOI、泰国亚洲理工学院AIT）有能力也应当对这些声称进行独立检验。

第四，**命名策略的双刃剑效应**。在东南亚华人圈子里，"妈祖"唤起的是亲切感和文化认同；但在非华人群体中，这个名字可能显得陌生甚至带有特定文化色彩。缩写优先的策略（强调MAZU的技术含义）有助于淡化宗教色彩，但也削弱了文化软力量的独特性。

## 结语：从"海上保护神"到"气象守护者"

湄洲妈祖祖庙董事会董事长林金赞在接受中新社采访时说了一句意味深长的话："古人将出海平安的期盼投射于妈祖，这并非迷信，而是农耕与海洋文明交织下，先民直面自然、渴望'人定胜天'的浪漫表达。"

千年之后，这种"直面自然"的精神以另一种形式延续了下来——只不过载体从香案上的神像变成了风云气象卫星，从口口相传的观云辨风经验变成了深度学习模型，从一村一庙的守望相助变成了覆盖40余国的云端预警平台。

对东南亚而言，"妈祖"方案的价值不在于它是否"取代"现有的日美欧体系，而在于它为这个气候高风险地区增加了一个可选的工具、一条新的能力建设渠道、以及一种将传统文化符号转化为现代公共产品的创新思路。如果中泰联合实验室能够顺利运行，如果"风和"开源计划能够吸引东南亚本土开发者参与，如果宫庙网络能够在合法合规的前提下成为官方预警的有益补充——那么"妈祖"从"海上保护神"到"气象守护者"的转型，将在其信俗最深厚的这片土地上获得最完整的意义。

据中国侨网"东西问"栏目报道，中国气象局将全民早期预警方案命名为"妈祖"，是"传统文化与现代科技的融合"。融合是否成功，最终要靠东南亚沿海村庄里的渔民和农民来判断——当台风来临前的那个夜晚，他们手机上收到的预警信息，是不是足够早、足够准、足够让他们听懂。

## 延伸阅读

**"妈祖"（MAZU）气象早期预警方案**：中国气象局推出的全民早期预警系统，英文名取自 Multi-hazard（多灾种）、Alert（预警）、Zero-gap（零差距）、Universal（普惠）四个词首字母，意在强调其技术属性而非宗教含义。

**全民早期预警（Early Warnings for All, EW4All）**：联合国与世界气象组织（WMO）于 2022 年发起的倡议，目标是到 2027 年底前让地球上每一个人都能获得灾害性天气、水和气候事件的预警，预计需投入约 31 亿美元。

**"单一权威声音"原则（Single Authoritative Voice）**：世界气象组织确立的预警发布准则，规定只有各国国家气象部门有权发布权威预警。因此华侨华人宫庙与会馆网络只能作为官方预警的辅助播报节点，而非替代渠道。

# From Mazu Temples to Early Warning Systems: When China's "MAZU" Meets Southeast Asia's Last Mile

In July 2026, at the World Artificial Intelligence Conference (WAIC) in Shanghai, Chinese President Xi Jinping made a commitment that sounded routine but carries layered implications: over the next five years, China would deploy its "MAZU" meteorological early warning solution in 30 countries. Named after the Chinese sea goddess worshipped along coastlines worldwide, the acronym carries a rigorously technical meaning — M for Multi-hazard, A for Alert, Z for Zero-gap, U for Universal.

Yet a striking reality persists: as of August 2026, MAZU has been deployed in seven countries — Pakistan, Ethiopia, the Solomon Islands, Jordan, Sri Lanka, Mongolia, and Djibouti — with meteorological agencies in more than 40 countries accessing it via cloud platforms. **Not a single Southeast Asian nation has formally onboarded.**

This is not an oversight. It is a structural paradox worth examining: Southeast Asia is precisely where Mazu folk religion runs deepest. Singapore's Thian Hock Keng (built 1842), Kuala Lumpur's Thean Hou Temple, Penang's Hainan Temple, Ho Chi Minh City's Thien Hau Pagoda in Cholon, Manila's Chinese temple cluster in Binondo — across the region, Mazu temples number in the thousands. When UNESCO inscribed "Mazu Belief and Custom" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009, the evaluation report specifically highlighted its living transmission among Chinese diaspora communities in Southeast Asia.

**In other words, brand recognition is already in place. Technology has taken a detour around it. Why?**

## The numbers: the cost of missing warnings

Consider some stark figures. In 2022, UN Secretary-General António Guterres launched the Early Warnings for All (EW4All) initiative, aiming to protect every person on Earth against hazardous weather, water, and climate events by the end of 2027. The initiative's Executive Action Plan calls for US$3.1 billion (about RMB 22.4 billion) in targeted investments between 2023 and 2027 — roughly US$0.50 per person per year. What does this buy? According to former WMO Secretary-General Petteri Taalas, just 24 hours' advance notice of an impending hazard can reduce resulting damage by up to 30 per cent.

The cost of inaction is starker still. Countries with limited early warning coverage suffer disaster-related mortality at least six times higher than those with robust systems — under some statistical definitions, up to eight times higher. Over the past five decades, weather-, water- and climate-related hazards have killed more than two million people, with developing countries accounting for 90 per cent of deaths.

Southeast Asia sits at the epicentre of global climate risk. The Philippines averages roughly 20 tropical cyclones per year. Indonesia straddles the Pacific Ring of Fire and the volcanic belt while facing floods, droughts, and transboundary haze. Mekong Basin countries — Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos — endure a punishing cycle of alternating floods and droughts. The ASEAN Coordinating Centre for Humanitarian Assistance on Disaster Management (AHA Centre) has responded to more than 40 disaster events annually on average since its founding in 2011.

Yet the bottleneck is not satellite coverage. Japan provides Himawari satellite data to ASEAN nations; the United States shares GOES products through NOAA; Europe's EUMETSAT also covers the region. The real gap lies in **the last mile**: how does a warning travel from a national meteorological agency to a remote village fisherman's radio, a market vendor's ear, or an elderly grandmother's phone?

## The paradox: where culture is thickest, technology is absent

MAZU's architecture is pragmatically designed. It is not a turnkey system handed over and abandoned. Its core is a cloud-based early warning platform where national forecasters register to access monitoring, forecasting, and alert products. They can compare outputs from China's CMA models alongside those from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). The system hosts a suite of AI-powered forecasting models — codenamed "Fenglei," "Fengqing," and "Fengshun" — covering 30 major categories and more than 200 types of meteorological hazards, with large language model capabilities to auto-generate role-specific, hazard-specific defensive guidance.

Chinese state media report that MAZU successfully captured severe convective signals ahead of a hailstorm in Afghanistan; helped Uganda respond to two sudden torrential rain events; and in Mongolia, integrated Fengyun-2H satellite snow-cover data to generate locally language-adapted evacuation guidance. The public cloud edition released in April 2026 added overseas nodes, boosting platform loading speeds sixfold and markedly improving access stability for users in Africa and South Asia.

Every single one of these cases unfolded in South Asia, the Middle East, or Africa. What about Southeast Asia?

One key reason is the **competitive landscape**. Southeast Asia's national meteorological services — PAGASA (Philippines), BMKG (Indonesia), TMD (Thailand), MetMalaysia (Malaysia), NCHMF (Vietnam) — already operate relatively mature systems with long-standing ties to Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA), NOAA, Australia's Bureau of Meteorology (BoM), and EU Copernicus. In these markets, MAZU faces not a blank slate but entrenched incumbency. Political sensitivity and negotiation complexity are orders of magnitude higher than in Djibouti or Mongolia.

A second reason is more delicate: **religious and cultural sensitivity**. Naming a public product after a Chinese folk deity poses no issue in Buddhist-majority Thailand and Vietnam or Catholic-majority the Philippines. But in Muslim-majority Indonesia (approximately 230 million Muslims) and Malaysia (around 61 per cent Muslim), a deity-named system could trigger unnecessary associations. This explains why Beijing consistently foregrounds MAZU as a technical acronym first — Multi-hazard, Alert, Zero-gap, Universal — rather than a religious symbol.

Nevertheless, an opening has appeared. At WAIC 2026, the China Meteorological Administration (CMA) announced the launch of a **China-Thailand Joint Laboratory for Intelligent Meteorological Disaster Forecasting and Early Warning**, alongside the global open-source release of the "Fenghe" meteorological foundation model. This marks MAZU's formal first step into Southeast Asia — and Thailand is a logical beachhead: a core ASEAN member with a capable meteorological service and relatively lower cooperation friction.

## The diaspora: overlooked last-mile infrastructure

If government-to-government coordination proceeds at diplomatic speed, then at community level, Southeast Asia's traditional Chinese networks constitute a ready-made last-mile infrastructure that remains largely untapped.

Mazu temples in Southeast Asia serve functions far beyond worship. Singapore's Thian Hock Keng, built in 1842, was the landing point, native-place association, and welfare hub for Hokkien immigrants. Kuala Lumpur's Thean Hou Temple operates education programmes and community services alongside religious ceremonies. Surabaya's Xing'an Hall in Indonesia and Yangon's Fujian Association in Myanmar have both mobilised resources for disaster relief repeatedly over the decades. These organisations possess three assets: **grassroots trust networks**, **multilingual capacity** (Hokkien, Mandarin, local languages), and **institutional memory of disaster response**.

According to reports, CMA announced at the WMO Executive Council's 80th session (June 2026) the establishment of an international MAZU scholarship programme targeting meteorological visiting scholars and frontline workers from Belt and Road countries and other developing nations. Since 2023, nearly 1,000 participants from over 100 countries and regions have joined CMA's early warning technical training programmes. This is top-down capacity building.

But no amount of top-down design replaces community-level reach. The 2018 Sunda Strait tsunami killed 430 people in Indonesia, partly because volcano-triggered tsunamis fell outside the then-existing warning system's scope and multiple deep-sea warning buoys were non-functional due to maintenance gaps. Cyclone Nargis claimed more than 138,000 lives in Myanmar in 2008; a critical failure factor was that warnings never reached rural communities in the Irrawaddy Delta. Typhoon Haiyan (Yolanda) killed over 6,000 people in the Philippines in 2013; many perished from storm surge — a term that meant nothing to ordinary Filipinos because translation and localised communication had catastrophically lagged.

Three tragedies, one conclusion: **the value of early warning technology ultimately depends on whether it arrives in a language people understand, through channels they trust, at the moment they need it.**

Southeast Asia's Mazu temple network possesses precisely these three elements. Trust need not be built from scratch — it has accumulated across generations of incense and offerings. Multilingualism is built into daily temple life — Hokkien, Mandarin, English, Malay, Thai, Vietnamese flow together in routine operations. Organisational memory of disaster response exists — from the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami to COVID-19, temples and clan associations have repeatedly mobilised fundraising and relief efforts.

This does not mean temples should become official warning dissemination channels. Under WMO's Single Authoritative Voice principle, only national meteorological authorities hold legal mandate to issue authoritative alerts. But temples can serve as **auxiliary relay nodes** — amplifying official warnings through WeChat groups, loudspeakers, or even traditional gong-beating to reach elderly residents, fishermen, and migrant workers who may not check smartphones regularly.

## A clear-eyed assessment: risks alongside opportunity

Several caveats are essential.

First, **sovereign sensitivity**. Any foreign-provided meteorological data and AI model can be framed as potential encroachment on national sovereignty, especially amid intensifying great-power competition. Southeast Asian states universally practise hedging diplomacy; accepting China's warning system does not require abandoning existing Japanese, American, or European partnerships, but balancing multiple relationships tests every decision-maker's judgement.

Second, **digital dependency risk**. If MAZU's cloud platform becomes a national meteorological service's primary tool, the provider's service continuity, data security, and algorithm transparency become critical questions. Open-sourcing (as with the Fenghe model's global release) partially mitigates this concern, but open source is not barrier-free — many developing countries still lack the talent to operate and maintain complex AI systems independently.

Third, **independent verification**. MAZU's reported successes in South Asia and Africa have been documented almost exclusively by Chinese media. Independent third-party evaluation remains absent. Southeast Asia's research institutions — Singapore's Earth Observatory (EOI), Thailand's Asian Institute of Technology (AIT), Vietnam's VNU University of Science — are well-positioned and ought to conduct independent validation of these claims.

Fourth, **the naming strategy's double-edged effect**. Among ethnic Chinese communities in Southeast Asia, "Mazu" evokes warmth and cultural belonging. Among non-Chinese populations, the name may feel alien or carry specific cultural connotations. An acronym-first strategy (emphasising MAZU's technical meaning) helps neutralise religious associations but also dilutes the unique soft-power asset that the cultural symbol represents.

## Conclusion: from sea goddess to climate guardian

Lin Jinzan, chairman of the board of Meizhou Mazu Ancestral Temple, offered a telling observation in his interview with China News Service: "The ancients projected their hopes for safe voyages onto Mazu. This was not superstition — it was a romantic expression of agrarian and maritime civilisations confronting nature head-on, yearning to 'triumph over nature'."

A millennium later, that spirit of confronting nature endures in a different form — the vessel has shifted from incense-altar statues to Fengyun meteorological satellites, from orally transmitted cloud-watching lore to deep learning models, from village-by-village mutual aid to a cloud-based early warning platform reaching more than 40 countries.

For Southeast Asia, MAZU's value lies not in whether it "replaces" existing Japanese-American-European systems, but in what it adds: an additional tool for a climate-vulnerable region, a new channel for capacity building, and an innovative approach to converting a traditional cultural symbol into a modern public good. If the China-Thailand joint laboratory operates smoothly, if the Fenghe open-source plan attracts Southeast Asian developers, if temple networks can supplement official warnings within legal bounds — then Mazu's transformation from "sea goddess" to "climate guardian" will find its fullest meaning on the very soil where her belief runs deepest.

As China Qiao Wang reported, CMA named its early warning solution after Mazu as "a fusion of traditional culture and modern technology." Whether that fusion succeeds will ultimately be judged by fishermen and farmers in Southeast Asia's coastal villages — when the night before a typhoon arrives, the warning on their phone is early enough, accurate enough, and clear enough to understand.

## Further reading

**MAZU meteorological early-warning system**: Launched by China's meteorological authority, its English name is built from the initials of Multi-hazard, Alert, Zero-gap, and Universal — foregrounding its technical identity over any religious connotation.

**Early Warnings for All (EW4All)**: A UN and World Meteorological Organization (WMO) initiative launched in 2022, aiming to give every person on Earth access to warnings for hazardous weather, water, and climate events by the end of 2027, at an estimated cost of about US$3.1 billion.

**The "Single Authoritative Voice" principle**: A WMO norm holding that only national meteorological agencies may issue official alerts. Southeast Asia's Chinese temple and clan networks can therefore serve only as auxiliary relay nodes for official warnings, not as substitutes.
