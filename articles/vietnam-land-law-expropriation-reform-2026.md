# 越南（Vietnam）土地法修订：征地提速之后，谁为地价与生计买单

据联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）报道，越南国会11月21日以466票赞成、5票反对、8人未投票通过《土地法》修订案。新法共112条，定于2027年7月1日生效，但地价表与地价调整系数要到2028年1月1日才适用。两个生效日期之间那半年，恰好把这部法律的核心矛盾摆上台面：征地的机器可以先转起来，定价的工具还要再等半年。速度为先，账日后算——这是理解此次修法最直接的入口。

## 466票赞成：一次几乎没有争议的立法

479名议员中5人反对、8人未投票，这样的票数分布放在越南国会并不常见。更早一天的会议里，国会还在两天内表决通过数十部法律与决议，从刑法修正案到数据安全法，节奏之快本身就说明这一届立法机关的任务清单有多满。土地法修订被塞进这张清单，官方理由是清除制度瓶颈、释放土地资源、稳定投资者信心。

问题是，一部决定千千万万家庭土地权益的法律，以近乎一致的顺序通过，究竟说明共识牢固，还是说明议程太挤？据彭博社报道，修正案要处理的正是长期困扰房地产与基建项目的土地清理僵局。胡志明市中心一栋2012年破土、2025年才恢复施工的塔楼，就是这种僵局的具象化：工地停在那里，资本沉在那里，住户与邻里关系也悬在那里。把慢项目当成交易成本，自然会开出一剂行政加速的药方。

## 先征地、后补偿：程序顺序被改写

此前，补偿与安置方案一般须先获批，国家才能作出征地决定。新法允许部分项目反其道而行之：只要无需搬迁安置、且补偿方案已经公开，征地决定可以在方案正式获批之前作出。适用范围包括国家重大项目、紧急公共投资项目，以及获得超过75%受影响土地使用者支持的项目。

越南农业与环境部长陈越勇（Trinh Viet Hung）对国会强调，新法同时强化了安置保障、最长3年的生计支持和扩大的职业培训。专门研究基建与房地产的法律界人士则提醒，从技术角度看，这一条是为了让优先项目按期推进，同时保留程序上的保障栏。

这话在专业上站得住，在政治经济上却值得追问：顺序一改，谈判桌也跟着重摆。过去补偿谈不拢，项目就卡住，农户手里握着事实上的否决权；现在征地决定先落地，争议被改写成「钱给多少、何时给」的问题。土地一旦先交出去，剩下的筹码还剩多少，法律条文没有回答。程序正义不只是有没有开过会、贴过公告，更包括双方进场时是否站在相对平等的位置。

## 10%的增长目标：土地瓶颈被当成制度税

越南为2026年设定了至少10%的增长目标，并希望在往后多年维持两位数增长。单靠出口显然撑不起这个数字，大规模基建、工业园区和制造业扩张必须同步上马，而这一切都要落在土地上。在土地归全民所有、国家统一管理、企业与个人取得使用权的制度下，征地几乎是每一条高速公路、每一个园区、每一片新城区绕不开的关口。

据路透社报道，土地纠纷在越南长期敏感，补偿谈不拢拖上数年并不罕见，偶尔还会走上街头。这解释了政府为何把征地慢视作必须削掉的制度税。但把土地问题简化为审批时长，是一种危险的会计学：审批表上的一格，可能是某个家庭三代人的生计、社交网络和身份来源。一次性货币补偿能买走地，买不走这些。增长目标越激进，越需要有人替这些无法折价的损失说话。

## 地价改革：数据驱动能否治住低价补偿

此次修法全面改革土地定价机制，官方说法是简化估价程序、提高透明度，并防止价格操纵。土地分配、租赁与用途变更的规定也被调整，数字化土地数据库将建立，更多登记与产权认证手续搬到线上。地价表与调整系数改由省级人民议会每3年决定一次，可用于计算国家土地收入、土地使用权拍卖起价、征地补偿和相关税费。

新机制听上去技术中性，实际上触碰了征地争议最敏感的那根神经：补偿按什么价算。农户常说补偿低于市场价，开发商则嫌成本高，中间的落差就是定价权的落点。据澎湃新闻报道，争议的核心正在于补偿标准能否贴近真实价值。若所谓「数据驱动」只是把行政低价换了个市场参考价的名字，程序再规范也只是在更快地执行一笔不公平的交易。

更微妙的是，地方政府同时获得更大的土地定价、补偿方案决定和整体土地管理权。定价者与征收者若是同一级政府，透明就只剩下公开的程序，而缺少外部约束。市场数据从哪来、谁有权质疑、异议如何进入复核程序，这些细节比「透明」二字本身更能决定改革成色。

## 地方放权：效率与边界是同一枚硬币

把定价与补偿权下放地方，逻辑并不难懂：地方政府更了解本地地价与安置条件，决策链条更短。越南官方以「地方决定、地方实施、地方负责」概括这一原则。但责任写在纸面上，执行靠的是财政能力与问责机制，而这两样恰恰是地方层面最不均衡的资源。

在土地出让收入对地方财政仍有吸引力的背景下，放权也可能放大激励扭曲：既当运动员又当裁判员的地方政府，有多少动力把补偿价定在市场高位？上级复核、独立评估和可用的司法救济，本应是对冲这种风险的闸门。新法在保障条款上写了不少漂亮词，却没有清楚交代这些闸门如何运转。放权不等于卸责；如果责任只是事后追责几个签字的人，而不是事前给纳税人一张可核查的账，效率越高，争议积压得越快。

## 75%的同意线：多数人的同意能否覆盖少数人的损失

超过75%的土地使用者同意，是启动加速程序的一把钥匙。多数决在这里被当作效率的证明：既然绝大多数人点头，项目就有了社会许可，剩下的人不该拖住整体。

这个推理听起来顺理成章，却混淆了两件事。多数人的同意可以证明项目有民意基础，却不能自动抹去少数人承受的具体损失。对一块地而言，25%是少数；对一个家庭而言，失去的那块地就是全部。越南新法规定最长3年的生计支持与职业培训，说明决策者知道货币补偿不足以解决长期问题。但生计恢复能否兑现，取决于培训是否对接真实岗位、补贴是否覆盖过渡期，以及失去土地者有没有重新议价的渠道。把同意率当成闸门，等于把权利问题改成了统计问题——数字过了线，个体的申诉就被默认为次要。

## 红河与280亿美元：项目清单比口号更长

今年早些时候，河内红河沿岸一项规模约280亿美元（约2016亿元人民币）的开发计划引发居民抗议，受影响者约28万人。居民挂起横幅、在街区游行，要求重新考虑方案。另一个高关注度案例是兴安省由特朗普集团参与支持的高尔夫项目，其中涉及从当地居民手中取得的农地。再加上那些停工十年才复工的市中心塔楼，这份清单说明土地瓶颈不是抽象指标。

这些案例里，居民未必反对发展本身，他们要的是把发展的账单摆上桌：补偿按什么标准、安置在哪里、原来的收入怎么接上。项目越被定义为「国家重大」「紧急」，越需要把补偿标准提前公开、把异议渠道提前铺好。否则提速换来的不是效率，而是把争议推到施工之后——那时的工地已经开工，谈判的天平只会更加倾斜。

## 真正的考验在2027：纸面保障如何兑现

新法要到2027年7月1日才生效，新的定价机制还要再等半年。这段时间不是空窗，而是规则落地前的准备期：地方要建数据库、要重设地价表、要训练基层执行人员，农户也要知道自己的申诉会被谁受理。法律写着补偿与安置须「客观、公平、公开、透明、及时」，这五个词能不能从文本走到田间地头，取决于随后每一个具体项目的操作。

对越南而言，土地制度改革的难点从来不是有没有法律，而是法律能否把增长目标翻译成可信的产权预期。投资者需要的是确定的工期，居民需要的是确定的补偿，两者并不天然冲突，但必须由同一套可预期的规则来衔接。466张赞成票能显示政治决心，却替代不了此后每一户人家的签字。真正的成本，从来不在表决那天结算。

## 延伸阅读

**土地征收（Land expropriation）**：国家为公共目的依法强制取得土地使用权并给予补偿的制度，其合法性取决于公共利益界定、补偿标准与救济渠道，而非仅取决于审批速度。

**地价表与调整系数（Land price table and K coefficient）**：越南用于计算土地预算收入、使用权拍卖起价与征收补偿的行政价格工具，由省级议会定期确定，与市场成交价之间可能存在落差。

**生计恢复（Livelihood restoration）**：征地安置中除一次性货币补偿外，对收入来源、住房与职业技能的持续支持，用来衡量补偿是否真正避免生活水平下降。

# Vietnam's Land Law Rewrite: Speeding Up Expropriation, Passing the Bill to Whom?

According to Lianhe Zaobao, Vietnam's National Assembly passed amendments to the Land Law on 21 November, with 466 in favour, five against and eight not voting. The revised law contains 112 articles and takes effect on 1 July 2027, but the new land-price table and adjustment coefficients will not apply until 1 January 2028. That six-month gap lays bare the central contradiction of the reform: the machinery of expropriation can start running first, while the tool for pricing land waits another half-year. Speed first, the bill later — that is the most direct way into this story.

## 466 in favour: an almost uncontested vote

Five opposed and eight abstained out of 479 deputies — an unusually lopsided result even by the standards of Vietnam's National Assembly. A day earlier the legislature had passed dozens of laws and resolutions in two sittings, from criminal-code amendments to a data-security law. The pace alone says how crowded the agenda was. The land-law revision was folded into that list, officially to remove institutional bottlenecks, unlock land resources and restore investor confidence.

The question is whether a law that determines the property rights of millions of households passes almost unanimously because the consensus is genuinely solid, or because the schedule leaves no room for argument. According to Bloomberg, the amendments target the clearance deadlocks that have long paralysed property and infrastructure projects. A tower in central Ho Chi Minh City that broke ground in 2012 and only resumed work in 2025 gives that deadlock a physical form: the site stood idle, capital sat frozen, and the neighbours' lives stayed suspended. Once slow projects are reframed as transaction costs, an administrative accelerator becomes the obvious prescription.

## Expropriate first, compensate later: the sequence changes

Previously, compensation and resettlement plans generally had to be approved before the state could take land. The revised law lets authorities issue an expropriation decision beforehand for certain projects, provided no resettlement is required and the compensation plan has been made public. The faster track covers nationally important projects, urgent public-investment projects, and cases where more than 75% of affected land users agree.

Trinh Viet Hung, Minister of Agriculture and Environment, told parliament that the law simultaneously strengthens resettlement protections, provides livelihood support for up to three years and expands vocational training. Legal practitioners specialising in infrastructure and real estate caution that, technically, the provision is designed to keep priority projects on schedule while retaining procedural safeguards.

That is defensible in professional terms and worth interrogating in political-economy terms. Change the sequence and you rearrange the negotiating table. When compensation talks stalled in the past, a project stopped, and households held a de facto veto. Now the expropriation decision lands first and the dispute is rewritten as a question of how much money, and when. Once the land has been handed over, how many chips remain? The statute does not say. Procedural justice is not only about holding meetings and posting notices; it is also about whether the parties enter the room from roughly equal positions.

## The 10% growth target: treating a land bottleneck as a tax on business

Vietnam has set a growth target of at least 10% for 2026 and hopes to sustain double-digit expansion for years to come. Exports alone cannot carry those numbers; large infrastructure, industrial parks and manufacturing capacity must expand in tandem, and all of it needs land. With land owned by the whole people and managed by the state, while enterprises and individuals hold use rights, expropriation is unavoidable for almost every highway, industrial zone and new urban district.

Reuters reports that land disputes have long been sensitive in Vietnam, with compensation talks often dragging on for years and occasionally spilling into protest. That explains why the government treats slow clearance as an institutional tax to be removed. But reducing land to a processing time is a dangerous piece of accounting: one box on an approval form may represent three generations of a family's livelihood, social network and identity. Lump-sum compensation can buy the soil; it cannot buy what grew out of it. The more ambitious the growth target, the more urgently someone must speak for losses that cannot be priced.

## Land valuation reform: can data-driven pricing curb low compensation?

The amendments overhaul the land-pricing mechanism, officially to simplify valuation, improve transparency and prevent price manipulation. Rules on land allocation, leasing and changes of use have also been revised; a digital land database will be built, and more registration and certification procedures will move online. The land-price table and adjustment coefficients will now be decided by provincial people's councils every three years, and used to calculate land-related budget revenue, auction starting prices, expropriation compensation and related taxes and fees.

The new mechanism sounds technically neutral, but it touches the most sensitive nerve in expropriation disputes: at what price is compensation calculated? Farmers say compensation falls below market value; developers say it is too high. The gap is where pricing power lands. The Paper reports that the crux of the controversy is precisely whether compensation can track real value. If "data-driven" merely renames an administrative low price as a market reference, a cleaner procedure only enforces an unfair transaction faster.

More delicate still, local governments gain greater authority over land prices, compensation packages and overall land management. When the pricer and the expropriator are the same tier of government, transparency is reduced to published procedure without external checks. Where the market data come from, who may challenge them, and how objections enter a review process matter far more than the word "transparency" itself.

## Devolution: efficiency and its limits are the same coin

Delegating pricing and compensation decisions to local governments is easy to justify: local officials know local prices and resettlement conditions best, and the decision chain is shorter. Hanoi has summarised the principle as "local decision, local implementation, local responsibility." But responsibility written on paper depends for its force on fiscal capacity and accountability — precisely the two resources distributed most unevenly at local level.

Where land-sale revenue still appeals to local budgets, devolution can also sharpen a conflict of interest: how much incentive does a government that acts as both referee and player have to set compensation at the top of the market? Higher-level review, independent valuation and usable judicial remedies should be the gates against that risk. The law writes down plenty of handsome safeguards but is far less clear about how those gates operate. Devolution is not the surrender of responsibility. If accountability means punishing a few signatories afterwards rather than giving taxpayers a verifiable ledger beforehand, the faster the machine runs, the faster disputes accumulate.

## The 75% threshold: does majority consent cover minority loss?

Support from more than 75% of affected land users is one key that unlocks the fast track. Majority approval is treated as evidence of efficiency: if the overwhelming majority has nodded, the project has a social licence and the remainder should not hold up the whole.

The reasoning sounds tidy but conflates two things. Majority consent can establish a project's popular basis; it cannot automatically erase the concrete losses borne by a minority. For a parcel of land, 25% is a minority; for one household, the plot it loses is everything. Vietnam's new law promises up to three years of livelihood support and vocational training, which suggests policymakers know cash alone cannot settle the long-term question. Whether livelihood restoration actually works depends on whether training leads to real jobs, whether subsidies cover the transition, and whether those who lose land retain any channel to renegotiate. Turning a consent rate into a gate converts a question of rights into a statistical one — once the number clears the line, the individual appeal is presumed secondary.

## The Red River and US$28 billion: the project list is longer than the slogan

Earlier this year, a redevelopment plan worth about US$28 billion along Hanoi's Red River drew protests from residents, roughly 280,000 of whom could be affected. They hung banners and marched through their neighbourhood, asking authorities to reconsider. Another prominent case is a golf development in Hung Yen province backed by the Trump Organization, which includes farmland acquired from local residents. Add the tower that stood half-built for more than a decade, and the list shows that the land bottleneck is no abstract indicator.

In these cases residents are not necessarily opposed to development. They want the bill laid out: on what basis is compensation calculated, where will people be resettled, and how does lost income get replaced? The more a project is labelled "nationally important" or "urgent," the more urgently compensation standards should be published and grievance channels prepared. Otherwise speed does not deliver efficiency; it simply postpones the dispute until after the ground has been broken — by which time the negotiating scales tip even further.

## The real test arrives in 2027: how paper guarantees cash out

The law takes effect on 1 July 2027, and the new pricing system another six months later. That interval is not a gap but a preparation period: local authorities must build databases, redraw price tables and train frontline staff, while farmers learn who will hear their appeals. The statute requires compensation and resettlement to be "objective, fair, open, transparent and timely." Whether those five words travel from text to field depends on how each concrete project is handled afterwards.

For Vietnam, the difficulty of land reform has never been the absence of a law, but whether a law can translate growth ambitions into credible property expectations. Investors need a certain timetable; residents need certain compensation. The two are not naturally in conflict, but only a predictable common rule can join them. The 466 votes express political resolve; they cannot substitute for the signature of every household that comes afterwards. Real costs are never settled on voting day.

## Further reading

**Land expropriation**: The compulsory transfer of land-use rights by the state for public purposes in exchange for compensation; its legitimacy rests on how the public purpose is defined, how compensation is set and what remedies exist — not on the speed of approval alone.

**Land price table and K coefficient**: Vietnamese administrative pricing tools used to calculate land-related budget revenue, auction starting prices and expropriation compensation, set periodically by provincial councils and potentially divergent from market transactions.

**Livelihood restoration**: In resettlement, sustained support for income sources, housing and skills beyond one-off cash compensation, used to measure whether a household's standard of living genuinely recovers.
