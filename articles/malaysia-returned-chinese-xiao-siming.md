# 从逃难孩童到归国战士：一位马来西亚归侨的"唐山"记忆与离散中国的百年回响


## 逃难：南洋沦陷下的华侨童年

肖思明1936年出生于广东惠阳，祖父早年赴马来西亚谋生。1939年日军进犯华南，年仅3岁的他随家人远赴马来西亚，开始了长达十年的异乡漂泊。"我几乎整个童年都是在逃难、流浪中度过的。"他后来接受中新网专访时这样回忆。

这句轻描淡写的话背后，藏着极重的分量。日军占领马来西亚期间烧杀抢掠，肖思明记得，听说有个地方一夜之间就有三千多人被杀害，房屋全被烧毁。他的家庭也未能幸免：一位小叔去镇上拉泔水喂猪，路上撞见日军，躲进阴沟仍被发现，最终惨遭杀害。对当地华侨而言，躲避日军搜查、四处逃难是生活的常态。日寇的暴行，在年少的肖思明心中埋下了家国伤痛的种子。

这不只是个人的创伤。1941至1945年东南亚的沦陷，对当地的华族而言是一段格外残酷的岁月：社群因与中国的真实或假定联系、因自身的经济实力、因对抵抗运动的支持而遭到针对。肖思明的记忆，正嵌在这幅更大的结构里——南洋（对东南亚的旧称）的华侨，既是受害者，也是帝国统治下默默不屈的臣民。

## 唐山：离散者的精神原乡

动荡岁月里，海外华侨的故土情结从未消解。肖思明坚持学习中文。那时，东南亚华侨都把祖国称作"唐山"——并非河北的那座城市，而是"大唐江山"的缩略，是心中一个想象里的中国。他虽年纪尚幼，但心中始终盼着能回到故土。

"唐山"这个词，值得驻足片刻。对一个散居马来亚、新加坡、荷属东印度、暹罗各地的离散群体而言，它从来不是精确的地理。它是一枚道德的指南针——一种"我们来自某处"的宣称，哪怕那"某处"远在重洋之外、政权已然更迭。家国情怀没有在战火中消散，反而被占领磨得更锐利。在异国的旗帜下自认"唐山"，等于在强权面前坚持自己的归属指向别处。

而贯穿整段故事的，正是一个安静的悖论：离得越远，对想象中故国的牵引反而越强。那个在马来亚躲避日军搜捕的男孩，按他自己的讲述，早已是一个心向唐山的游子。

## 归国：1949年的那艘货轮与"投笔从戎"

1949年，新中国成立的消息传到马来西亚。肖思明的祖父既牵挂故土，也担忧子孙在异乡的安危，当即决定让母亲带他归国。1949年12月，13岁的肖思明登上货轮。"船上没有固定座位，大家只能挤在角落，就这样在海上漂了半个多月。"经辗转多地，他于1950年1月终于踏上祖国大地。

亲历过战乱之苦，肖思明格外珍视和平岁月的读书机会。归国后他刻苦学习，即便跳级插班仍成绩优异。他就读的惠阳崇雅中学素有"东江革命摇篮"之称，革命先驱的事迹与童年战乱记忆交织，让他的报国念头愈发坚定。1953年，肖思明响应国家征兵号召，与十几名同学一同投笔从戎。

"部队就像一座大熔炉，磨炼了我的意志，也让我学到一身本领。"他说。在11年的军旅生涯中，他驻守上海及周边地区，投身国防建设，其间荣立三等功。当年因战火流离的少年，在上海以另一种方式守护家国安宁；转业后他在平凡岗位恪尽职守，见证上海数十年的城市变迁。

## 从南侨机工到今日新侨：东南亚华人的代际之迁

肖思明的轨迹是个人的，却与一个结构性的模式同声相应。抗战时期，东南亚华侨是对中国存续最倾力奉献的海外群体之一：南侨机工沿滇缅公路抢运物资，陈嘉庚等侨领组织巨额筹款，侨汇与捐款源源汇回。"投笔从戎"对那一代人而言，是一次切实的、也是集体的行动。

把这代人，与原报道末尾出现的人物对照：新侨王玮成，听闻肖思明事迹后，立誓要在飞速发展的时代"接过接力棒"。离散群体的忠诚并未消失，只是换了形态。救亡年代的华侨以身体回应祖国的召唤——归国、从军、汇款；全球化时代的新侨则以网络、资本、专长与文化创业作答。工具不同，许多家族里那份取向却奇异地一脉相承。

但我们不该浪漫化。同一篇报道提到，肖思明的家族在马来西亚已逾两百人。"无论我们身处何处，永远根在中国，永远珍视和平。"这句"根在中国"，与一个彻头彻尾马来西亚化的庞大亲族网络，毫不冲突地并存着。这才是当今东南亚华人的真实处境：忠于一个自己或许知之甚少的中国，又根植于一个毫无疑问属于自己的国家。若只把这段口述读成"华侨爱中国"的证明，便错过了那更硬核、也更有趣的真相——离散身份的归属，从来都是协商出来的，而非天然给定。

## 记忆的政治：历史资产还是身份负担？

那么，今天该如何看待这样的故事？两种读法，指向相反的方向。

第一种宽厚，且在某方面正确。南洋华侨关于抵抗与归国的记忆，是中国与东南亚关系中的一笔软资产。它提供了一套共享的语汇——牺牲、亲缘、"根"——足以润滑那些本易生嫌隙的边界之间的人心往来。当一位九旬上海居民忆起马来亚的童年，他无论自觉与否，都在为两个海岸之间保留一条人的通道。

第二种更为冷峻。记忆也会被工具化。风险在于，像肖思明这样的故事被榨取出单一道德——"华侨永远是中国人"——从而抹平东南亚华族实际而复杂的生命，他们中许多人世代经营的，本就不是中国。那句"根在中国"，落在不怀好意者手中，会被读成对治外忠诚的主张，恰好喂饱本博客他处已批评过的"第五纵队"猜疑。

负责任的读法，落在二者之间。我们可以尊崇一位九旬长者真切的记忆，而不必把它改写成政治宣言。华侨对祖国的历史贡献真实且值得记录；他们作为马来西亚或任何地方的、拥有自身忠诚的定居社群的权利，同样真实。记忆架桥，却不该被用来绑缚。

肖思明的一生——从南洋逃难的孩童，到归国的战士，再到扎根上海的建设者——是一个人，对每一个离散群体都须回答的问题给出的答案：家在何处？他的答案"根在中国"，由他做主。而我们其余人的任务，是把这答案当作口述来倾听，而非当作敕令来奉行。

## 延伸阅读

- **南侨机工（Nanyang Mechanics / 南洋机工）**：抗战时期由东南亚华侨组成的汽车司机与机修队伍，沿滇缅公路抢运战略物资，是南洋华侨支援祖国抗战最具代表性的集体行动之一。
- **唐山（Tangshan / 侨乡口语中的"中国"）**：东南亚老一代华侨对祖国的习惯称谓，并非仅指河北唐山，而是"大唐江山"的缩略，承载离散群体对精神原乡的想象。
- **归侨（Returned Overseas Chinese / guiqiao）**：已取得居住国身份或长期居留海外、后回国定居的华侨，是中国侨务工作中一个特定的身份类别，兼具"海外"与"中国"双重属性。

# From a Fleeing Child to a Homecoming Soldier: A Malaysian Returned Chinese and the Century-Long Echo of a Diasporic China


## Flight: a Nanyang childhood under occupation

Xiao Siming was born in 1936 in Huiyang, Guangdong. His grandfather had gone to Malaya to make a living in the early years of the century. When Japanese forces pushed into South China in 1939, the three-year-old was taken by his family to Malaya, beginning a decade of displacement in a foreign land. "Almost my entire childhood was spent fleeing and wandering," he later told China News Service.

That understatement hides a great deal. Under the Japanese occupation, Malaya burned and looted. Xiao recalls hearing that in one place, more than three thousand people were killed in a single night, their houses burned to the ground. His own family was not spared: a young uncle, sent to fetch swill for the pigs, ran into Japanese soldiers on the road; he hid in a drain but was found and killed. For the local Chinese, dodging searches and fleeing became the rhythm of daily life. The atrocities planted the seed of a national wound in the boy's memory.

This is not merely one man's trauma. The occupation of Southeast Asia between 1941 and 1945 was, for the region's ethnic Chinese, a particularly brutal chapter: communities were targeted for their real or assumed ties to China, for their economic standing, and for their support of the resistance. Xiao's memory sits inside that larger structure — the Nanyang (南洋, the "Southern Ocean," the old Chinese name for Southeast Asia) Chinese as both victims and quietly defiant subjects of empire.

## Tangshan: the spiritual homeland of the dispersed

Amid the turmoil, the overseas Chinese never let go of their attachment to the motherland. Xiao kept studying Chinese. In those years, Southeast Asian Chinese referred to the homeland as "Tangshan" (唐山) — not the city in Hebei, but a contraction of "Tang dynasty's territory," an imagined China of the mind. Though still a child, he says, he always hoped to return to his native soil.

"Tangshan" is worth pausing on. For a diaspora scattered across Malaya, Singapore, the Dutch East Indies, Siam and beyond, the word was never a precise geography. It was a moral coordinate — a way of saying "we are from somewhere," even when that somewhere was an ocean away and a regime had changed. The homeland sentiment did not dissolve under war; if anything, occupation sharpened it. To call China "Tangshan" was to insist, under a foreign flag, that one's belonging pointed elsewhere.

And here is the quiet paradox that runs through the whole story: the deeper the displacement, the stronger the pull of the imagined homeland. The boy who fled Japanese searches in Malaya was, in his own telling, already a wanderer whose heart already faced Tangshan.

## Homecoming: the 1949 freighter and "dropping the pen for the sword"

In 1949, news of the founding of the People's Republic reached Malaya. Xiao's grandfather, torn between love for the native soil and worry for his descendants in a foreign land, decided at once to send the boy home with his mother. In December 1949, the thirteen-year-old boarded a freighter. "There were no fixed seats; we squeezed into corners and drifted at sea for more than half a month," he recalled. After a roundabout journey, he set foot on the motherland in January 1950.

Having known the misery of war, Xiao treasured the chance to study in peace. He excelled even after being placed in a higher grade mid-stream. The Chongya Middle School in Huiyang he attended was known as the "cradle of the Dongjiang revolution"; the deeds of revolutionary pioneers, woven together with his own wartime memories, hardened his resolve to serve the country. In 1953, responding to the state's call for conscripts, he and a dozen schoolmates "dropped the pen for the sword" (投笔从戎).

The army, he said, was "a great furnace" that tempered his will and taught him skills. Across eleven years of service, he garrisoned Shanghai and its surroundings, threw himself into national defense, and earned a third-class merit. The child who had fled war in the south found, in Shanghai, another way to guard his homeland's peace. After demobilization he worked an ordinary post with quiet devotion, watching the city transform over decades.

## From the Nanyang mechanics to today's new overseas Chinese: a generational shift

Xiao's arc is personal, but it rhymes with a structural pattern. During the War of Resistance, Southeast Asia's Chinese were among the most committed overseas contributors to China's survival: the Nanyang Mechanics (南侨机工) drove trucks of supplies along the Burma Road; community leaders like Tan Kah Kee (陈嘉庚) organized vast fundraising; remittances and donations flowed home. "Dropping the pen for the sword" was, for that generation, a literal act — and a collective one.

Contrast that with the figure who appears at the end of the original report: Wang Weicheng, a "new overseas Chinese" (新侨) who, after hearing Xiao's story, vowed to "take up the baton" in a fast-developing age. The loyalty of the diaspora has not disappeared; it has changed form. The struggle-era overseas Chinese answered the homeland's call with the body — by returning, by enlisting, by sending money. The globalization-era overseas Chinese answer with networks, capital, expertise and cultural entrepreneurship. The instrument is different; the orientation, in many families, is oddly continuous.

Yet we should not romanticize. The same report notes Xiao's family now numbers more than two hundred in Malaysia. "No matter where we are," he says, "our roots are always in China, and we always cherish peace." The phrase "roots in China" coexists, unremarkably, with a large kin network that is thoroughly Malaysian. That is the real condition of the Southeast Asian Chinese today: loyal to a homeland they may barely know, rooted in a country that is unambiguously theirs. To read the memoir only as proof of "overseas Chinese love for China" is to miss the harder, more interesting truth — that diaspora belonging has always been negotiated, never simply given.

## The politics of memory: asset or burden?

So what should we make of such a story today? Two readings pull in opposite directions.

The first is generous and, in its own way, correct. The Nanyang Chinese memory of resistance and return is a soft asset for China–Southeast Asia relations. It supplies a shared vocabulary — sacrifice, kinship, "roots" — that can lubricate people-to-people ties across otherwise fractious borders. When a Shanghai resident in his nineties remembers a Malayan childhood, he is, however unconsciously, keeping open a human channel between two shores.

The second reading is sterner. Memory can also be instrumentalized. The risk is that stories like Xiao's are mined for a single moral — "overseas Chinese are forever China's" — that flattens the actual, complicated lives of Southeast Asia's ethnic Chinese, many of whom have spent generations building nations that are not China. The very phrase "roots in China" can, in the wrong hands, be read as a claim of extraterritorial loyalty, feeding exactly the "fifth column" suspicion that this blog has critiqued elsewhere.

The responsible reading sits between. We can honor a 90-year-old's genuine memory without converting it into a political brief. The diaspora's historical contribution to China is real and worth recording; so is its right to be, in Malaysia or anywhere, a settled local community with its own loyalties. Memory bridges; it should not be made to bind.

Xiao Siming's life — from a fleeing Nanyang child, to a homecoming soldier, to a builder rooted in Shanghai — is one man's answer to a question every diaspora must answer: where is home? His answer, "the roots are in China," is his to make. The task for the rest of us is to listen to it as memoir, not as mandate.

## Further reading

- **Nanyang Mechanics (南侨机工 / Nanyang Jiugong)**: During the War of Resistance, a corps of ethnic-Chinese drivers and mechanics from Southeast Asia who trucked strategic supplies along the Burma Road — among the most emblematic collective acts of overseas Chinese support for the homeland.
- **Tangshan (唐山 / "China" in diaspora vernacular)**: The old term Southeast Asian Chinese used for the motherland, not the city in Hebei but a contraction of "Tang dynasty's realm" (大唐江山), carrying the dispersed community's imagined spiritual homeland.
- **Returned Overseas Chinese (归侨 / guiqiao)**: Overseas Chinese who obtained resident status abroad or lived there long-term and later settled back in China — a distinct category in China's diaspora governance, bearing both "overseas" and "Chinese" attributes.
