# 游客天堂的反噬：泰国整顿外资与游客，照见东南亚开放模式的两难

靠旅游业立国的泰国，几十年来精心经营着对外开放的招牌；如今这块招牌正在被自己的成功反噬。据彭博社报道，从曼谷到帕岸岛、芭堤雅，“收复泰国”（Take Back Thailand）的示威接连不断，抗议外籍游客与资本挤占本地资源却不回馈社区。阿努廷政府今年7月启动“帕岸模式”专项整治，警方已逮捕超过110人；多数国家游客的免签停留期也从60天缩短至30天。这轮整顿与其说是排外，不如说是一道迟到的治理补课——当开放只带来客流与地价、却不带来规则与融合，“游客天堂”迟早变成社会情绪的火药桶。

## 反噬：从“游客天堂”到“收复泰国”

旅游业是泰国的经济支柱，直接与间接贡献据各方估算接近国内生产总值的20%。为了这门生意，泰国几十年如一日地开放：落地签变免签、免签停留一延再延，“微笑之国”的品牌由此而来。但开放的另一面正在显现。从首都曼谷到帕岸岛和芭堤雅等旅游胜地，多场“收复泰国”示威接连上演，抗议者的诉求很直白：外籍游客抢占了本地人有限的资源，却没有带来相应的经济利益和回馈。

开放从来不是单向的。当本地人觉得自己的家园正在被改造成别人的度假地，而自己的生计却被推高的房租与地价挤压，政治反弹只是时间问题。阿努廷政府显然听懂了这个信号。

## 数据：帕岸岛近70%企业有外资身影

帕岸岛是东南亚最热门的旅游胜地之一，每年接待超过100万人次的海外游客。土生土长的当地房地产投资者比差指着山坡上的豪华别墅对记者说：“现在山上的项目全都属于外国人，新建的别墅实在太多了。”

数字更能说明问题。据彭博社报道，帕岸岛和邻近苏梅岛的大约1万6800家企业中，近70%有外国人共同持股，远高于全国约12%的比率。全国均值12%，两岛却近70%——这已经不是“国际化的正常水平”，而是结构性失衡：整座岛屿的经济命脉正在易主。

## 代持：红线下的灰色生意

按泰国法律，外国人被限制持有酒店、餐厅等行业的多数股权，原则上也禁止拥有土地。但资本总有办法：找泰国人充当名义大股东，自己实际控制企业，再通过公司购地。这种“代持”结构属于违法行为，却是行业里公开的秘密。

当局已发现帕岸岛与苏梅岛超过240家公司涉及代持结构，另有数千家公司正被调查。7月启动的“帕岸模式”专项整治已扩大至普吉、甲米、苏梅等七个府，警方逮捕超过110人，另发出数十张逮捕令。

整顿值得肯定，但也要看到另一层：代持是管制与资本的“猫鼠游戏”。管制越紧、红线越硬，绕行的生意就越火。真正的问题不在于抓了多少人，而在于泰国法律与市场现实长期脱节——外资想来，法律不让，于是催生一整个灰色产业。与其事后清剿，不如把准入规则改到与真实的资本流动相匹配。

## 免签收紧：60天砍到30天

与企业整顿同步，政府把多数国家游客的免签停留期从60天缩短至30天，收窄外国人利用旅客身份长期居留和经商的空间；8月底进一步放宽驱逐外国人的适用条件，并在南部旅游区密集突击检查酒店和企业。

免签是把双刃剑。它带来客流，也带来套利者——用游客身份长住、经商、置业的人。把停留期砍半，本质上是承认此前的边界画得太宽。对真正来消费的游客影响有限，受影响最大的恰恰是那些把泰国当作“低成本居留地”的长期滞留者。

## 不只是以色列：被累积的怨气

这轮情绪的一个导火索是以色列游客。2025年，以色列访泰游客激增46%、超过40万人次，而泰国同年入境旅客整体减少7%。在帕岸岛，出现了提供希伯来文菜单的犹太洁食餐厅和面向以色列人的社区设施；一名以色列游客形容：“这里海滩很美，食物也很好，在心理上，我觉得自己就在以色列。”这句话在泰国社交媒体上激起的不是好感，而是不安——客人把“家”搬来了，主人却觉得陌生。

这与2022年俄乌开战后大批俄罗斯人旅居普吉岛的情形如出一辙。帕岸岛餐厅业主帕塔拉猜说：“他们来这里盖别墅，然后只做自己人的生意。我敢肯定，这些钱根本不会在泰国经济中循环。”

泰国朱拉隆功大学学者吉拉育指出，这股情绪并非只针对以色列人：“过去，中国人、俄罗斯人和印度人来到时，我们也看到类似情况。问题一直在累积，只不过现在集中爆发。”这句话值得所有在东南亚做生意的中国人细读。当地人的不满不是排外那么简单，而是分配问题的爆发：外来群体的商业网络自成一体，收益在内部循环，本地社区只承担了房价与物价的代价。

## 开放模式的焦虑：东南亚能否答好这道题

帕岸岛不是孤例。随着亚洲社会愈发自信，从峇厘岛到越南，类似的“外来者与本地人”的紧张都在抬头；吉拉育点出，当地年轻人开始质疑是否还要继续容忍这种“新殖民主义”现象。这个词很重，但它指向的问题并不玄：外来长期居留者利用当地便宜的资源和劳动力，却对在地文化缺乏敏感度，收益外流、成本留下。

东南亚的开放不会逆转，也不该逆转，但“无边界开放”的时代正在结束。接下来真正考验各国治理能力的，不是把门关多紧，而是规则画多细：外资准入如何与监管能力匹配，长住经济如何纳税、雇本地人、回馈社区。对中国投资者和游客来说，这同样是一面镜子——融入而非飞地，才是长久之计。

## 延伸阅读

**代持结构（Nominee structure）**：外国投资者借泰国名义股东持有企业、规避外资持股限制的安排，在泰国属违法，是本轮整顿的核心对象。

**飞地经济（Enclave economy）**：外来群体形成自成一体的商业与生活网络，收益在内部循环、不流入本地经济，是“游客天堂”不满情绪的结构根源。

**收复泰国（Take Back Thailand）**：2026年起在泰国多地爆发的民间示威，抗议外籍游客与资本挤占本地资源，推动政府收紧免签并整顿外资。

# The Backlash Against the Tourist Paradise: Thailand's Crackdown on Foreign Capital and Visitors Exposes Southeast Asia's Openness Dilemma

Thailand has built its economy on tourism, cultivating the branding of an open "Land of Smiles" for decades. Now that brand is being consumed by its own success. According to Bloomberg, "Take Back Thailand" protests have spread from Bangkok to Koh Phangan and Pattaya, with demonstrators accusing foreign visitors and capital of squeezing local resources while giving little back. In July the Anutin government launched a special rectification campaign modelled on Koh Phangan; police have arrested more than 110 people, and visa-free stays for most nationalities have been cut from 60 days to 30. This is less xenophobia than overdue governance: when openness delivers footfall and land prices but not rules or integration, a "tourist paradise" eventually becomes a pressure cooker.

## The backlash: from "tourist paradise" to "Take Back Thailand"

Tourism is the pillar of Thailand's economy, directly and indirectly contributing close to a fifth of GDP by most estimates. To court this business, Thailand has opened relentlessly: visas on arrival gave way to visa-free entry, stays were extended again and again, and the "Land of Smiles" brand was born. But the other side of openness is now showing. From Bangkok to Koh Phangan and Pattaya, "Take Back Thailand" protests have multiplied, and the demand is blunt: foreign visitors are occupying scarce local resources without delivering commensurate benefits.

Openness has never been a one-way street. When locals feel their homeland is being turned into someone else's resort while rising rents and land prices squeeze their livelihoods, political backlash is only a matter of time. The Anutin government has clearly registered the signal.

## The numbers: nearly 70 percent of firms on Phangan have foreign stakes

Koh Phangan is one of Southeast Asia's most popular destinations, receiving more than one million foreign visitors a year. Pichet, a local property investor born and raised on the island, pointed at the luxury villas covering the hillsides and told reporters: "Everything on the hills now belongs to foreigners. There are simply too many new villas."

The data say even more. According to Bloomberg, of the roughly 16,800 businesses on Koh Phangan and neighbouring Koh Samui, nearly 70 percent have foreign co-owners — far above the national average of about 12 percent. A 12 percent national mean against nearly 70 percent on two islands is not "normal internationalisation"; it is structural imbalance. The economic arteries of an entire island are changing hands.

## Nominee structures: the grey trade behind the legal red line

Thai law restricts foreign majority ownership of hotels and restaurants, and in principle bars foreigners from owning land. But capital always finds a way: use Thai citizens as nominee shareholders, keep real control, and buy land through the company. Such "nominee" arrangements are illegal — and an open secret in the industry.

Authorities have identified more than 240 companies on the two islands using nominee structures, with thousands more under investigation. The "Phangan model" campaign launched in July has expanded to seven provinces including Phuket, Krabi and Samui; police have arrested more than 110 people and issued dozens of arrest warrants.

The crackdown deserves credit, but there is another layer: nominee structures are a cat-and-mouse game between regulation and capital. The tighter the rules and the harder the red line, the busier the workaround. The real issue is not how many arrests have been made but that Thai law has long been out of sync with market reality — capital wants in, the law says no, and a grey industry grows in between. Rather than sweeping up after the fact, it would be better to align market-access rules with how capital actually moves.

## Tightening the visa: 60 days cut to 30

Alongside the corporate purge, the government cut visa-free stays for most nationalities from 60 days to 30, narrowing the space for foreigners to reside and do business on tourist status; in late August it further eased the conditions for deporting foreigners and has been conducting intensive inspections of hotels and businesses in the southern tourist belt.

Visa-free entry is a double-edged sword. It brings visitors — and arbitrageurs who live, trade and buy property as "tourists". Halving the stay is, in effect, an admission that the boundary was drawn too wide. Genuine tourists will barely notice; those most affected are precisely the long-term residents treating Thailand as a low-cost base.

## Not just Israelis: resentment that has been accumulating

One trigger for the current wave is Israeli visitors. In 2025 Israeli arrivals to Thailand surged 46 percent to more than 400,000, even as total inbound tourism fell 7 percent. On Koh Phangan, kosher restaurants with Hebrew menus and community facilities catering to Israelis have appeared. One Israeli visitor put it this way: "The beaches are beautiful, the food is great, and psychologically I feel I'm in Israel." On Thai social media, that quote inspired unease rather than warmth — the guests have brought their home with them, and the hosts feel like strangers.

It mirrors the wave of Russians who settled in Phuket after the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. Phatarachai, a restaurant owner on Phangan, said: "They come here, build their villas, and then only do business among themselves. I'm certain none of this money circulates in the Thai economy."

As Chirayu of Chulalongkorn University noted, the sentiment is not aimed only at Israelis: "In the past, when Chinese, Russians and Indians came, we saw similar situations. The problems have been accumulating — they are just erupting now." That line deserves a careful read from any Chinese doing business in Southeast Asia. The discontent is not simple xenophobia; it is a distribution problem erupting: expatriate business networks loop inward, benefits circulate internally, while local communities bear the cost in rising housing prices and living costs.

## The anxiety of the open model: can Southeast Asia answer the question?

Koh Phangan is not an isolated case. As Asian societies grow more confident, similar tensions between newcomers and locals are rising from Bali to Vietnam. Chirayu noted that young locals are beginning to question whether to keep tolerating what he described as a form of "neo-colonialism". The word is heavy, but the problem it points to is concrete: long-term foreign residents exploit cheaper local resources and labour while remaining culturally insensitive; the gains flow out and the costs stay behind.

Southeast Asia's openness will not be reversed — nor should it be — but the era of borderless openness is ending. The real test of governance is not how tightly the door closes but how finely the rules are drawn: matching market access to regulatory capacity, and making long-stay economies pay taxes, hire locally and give back to communities. For Chinese investors and visitors alike, this is a mirror: integration, not enclaves, is the only durable way.

## Further reading

**Nominee structure**: an arrangement in which foreign investors hold businesses through Thai nominee shareholders to skirt foreign-ownership limits; illegal in Thailand and the central target of the current crackdown.

**Enclave economy**: self-contained commercial and social networks formed by outsider communities, in which profits circulate internally rather than flowing into the local economy — the structural root of resentment in "tourist paradises".

**Take Back Thailand**: a grassroots protest movement spreading across Thailand in 2026 against foreign visitors and capital squeezing local resources, which has pushed the government to tighten visa-free entry and crack down on nominee firms.
