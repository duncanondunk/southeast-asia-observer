# 三成新加坡人松口核电：小红点的能源赌局与“能力先行”逻辑

一项覆盖东盟11国的气候民调显示，31.4%的新加坡受访者把核能视为本国最具潜力的清洁能源选项，比例居东盟之首，较2024年大幅上升11.1个百分点。在长久被视为核能“禁区”的花园城市，这道心理防线正在松动。但数据背后，是一场关于土地资源、区域安全与战略耐心的复杂算计——新加坡未必真要建核电站，却必须把“能否建”的能力先备齐。

## 数字：三成新加坡人松口

据尤索夫伊萨东南亚研究院与新加坡科技设计大学李光耀创新城市中心联合发布的《东南亚气候展望2026》调查，这项在6月8日至7月13日进行、回收3706份问卷的研究发现，太阳能仍以65%的支持率高居区域清洁能源榜首，水电（33%）与生物燃料（27.4%）次之。但在新加坡，核能的接受度达到31.4%，显著高于区域其他经济体。更值得注意的是趋势：从2023年的14.7%、2024年的20.3%，到如今的31.4%，三年间几乎翻了一番。新加坡国立大学学者陈昭勋指出，这一变化源于“除了太阳能，新加坡几乎没有其他大规模本土低碳选项”。

## 为何偏偏是新加坡

这看似反直觉。一个面积仅700多平方公里、连淡水都要进口的城邦，为什么反而最接纳核能？答案恰恰在它的“小”。太阳能受国土与日照效率所限，难以独挑大梁；风电、水电更无施展空间。核能因此成为一种“不同命题”：它能在境内提供可观的低碳基荷电力，同时把能源系统的对外依赖从“全进口”拓宽到“部分自产”。对长期被“四面环海、资源匮乏”叙事包围的新加坡而言，这是一种稀缺的主动权。

## 被打破的禁忌

核电在新加坡长期是政治禁忌。建国以来，历届政府都回避这一选项，公众也因安全与选址疑虑敬而远之。但禁忌的松动并非凭空而来：政府已将小型模块化反应堆等先进核技术纳入长期净零规划，并计划于2027年接受国际原子能机构的“综合核基础设施评估”。总理黄循财在国庆群众大会上提出，将毛广岛、苏东岛、实马高岛及周边小岛合并为大型新岛，承载“新一代发电基础设施”，更引发外界对核电选址的广泛猜测。换言之，决策层已在不动声色地为可能性铺路。

## 邻国已经抢跑

真正让新加坡焦虑的，是周边。印尼、菲律宾、泰国、越南都已将核电纳入研究或准备阶段；越南重启宁顺项目，菲律宾规划2032年投产1.2吉瓦机组，印尼瞄准2030年代初投运小型模块化反应堆。当邻居纷纷把核电从纸面推向工地，新加坡即便自己按兵不动，也将被动卷入一个核电密度上升的区域电网。正如陈昭勋所言，“新加坡发生的事，影响会溢出边境”。

## 现实的三道坎

然而，“接受度上升”与“机组落地”之间隔着三道坎。其一，邻避效应：一旦具体到厂址与安全，支持率很可能回落——这是全球核电的共性难题。其二，地理风险：新加坡地处地壳稳定区本是优势，但整个东南亚坐落在环太平洋火山带边缘，跨境安全外部性极强。其三，时间：小型模块化反应堆即便获批，也是十年量级工程，远水难救近火。核能充其量是2035年后的选项，远水救不了当下的电力焦虑。

## 不是一国的事：区域维度

这正是新加坡叙事的关键转折：核电从来不是单一国家的内部事务。一座反应堆的冷却、应急与废料处理，都会成为整个区域的共同风险。因此，比起孤注一掷建电站，更现实的路径是加快推进东盟电网——把老挝、泰国、马来西亚的再生水电与新加坡的需求互联。傅海燕在报告发布式上强调，能源危机恰恰说明区域电网与气候融资的紧迫性；新加坡的气候融资平台已为区域绿色项目筹得约82亿元人民币（约11.4亿美元），便是这类“软基建”的先手。

## “能力先行”的小国逻辑

于是我们看到一种典型的小国逻辑：未必建，但必须先懂、先备、先合规。即便国际原子能机构的审查或本国研究最终判定“核电并非正确路径”，这些前期投入也不算浪费——因为当核能成为东南亚能源组合的组成部分时，新加坡必须拥有评估、监管与应急的话语权。这是一种把“选择权”握在手里的战略耐心。对观察东南亚能源转型的人而言，新加坡的核电故事，主角或许不是反应堆，而是这个城市国家面对不确定性时那套“地基先打牢”的生存哲学。

## 延伸阅读

**小型模块化反应堆（Small Modular Reactor）**：单机功率通常小于300兆瓦、可模块化制造与部署的下一代核技术，因占地小、安全性高被视为城市国家潜在选项，但商用与监管框架仍不成熟。

**东盟电网（ASEAN Power Grid）**：旨在把成员国电网互联、实现多边电力交易的一体化计划；老挝—泰国—马来西亚—新加坡电力集成项目已验证跨境再生水电交易可行，被视为比单体核电站更近的低碳路径。

**综合核基础设施评估（Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review）**：国际原子能机构用以评估一国是否具备引入核电所需法律、监管、安全与人才基础的阶段性审查，新加坡计划2027年接受该评估。

# A Third of Singaporeans Warm to Nuclear Power: The City-State's Energy Gamble and a Logic of 'Capability First'

A climate survey covering all eleven ASEAN members has found that 31.4 per cent of Singaporean respondents regard nuclear energy as the clean-power option with the greatest potential in the city-state — the highest acceptance rate in Southeast Asia, and an 11.1-point jump from 2024. In a garden city long treated as a nuclear no-go zone, that psychological line is eroding. But behind the numbers lies a tangled calculation of land, regional security and strategic patience: Singapore may never actually build a reactor, yet it must first acquire the capability to decide whether it could.

## The numbers: a third open up

The Southeast Asia Climate Outlook 2026 Survey, jointly released by the ISEAS – Yusof Ishak Institute and the Lee Kuan Yew Centre for Innovative Cities at the Singapore University of Technology and Design, ran from 8 June to 13 July and drew 3,706 responses. Solar remained the regional favourite at 65 per cent, followed by hydropower (33 per cent) and biofuels (27.4 per cent). But in Singapore, nuclear acceptance reached 31.4 per cent — markedly above the rest of the region. The trend is the real story: from 14.7 per cent in 2023 to 20.3 per cent in 2024 and now 31.4 per cent, public openness has nearly doubled in three years. Tan-Soo Jie Sheng of the National University of Singapore attributes the shift to a simple constraint: "Beyond solar, Singapore has relatively few non-fossil options for large-scale domestic generation."

## Why Singapore, of all places

This looks counter-intuitive. A city-state of just over 700 square kilometres that imports even its fresh water — why would it be the most nuclear-friendly? The answer is precisely its smallness. Solar is capped by land and daylight efficiency; wind and hydro have no room to scale. Nuclear thus becomes a different proposition: a sizeable source of low-carbon baseload electricity generated within Singapore's own borders, widening energy autonomy from "fully imported" toward "partly self-produced." For a polity long defined by the narrative of a resource-scarce island, that is a rare measure of agency.

## The taboo is loosening

Nuclear power was, for decades, a political taboo in Singapore. Successive governments avoided the option, and the public kept its distance over safety and siting fears. Yet the thaw did not appear from nowhere. The state has folded advanced nuclear technologies, including small modular reactors, into long-term net-zero planning, and plans an International Atomic Energy Agency Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review in 2027. At the National Day Rally, Prime Minister Lawrence Wong proposed merging Pulau Bukom, Sudong, Semakau and surrounding islets into a single large island to host "next-generation power-generation infrastructure," prompting wide speculation about a nuclear site. In other words, the leadership has been quietly laying the groundwork for the possibility.

## Neighbours are already moving

What truly unsettles Singapore is the neighbourhood. Indonesia, the Philippines, Thailand and Vietnam have all placed nuclear power in the study or preparation phase; Vietnam has revived the Ninh Thuan project, the Philippines plans a 1.2-gigawatt plant by 2032, and Indonesia targets SMR operation in the early 2030s. As neighbours push nuclear from paper toward construction, Singapore — even if it stands still — will be drawn passively into a region of rising nuclear density. As Tan-Soo put it, "whatever happens in Singapore will have implications beyond our borders."

## Three hard realities

Still, a rising acceptance rate and an operating reactor sit on opposite sides of three barriers. First, the not-in-my-backyard effect: once siting and safety turn concrete, support tends to fall — a universal puzzle for nuclear power. Second, geography: Singapore's tectonic stability is an advantage, but the wider region sits on the Pacific Ring of Fire, where cross-border safety externalities are severe. Third, time: even if SMRs win approval, they are a decade-scale undertaking — too slow to relieve today's power anxiety. Nuclear is, at best, a post-2035 option; distant water cannot quench a present thirst.

## Not one country's call: the regional dimension

This is the pivot in Singapore's narrative: nuclear energy was never a purely domestic matter. The cooling, emergency response and waste of any plant would become shared regional risk. Therefore, a more realistic path than betting on a single plant is to accelerate the ASEAN Power Grid — interconnecting the renewable hydropower of Laos, Thailand and Malaysia with Singapore's demand. At the report launch, Minister for Sustainability and the Environment Grace Fu stressed that the energy crisis only sharpens the urgency of the regional grid and climate finance; Singapore's blended-finance platform has already raised about US$1.14 billion (roughly S$1.5 billion) for regional green projects — a first move in this "soft infrastructure."

## A small-state logic of 'capability first'

What we see, then, is a classic small-state logic: it may not build, but it must first understand, prepare and comply. Even if the IAEA review or domestic studies ultimately conclude that nuclear is not the right path, the upfront investment is not wasted — because once nuclear becomes part of Southeast Asia's energy mix, Singapore must hold a voice in assessment, regulation and emergency response. That is strategic patience aimed at keeping the option in its own hands. For observers of the region's energy transition, the protagonist of Singapore's nuclear story may not be a reactor at all, but the city-state's "lay the foundations first" philosophy for surviving uncertainty.

## Further reading

**Small Modular Reactor (SMR):** Next-generation nuclear technology, typically under 300 MW per unit and factory-built in modules; prized for small footprint and enhanced safety, yet its commercial and regulatory frameworks remain immature.

**ASEAN Power Grid:** A plan to interconnect member states' grids and enable multilateral electricity trade; the Laos–Thailand–Malaysia–Singapore Power Integration Project has already proven cross-border renewable-hydro trade feasible, a nearer-term low-carbon route than any single reactor.

**Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review (INIR):** The IAEA's phased review assessing whether a country has the legal, regulatory, safety and talent base to introduce nuclear power; Singapore plans to undergo the assessment in 2027.
