# 6亿令吉诉讼：马来西亚（Malaysia）为什么要谷歌为「清理内容」买单

据联合早报（Lianhe Zaobao）报道，马来西亚政府11月25日对谷歌母公司提起两起民事诉讼，索赔总额6亿令吉（约9.8亿元人民币）。理由既不是税，也不是反垄断，而是谷歌及旗下优管（YouTube）「未能及时防制平台上的有害内容」。据路透社报道，两起诉讼各索3亿令吉；马来西亚通讯及多媒体委员会说，这笔钱用来弥补监管机构为处理超过5万条有害内容投入的人力与资源。这不是传统的罚款逻辑，而是监管部门向平台追讨自己替平台干的活——把审核成本第一次明码标价。

值得把这件事从「科技公司又被告了」的惯性反应里拎出来。东南亚（Southeast Asia）各国对平台的抱怨积累了多年，真正走进法院、把监管工时算成损害赔偿的并不多。马来西亚这一步，试探的不是谷歌的钱包，是一套新的治理算法：如果平台不清理，国家替它清理，然后开发票。

## 两起诉讼，一张结构清楚的账单

先把事实摆平。委员会11月25日的文告说，两起诉讼各索偿3亿令吉，每起都拆成1亿令吉一般损害赔偿、1亿令吉加重损害赔偿与1亿令吉惩罚性损害赔偿。第一款补实际支出，后两款不为补损失，而是为了标记行为的严重程度、形成威慑。这个结构说明诉讼目标不止于「回本」。

第一起针对网赌、诈骗与欺诈内容，指控谷歌违反法定责任、执照条件，并有疏忽之嫌；第二起针对网红帕帕戈莫（Papagomo）影片被转载后未在规定时间内下架。委员会强调，谷歌持有应用服务供应商执照，受《1998年通讯与多媒体法令》和《2025年网络安全法令》约束。换句话说，诉讼不是把平台当普通旁观者，而是把它当成领了牌照、承担特定义务的持牌机构。牌照逻辑一旦成立，平台就不能再用「我们只是技术中介」来推卸责任。

## 99.8%是网赌：这不是言论自由之争

投诉数据是最有说服力的部分。今年1月1日至10月28日，委员会向优管提交7万4703份举报，其中7万4569份涉及网络赌博，占99.8%。这句话很重要：它把整场争论的重心，从「政府是不是要管住批评声音」拉回到一个更难反驳的地面——赌博、诈骗与儿童安全。

外媒报道常把东南亚的内容监管简单归类为「威权工具」，这次的数据不太配合这种叙事。当99.8%的举报指向网赌，监管机构说「我们是在清赌摊，不是在清异见」，至少在数字上站得住。评论者若仍要把它读成纯粹的政治审查，就得先解释为什么平台连赌广告都拖着不删。

但这里也有另一面。第二起诉讼涉及的内容来自一位曾批评委员会的网红，吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）高庭6月19日已就包括涉嫌诽谤委员会在内的内容发出临时禁令。公共利益与机构声誉在同一份诉状里并排出现，边界并不总是清楚。数字能证明清理网赌的正当性，却不能自动证明每一条被要求下架的内容都属于同一类别。监管的可信度，恰恰取决于它能否把这两类内容分开处理。

## 4950次沉默，比赔偿数字更刺眼

同样值得盯住的是回应速度。委员会披露，在7万4703份举报中，3万2781起在一天内得到回应或下架；1468起超过30日才有动作，35起超过90日；另有4950起至今没有任何回应。约四成四在一天内处理，约6.6%石沉大海——这不是「平台完全不作为」，而是「不作为集中在长尾」。

长尾恰恰是受害者所在的地方。热门赌广告容易被自动系统抓到，伪装成生活分享、评论区链接或短视频导流的内容，才是监管举报的主要来源。平台愿意在一日内处理被明确标注的投诉，却让没有商业标签、没有舆情压力的投诉悬着，暴露的是资源分配问题，不是技术能力问题。把这个问题说成「算法不够聪明」，等于替平台把选择性的勤勉包装成客观限制。

赔偿金额因此有了另一层含义：它是对「选择性合规」的计价。监管机构说，索赔反映处理5万多则有害内容所耗的行政成本。翻译过来就是：你们省下的人工，我们自己垫了；这笔账不能一直由纳税人承担。

## 12小时下架令：把法院禁令变成平台的速度考核

第二起诉讼的核心是时间。委员会10月19日发出书面通知，要求谷歌在12小时内下架被转载的帕帕戈莫影片，谷歌没有照办。此前，委员会5月27日已就涉嫌诽谤提诉，高庭6月19日颁布临时禁令；据马来西亚媒体披露，案件10月23日入禀，定于12月8日进行案件管理。

12小时是一个极其苛刻的标准，几乎不给人工复核留余地。支持者会说，法院已经裁定，平台没有理由再拖；反对者则会问：谁来判断转载影片与禁令针对的内容「相同」？剪辑、截图、二次评论是否算转载？平台若为了赶时限宁可错删，责任由谁承担？这些问题不是为拖延找借口，而是任何可执行的下架制度都必须回答的程序问题。

更关键的是「被转载」三个字。禁令约束的是原发布者，诉讼却指向平台对第三方转载的处理速度。这实际上把司法命令的效力延伸到内容分发的每一个节点。东南亚多语、多族群的社会里，同一段视频可能在不同语言社群中被反复重述；如果平台只能在「全面封禁」与「完全放任」之间二选一，12小时的时钟就会把复杂性压扁成一次简单删除。速度提高了，精确度未必提高。

## 一边招商150亿美元，一边递上法院传票

时间线让这起诉讼更有意思。就在几周前，谷歌宣布2027年至2028年将在马来西亚投入150亿美元（约1080亿元人民币），用于数据中心、洁净能源、水资源保障与技能培训。一边是创纪录的投资承诺，一边是创纪录的内容索赔，看起来矛盾，其实是同一套策略的两面。

马来西亚的逻辑并不复杂：市场准入不是免费礼物，而是谈判筹码。你进来建算力，就要遵守这里的牌照与法院命令；你享受东道国的电力、水与人才，就要分担治理有害内容的成本。把投资与诉讼对立起来看，是低估了中等国家的议价能力。真正的问题在于这种议价能否持续——如果平台把诉讼当成一次性成本，把投资当成换取宽容的筹码，监管就又回到了原点。

这也是为什么索赔结构里要有惩罚性赔偿。一般损害赔偿可以当作做生意的成本，惩罚性赔偿则是要改变成本收益计算。马来西亚在赌：比起罚款，它更需要一个能被写进平台合规手册的先例。

## 从《1998年法令》到《2025年法令》：监管工具箱换了齿轮

这起诉讼的法律组合值得单独看。委员会援引的是《1998年通讯与多媒体法令》《2025年网络安全法令》以及执照条件，路径是「法定义务＋牌照义务＋民事赔偿」，而不是单纯依靠行政罚款。它更接近一种责任型监管：国家不再只是发号施令，而是把执行不力的后果交给法院定价。

这套做法与欧盟《数字服务法》的思路有亲缘关系——都要求平台对系统性风险负责。但差别也很明显：欧盟以全球营业额为罚基，马来西亚以自身行政支出为索赔基准。前者追求震慑，后者强调补偿。对小国而言，后者在政治上更容易说服公众：我们花的每一分钱，都要从平台那里要回来。

风险也在这里。以行政成本为标尺，赔偿上限天然受制于监管机构能证明自己花了多少人力，而不是平台从违规中获益多少。若评估结果只是「审核部门加了几个人的预算」，诉讼的威慑就会停留在账面上。把治理成本转嫁出去是合理的第一步，但平台是否因此改变排序机制、推荐逻辑与多语审核投入，才是真正的分水岭。

## 谁在替平台做本分工作

把两起诉讼放在一起，最刺眼的不是金额，而是角色的错位。平台靠用户注意力赚钱，也靠内容分发制造风险；但当风险外溢为网赌、诈骗与诽谤时，清点、举报、下架、复核的工作却由监管机构承担。委员会说自己处理了5万多则有害内容，这句话听起来像政绩，读起来更像一份外包账单。

东南亚各国正在同时经历两件事：数字广告收入向少数平台集中，治理成本向各国财政分散。若不改变这种不对称，平台只要把合规预算维持在「低于被诉概率×赔偿额」的水平，就会继续把治理当成可延迟的支出。马来西亚这次把被诉概率提高了，也把赔偿额明确标出；它能否改变平台的算式，要看后续判决与执法，而不是文告的措辞。

对华人社会而言，还有一层实际问题。网络赌博与诈骗内容往往以华语社群为目标，跨平台、跨语言、跨境流动；审核体系若只熟悉英语或马来语的语义线索，中文关键词与变体就会长期处在盲区。要求平台承担本地化审核能力，不应被理解为额外的政治要求，而是它拿牌照经营就必须兑现的服务义务。

## 判决之外，真正要盯的三件事

这起诉讼离终局还很远：谷歌尚未公开回应诉状细节，法院是否认可「系统性失职」的归责路径也未知。因此，别急着宣布平台监管进入了新纪元。接下来有三件事值得持续追踪。

其一，举报回应时间是否真的下降。若一年后4950起「无回应」变成几百起，诉讼就产生了判决之外的效果；若数字纹丝不动，6亿令吉只是一张漂亮的新闻标题。其二，委员会是否把网赌、诈骗与政治性内容严格分轨处理。前者需要执法协作，后者需要更高的证据与程序门槛，混在一起只会消耗监管公信力。其三，其他平台是否收到同类通知。如果只有谷歌被追讨，外界有理由怀疑选择性执法；如果优管、脸书与短视频平台一视同仁，规则才真正立起来。

马来西亚给出的不是一个答案，而是一次测试：中小国家能不能用法律与牌照，把跨国平台从「合作者」变成「可问责者」。测试成败不取决于索赔数字有多大，而取决于下一次举报，平台会不会在12小时内给出认真回答。

## 延伸阅读

**平台责任（Platform liability）**：平台因自身在内容分发、审核与风控中的作为或不作为，而对第三方内容造成的损害承担的法律责任。

**内容审核（Content moderation）**：平台依规则对违法或有害内容进行识别、下架与复核的机制，其效率与准确性取决于投入的语言与人力配置。

**惩罚性赔偿（Exemplary damages）**：超出实际损失的赔偿，用以惩罚严重过失并威慑同类行为，与补偿性赔偿的功能不同。

# Malaysia's RM600 million suit: putting a price on platform neglect

Lianhe Zaobao reports that the Malaysian government filed two civil suits against Google's parent company on 25 November, seeking a total of RM600 million (about US$140 million). The cause of action is neither tax nor antitrust; it is Google and YouTube's alleged failure to take adequate measures against harmful content. Reuters reports that each suit claims RM300 million. The Malaysian Communications and Multimedia Commission (MCMC) says the sum reflects the staff time and resources it spent dealing with more than 50,000 items of harmful content. This is not the logic of a fine. It is a regulator billing a platform for the work the regulator had to do in the platform's place — the first time the cost of moderation has been given an explicit price tag.

It is worth pulling this case out of the reflexive "another tech company sued" category. Southeast Asian governments have complained about platforms for years; few have gone to court and converted regulatory man-hours into damages. What Malaysia is testing is not the size of Google's wallet but a new governing algorithm: if the platform will not clean up, the state cleans up for it — and then sends the invoice.

## Two suits, one clearly structured bill

Start with the facts. In its 25 November statement, MCMC said each suit seeks RM300 million, split into RM100 million in general damages, RM100 million in aggravated damages and RM100 million in exemplary damages. The first head compensates actual expenditure; the other two are not about reimbursement but about marking the seriousness of the conduct and deterring repetition. The structure shows that the objective is more than breaking even.

The first suit concerns gambling, scams and fraud, alleging breaches of statutory duties and licence conditions as well as negligence. The second concerns videos by the blogger known as Papagomo that were republished on YouTube and allegedly not taken down within the required time. MCMC stresses that Google holds an application service provider licence and is bound by the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998, the Online Safety Act 2025 and the conditions of that licence. In other words, the platform is not treated as a bystander but as a licensee with specific obligations. Once that logic is accepted, "we are only a technical intermediary" stops being an adequate defence.

## 99.8 percent is gambling: this is not a free-speech fight

The complaint data is the most persuasive part of the case. Between 1 January and 28 October this year, MCMC submitted 74,703 reports to YouTube, of which 74,569 — 99.8 percent — concerned online gambling. That sentence matters: it moves the centre of gravity away from "is the government trying to muzzle criticism" and back to a much harder charge to rebut — gambling, fraud and child safety.

Foreign coverage often files Southeast Asian content regulation under the convenient heading of "authoritarian tool". These figures do not fit that narrative. When 99.8 percent of reports concern online gambling, the regulator's claim that it is clearing betting operations rather than dissent has at least numerical support. Anyone who still reads the case as pure political censorship owes an explanation of why the platform could not remove gambling advertisements either.

There is, however, a second side. The content in the second suit comes from a blogger who had criticised the commission; the Kuala Lumpur High Court issued an interim injunction on 19 June over material that allegedly defamed MCMC. Public interest and institutional reputation sit side by side in the same statement of claim, and the boundary is not always clean. The numbers establish the legitimacy of clearing gambling content; they cannot automatically establish that every item ordered down belongs to the same category. Regulatory credibility depends precisely on keeping the two tracks separate.

## 4,950 silences are more damning than the damages

The response times deserve equal attention. Of the 74,703 reports, MCMC said 32,781 were answered or removed within a day; 1,468 took more than 30 days and 35 more than 90; another 4,950 received no response at all. Roughly two-thirds were handled within a day, and about 6.6 percent disappeared into a void. This is not "the platform does nothing"; it is "inaction concentrated in the long tail".

The long tail is where the victims are. Flashy betting advertisements are easy for automated systems to catch. Material disguised as lifestyle posts, comment-section links or short-video funnels is the main source of regulatory reports. A platform that responds within a day to clearly labelled complaints but leaves unlabelled, low-visibility complaints pending is revealing a resource-allocation choice, not a technical limit. Calling it "the algorithm is not smart enough" packages selective diligence as an objective constraint.

The damages claim therefore acquires a second meaning: it is a price on selective compliance. MCMC says the claim reflects the administrative cost of handling more than 50,000 harmful items. Translated: the labour the platform saved, the regulator advanced; that bill cannot be left with taxpayers indefinitely.

## A 12-hour takedown order turns a court injunction into a speed test

Time is the core of the second suit. On 19 October MCMC issued a written notice requiring Google to remove the republished Papagomo videos within 12 hours; Google did not comply. MCMC had sued the blogger for alleged defamation on 27 May, and the High Court granted an interim injunction on 19 June. According to Malaysian media, the case was filed on 23 October with case management set for 8 December.

Twelve hours is an extremely tight standard, leaving almost no room for human review. Supporters will say the court has already ruled, so the platform has no reason to delay. Critics will ask who decides whether a republished clip is the "same" content as the material covered by the injunction. Does a re-edit, a screenshot or a second-round commentary count as republication? If a platform prefers wrongful removal to missing the deadline, who bears the responsibility? These are not excuses for delay; they are procedural questions any workable takedown regime must answer.

The phrase "republished" is the more consequential one. The injunction binds the original publisher, while the suit targets the platform's handling of third-party reposts. That effectively extends a court order to every node of content distribution. In a multilingual, multi-ethnic society, one clip can be retold across language communities; if a platform must choose between blanket blocking and complete permissiveness, the 12-hour clock flattens complexity into a single deletion. Speed rises; accuracy need not.

## Courting US$15 billion while filing a writ

The timeline makes the suit more revealing. Only weeks earlier, Google announced it would invest US$15 billion in Malaysia across 2027 and 2028 in data centres, clean energy, water security and skills training. A record investment commitment and a record content claim sit side by side. They look contradictory but are two faces of one strategy.

Malaysia's logic is not complicated: market access is not a free gift, it is negotiating leverage. You come here to build compute, so you obey local licensing and court orders; you draw on the host country's power, water and talent, so you share the cost of governing harmful content. Reading the investment and the lawsuit as opposition underestimates a middle-sized state's bargaining capacity. The real question is whether that bargaining can be sustained. If the platform treats the suit as a one-off cost and the investment as a licence for tolerance, regulation returns to square one.

That is why the damages structure includes exemplary damages. General damages can be booked as an operating expense; exemplary damages are meant to change the cost-benefit calculation. Malaysia is betting that, more than a fine, it needs a precedent that can be written into a platform's compliance manual.

## From the 1998 Act to the 2025 Act: a different regulatory gearbox

The legal combination deserves separate attention. MCMC relies on the Communications and Multimedia Act 1998, the Online Safety Act 2025 and licence conditions — a route of statutory duty plus licensing obligation plus civil damages, rather than administrative fines alone. It is closer to liability-based regulation: the state no longer merely issues instructions but hands the consequences of non-compliance to a court for pricing.

There is a family resemblance to the EU's Digital Services Act, which also demands platform responsibility for systemic risk. The difference is the measure: the EU calibrates fines to global turnover, while Malaysia calibrates its claim to its own administrative expenditure. One seeks deterrence, the other reimbursement. For a smaller country, the second is easier to sell domestically: every ringgit we spent, we will ask back.

The risk sits in the same place. Measuring damages by administrative cost caps the claim at what the regulator can prove it spent, not at what the platform gained from non-compliance. If the outcome amounts to "the review unit hired a few more people", the deterrent remains on paper. Transferring governance costs is a reasonable first step; whether platforms change their ranking systems, recommendation logic and multilingual moderation budgets is the real dividing line.

## Who is doing whose job

Put the two suits together and the most striking thing is not the amount but the role reversal. Platforms earn from attention and generate risk through distribution; when that risk spills over into gambling, scams and defamation, the inventory, reporting, removal and review work falls on the regulator. MCMC's statement that it handled more than 50,000 harmful items reads like an achievement and sounds more like an outsourced invoice.

Southeast Asia is living through two trends at once: digital advertising revenue concentrates in a handful of platforms while governance costs disperse across national budgets. Without changing that asymmetry, a platform only has to keep its compliance spending below the probability of being sued multiplied by the damages, and moderation remains a deferrable expense. Malaysia has raised the probability and put a number on the damages. Whether that changes the platform's arithmetic will depend on the judgment and enforcement that follow, not on the wording of a statement.

For Chinese communities there is an additional practical layer. Gambling and scam content often targets Chinese-speaking audiences, moving across platforms, languages and borders. If moderation systems recognise only English or Malay semantic cues, Chinese keywords and variants stay in a blind spot for years. Demanding local moderation capacity is not an extra political demand; it is a service obligation owed by a licensee.

## Beyond the judgment: three things to watch

The case is a long way from resolution. Google has yet to address the details publicly, and it is unclear whether the court will accept the theory of systemic failure. So it is too early to announce a new era of platform regulation. Three things are worth tracking.

First, whether response times actually fall. If a year later the "no response" bucket has shrunk from 4,950 to a few hundred, the suit will have produced effects beyond the judgment; if the figures barely move, RM600 million will remain a handsome headline. Second, whether MCMC keeps gambling and scam enforcement strictly separate from politically contentious content. The former calls for enforcement cooperation, the latter for a higher evidentiary and procedural threshold; merging them only spends regulatory credibility. Third, whether other platforms receive comparable notices. If only Google is pursued, selective enforcement will be a fair suspicion; if YouTube, Facebook and short-video services are treated alike, a rule begins to exist.

Malaysia has offered not an answer but a test: can a mid-sized state use law and licensing to turn a multinational platform from a partner into an accountable party? The test will not be decided by the size of the claim, but by whether the platform gives a serious answer to the next report within 12 hours.

## Further reading

**Platform liability**: A platform's legal responsibility for harm from third-party content arising from its own decisions about distribution, moderation and risk controls.

**Content moderation**: The mechanisms by which a platform identifies, removes and reviews unlawful or harmful content; effectiveness depends on language coverage and human resources.

**Exemplary damages**: Compensation beyond actual loss, imposed to punish serious misconduct and deter similar conduct, distinct from compensatory damages.
