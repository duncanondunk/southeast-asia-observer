# 吉隆坡机场查获逾1500万新元人工智能晶片 走私网络现形

10月15日，吉隆坡国际机场自由贸易区的海关人员打开30台服务器，发现里面塞满了先进的人工智能晶片。这批货物估值约4950万林吉特（约1555万新元，约合8700万元人民币），报关时却写着普通的"电子零件"。13天后的10月28日，机场关税局主管阿斯帕丽拉向媒体证实，这并非孤立事件，而是指向一个把马来西亚当作隐秘走廊、将受限半导体转运到第三国的走私网络。此案生动说明：中美科技博弈如何把东南亚的物流枢纽变成了新的前线，而原本用来封堵的管制，为何总是"漏水"。

## 事件：吉隆坡机场起获30台人工智能晶片服务器

数字本身已足够触目惊心。10月15日的例行检查中，海关在吉隆坡机场自贸区查到30台装载高性能人工智能晶片的服务器。货物来自某个亚洲国家，计划转口到另一个亚洲国家，但官方以调查仍在进行为由拒绝透露具体来去向。可以确定的是，这些服务器被虚报为"电子零件"，目的正是借道马来西亚转运时规避查缉。

阿斯帕丽拉表示，同一个网络正是6月另一起走私未遂案的主使——当时在同一自贸区查扣了72台服务器。她说，该网络至少涉及5起案件，累计起获晶片总值超过1亿林吉特（约合1.6亿元人民币）。一家协助安排运输的马来西亚企业已被传唤协助调查。同一机场、数月之内再度得手，说明这套手法既猖狂，又高度可复制。

## 手法：谎报货品、借道转口规避查缉

手法简单，却显然有效。把先进人工智能晶片塞进服务器，报关单上写成平平无奇的"电子零件"，再利用自由贸易区——这里的开箱查验是例外而非常态——让货物溜过去。马来西亚身处全球最繁忙的海空走廊之一，自贸区本就为"减摩擦"而生；对走私集团而言，这种低摩擦环境恰恰是可乘之机。

这并非马来西亚独有。邻国新加坡2025年就曾爆出欺诈丑闻：戴尔与美超微供应的服务器（可能内含受美国管制的英伟达晶片）被谎称为本地终端用户所用，随后被转运，马来西亚正是疑似中转地之一。美国检方此后已对多起通过东南亚空壳实体向中方输送受限服务器的个人与企业提出指控。马来西亚6月与10月的两度查扣显示，同样的空壳安排、货品贴错标签、借道转运套路正在被反复使用。

## 背景：马来西亚为何成了"中转站"

为何是马来西亚？答案不复杂：地利加上制度诱因。作为快速崛起的数据中心枢纽，马来西亚合法进口海量服务器与半导体，真实需求为非法货品提供了掩护；其自贸区与身处主要制造与消费市场之间的战略位置，使之成为再出口的天然节点。而涉案晶片多为美国来源，真正让这批货"非法"的，是美国出口管制而非马来西亚本国法律。

这给吉隆坡出了一道难题。马来西亚希望被视为全球科技供应链中可信、守规则的目的地，但正是这种开放吸引了投资，也吸引了转口套利。当局多次承诺要"堵住任何可能被利用的漏洞"，可10月的查扣说明，漏洞被探测的速度，快过被封堵的速度。

## 规制：一年前的出口管制与《战略贸易法》

马来西亚并非无力可施。依据2010年《战略贸易法》，该国去年在美国施压盟友、阻止敏感技术经第三国流入中方之后，针对美国来源高性能人工智能晶片的出口、过境与转运引入许可要求。如今相关转移须取得战略贸易许可，一旦怀疑被转用还须提前申报。

法律框架已经搭起，难点在执行。海关确实能截下货物，可每一次成功查扣，只露出跨越多个司法辖区、藏身层层公司形式背后的网络冰山一角。美方的回应也在升级：参议员已敦促商务部暂停面向东南亚中间商的先进英伟达晶片出口许可，并要求落实穿透至最终用户的"了解你的客户"尽职调查。

## 困局：封堵难，猫鼠游戏持续

退一步看，马来西亚的查扣折射出一个更宏大的现实：对尖端晶片的出口管制，在全球化的供应链里极难执行。晶片体积小、通用性强、易于藏匿在合法硬件之中；转运的商业诱因巨大；而中间的司法辖区既多又监管松弛。随着华盛顿收紧规则，走私手法也在进化——虚构终端用户、复制序列号、用无标识纸箱重新包装。

对东南亚而言，沦为"漏水的夹心层"，代价既是法律也是声誉。新加坡与马来西亚都把经济建立在"可信枢纽"之上；每一次高调查扣，都招来美国更严的审视，以及更迫切地自查港口的压力。10月15日的查扣是马来西亚海关的一场胜仗，却也提醒人们：在半导体的冷战里，边境就在下一个集装箱被打开的地方——而某处，已有人填好了下一个。

## 延伸阅读

- **战略贸易法（Strategic Trade Act）**：马来西亚管制战略物资出口、过境与转运的法律，去年针对人工智能晶片设立的许可制度即以此为据。
- **了解你的客户（Know-Your-Customer）**：美国出口管制中的尽职调查要求，供应商须核实受限晶片的最终用户，而非轻信中间方的申报。

# Over S$15.5 Million in AI Chips Seized at Kuala Lumpur Airport as a Smuggling Network Surfaces

On October 15, customs officers at Kuala Lumpur International Airport's free trade zone pried open 30 server units and found them packed with advanced artificial intelligence chips. The shipment, valued at about 49.5 million ringgit (roughly S$15.55 million, ~US$12 million), had been declared as ordinary "electronic components." Thirteen days later, on October 28, airport customs director Aspalila Ag Tuah told reporters that the seizure was not a one-off: it pointed to a syndicate that has used Malaysia as a covert corridor to move restricted semiconductors onward to a third country. The case is a vivid illustration of how the US–China technology contest has turned Southeast Asia's logistics hubs into a new front line — and why the controls meant to contain it keep leaking.

## The incident: 30 server units with AI chips seized at KLIA

The numbers alone are striking. Customs found 30 servers loaded with high-performance AI chips during a routine inspection at the KLIA free trade zone on October 15. The cargo originated in one Asian country and was bound for another, but officials have refused to name either, citing an ongoing investigation. What is clear is that the servers were falsely declared as electronic parts, a dodge designed to slip them past scrutiny while they transited Malaysia.

Aspalila said the same network was behind a similar attempt in June, when 72 server units were seized at the same free trade zone. In total, she said, the syndicate is linked to at least five cases, with the value of chips seized exceeding 100 million ringgit (about US$24 million). A Malaysian firm that helped arrange the shipment has been called in to assist investigations. The fact that the network struck again at the same airport within months shows how brazen — and how repeatable — the playbook has become.

## The method: misdeclared cargo and transshipment to dodge controls

The technique is simple and, evidently, effective. Advanced AI chips are embedded inside servers, the servers are described on paper as mundane "electronic components," and the consignment is routed through a free trade zone where physical checks are the exception rather than the rule. Malaysia sits on one of the world's busiest air and sea corridors, and its free trade zones are built precisely to minimise friction. For a smuggling syndicate, that friction-free environment is an opportunity.

This is not unique to Malaysia. Neighbouring Singapore was rocked in 2025 by a fraud scandal in which servers supplied by Dell and Super Micro — possibly containing US-controlled Nvidia chips — were falsely described as destined for local end-users before being diverted, with Malaysia among the suspected transit points. US prosecutors have since charged multiple individuals and companies over schemes to funnel restricted servers to China through Southeast Asian front entities. Malaysia's own June and October seizures suggest the same architecture of shell arrangements, mislabelled goods and transshipment is being reused.

## The backdrop: why Malaysia became a transit hub

Why Malaysia? The short answer is geography plus incentives. As a fast-growing data-centre hub, Malaysia imports enormous volumes of servers and semiconductors legitimately; genuine demand provides cover for the illicit. Its free trade zones and strategic location between major manufacturing and consumer markets make it a natural node for re-export. And because the chips in question are of US origin, Washington's export controls — not Malaysian law alone — are what give the cargo its illicit character.

That creates a dilemma for Kuala Lumpur. Malaysia wants to be seen as a trusted, rules-based destination for the global tech supply chain, yet the same openness that attracts investment also attracts diversion. The country has repeatedly pledged to "close any loopholes" through which advanced semiconductors might pass, but the October bust shows the loopholes are being probed faster than they are sealed.

## The rules: last year's export controls and the Strategic Trade Act

Malaysia is not powerless. Under the Strategic Trade Act 2010, it introduced permit requirements last year for the export, transit and transshipment of high-performance AI chips of US origin, after Washington pressed allies to stop sensitive technology from being rerouted to China. Transfers now require a strategic trade permit, and authorities must be notified in advance where diversion is suspected.

The legal scaffolding exists; enforcement is the hard part. Customs has shown it can intercept shipments, yet each successful bust reveals only the tip of a network that spans multiple jurisdictions and hides behind layers of corporate formalism. The US response has escalated too: senators have urged Commerce to pause export licences for advanced Nvidia chips destined for Southeast Asian intermediaries, and Washington now expects "know-your-customer" diligence that reaches through to the ultimate end user.

## The dilemma: a cat-and-mouse game that won't end

Step back, and the Malaysia seizures are a symptom of a wider truth: export controls on cutting-edge chips are extraordinarily hard to enforce in a globalised supply chain. Chips are small, fungible and easy to conceal inside legitimate hardware; the commercial incentives to move them are enormous; and the jurisdictions in between are numerous and porous. As Washington tightens the rules, the smuggling methods adapt — false end-users, duplicated serial numbers, repackaging in unmarked boxes.

For Southeast Asia, the cost of being the leaky middle is reputational as much as legal. Singapore and Malaysia alike have staked their economies on being trusted hubs; every high-profile bust invites tougher US scrutiny and pressure to police their own ports more intrusively. The Oct. 15 seizure is a win for Malaysian customs, but it is also a reminder that, in the semiconductor cold war, the border is wherever the next container is opened — and someone, somewhere, is already filling the next one.

## Further reading

- **Strategic Trade Act 2010**: Malaysia's law governing the export, transit and transshipment of strategic goods, including dual-use semiconductors; the basis for last year's AI-chip permit regime.
- **Know-Your-Customer**: US export-control diligence requiring suppliers to verify the ultimate end user of restricted chips rather than trust intermediary filings.
