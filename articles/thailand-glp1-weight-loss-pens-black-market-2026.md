# 一纸"处方药"令：泰国为何堵不住减肥针黑市

## 现象：当"减肥针"成为网红标配

在泰国的抖音（TikTok）上，一类视频悄然走红：年轻女孩对着镜头给自己打"减肥针"——针管里装着司美格鲁肽，即丹麦药企诺和诺德生产的减重针剂——并配上泰语"pak pakaa"，意为"扎这一针"。对于一心想瘦、却没时间也没钱健身的这届年轻人来说，这一针成了某种成人礼。泰国医生警告，整整一代人正在自行注射这种强效代谢药物，却对针头、剂量与风险知之甚少。

这绝不只是个健康话题。它像一扇小小的窗户，映出一场全球医药狂热如何被社交媒体放大、又被"以瘦为美"的审美锚定，最终与东南亚中等收入国家薄弱的监管执行力猛烈相撞。当一款本用于二型糖尿病的药物被重新包装成生活方式的捷径，国家总是最后一个追上来的。

## 数字：合法与黑市的价格鸿沟

经济账解释了这场狂热。在泰国，由医生处方的司美格鲁肽疗程每月约需二百四十至三百六十美元（约一千七百至两千六百元人民币）；而在黑市，无牌卖家把同样的针剂卖到八十至九十美元（约五百七十至六百四十元人民币）——正如一位贩子对媒体所言，"比诊所便宜三倍"。这道价差，恰恰催生了由走私与假药构成的灰色供应链。

按区域标准，泰国绝不算穷；它本是本地区的医疗旅游枢纽，多家私立医院吸引着来自海湾国家乃至更远的患者。然而，同一股消费需求，一面撑起合法的康养产业，一面又溢入无人监管的市集——任何有抖音账号的人都能摇身变成经销商。价格落差，是市场对国家无力让正规医疗"可及且可负担"给出的判决。

## 监管：一纸处方药能否堵住漏洞

九月十五日，泰国食品药品监督管理局将减肥针列为"特别管制药品"，意味着在药房购买须持医生处方。监管机构同时承诺建立全程药品追溯系统，监控药品从出厂到终端的流向，并强调目的不是拒绝真正有需要的患者，而是帮他们拿到"质量更高、更安全、更对症"的药。

但旁观者宜收起乐观。处方药之所以有效，前提是在销售终端与边境线上有配套执法。泰国本就有着"臭名昭著的假药黑市"；倘若那些兜售无牌针剂的诊所、网店与药房无需付出真实代价，瓶身上多一个标签改变不了什么。正如一位曼谷药剂师所言，这是"全国最荒唐的产业之一，几乎毫无监管"。一道没有稽查员的政令，不过是一句愿望。

## 根源：审美、网红与医疗化的身体

要理解需求为何如此刚性，得看文化。泰国审美向来以纤瘦为上，曼谷、芭提雅、普吉的主干道上康养诊所一家挨着一家。在这片沃土上，一群年轻内容创作者登场，在短视频平台放大"这一针"的神效，却对"需要医生监管"那部分轻描淡写。

我们目睹的，是身体理想的"医疗化"：一种再正常不过的瘦身愿望，被重新框定为需要每周注射的"病症"，药企的产品则顺势成为社会焦虑的解药。危险不在分子本身——司美格鲁肽对肥胖与糖尿病确是突破——而在于一款医疗工具脱离医学、沦为消费配饰的速度：它由网红售卖，而非医生开具。

## 风险：假药、误用与无人监管的针管

人的代价已经显现。医生反映，年轻人在镜头前自行注射，却不知如何安全使用针头，并警告自行以不安全剂量滥用来路不明、不受监管的药物可能带来严重后果。假针剂——常被不当处置、暴露于高温，甚至根本是伪造的——在市场上自由流通。由于司美格鲁肽需冷链保存，从匿名卖家手中买来的针剂，最好的情况是失效，最坏的情况是致命。

"只有在试过其他方法都无效后，才应考虑减肥针，"坐拥六十万粉丝的短视频顾问、"Mom 医生"皮茨里·库马努翁提醒。她自己的平台恰恰印证了悖论：同一社交媒体，既在科普，也在诱惑；知情选择与病毒式同侪压力之间的界线，早已模糊。

## 镜鉴：东南亚的药品监管困境

泰国的困局并非孤例。在整个东南亚，全球医药资本的扩散——从诺和诺德到各类远程医疗平台——始终快过监管型国家的能力建设。印尼为假抗生素所困，菲律宾受制于未注册保健品，湄公河次区域则饱受跨境药品走私之苦。凡制度稀薄之处，黑市便来填空。

这个教训，对把"医疗旅游"当作区域成就来庆祝的人，多少有些刺耳：一个向世界营销自己的医疗体系，也必须保护本国年轻人免受同一市场阴暗面的侵害。处方药令是个开端。但在执法、教育与可负担供给三者齐步前行之前，针剂仍会不断落入没有受过训练的手中——而黑市，也仍会一直笑着。

## 延伸阅读

**司美格鲁肽（Semaglutide）**：GLP-1 受体激动剂，原用于二型糖尿病，后因其显著减重效果被广泛用于体重管理；需冷链保存，滥用有健康风险。

**GLP-1 类药物（GLP-1 receptor agonists）**：一类通过模拟肠道激素抑制食欲、延缓胃排空的药物，被视为肥胖与糖尿病治疗的突破，也因被社交媒体炒作为"生活方式捷径"而引发监管担忧。

**泰国食品药品监督管理局（Thai FDA）**：泰国负责药品与食品安全的监管机构；二〇二六年九月将减肥针列为"特别管制药品"，要求凭处方购买并推行全程追溯系统。

# A prescription on paper: why Thailand cannot empty its weight-loss-pen black market

## The spectacle: when the weight-loss pen became a social-media staple

On TikTok, a genre of videos has quietly gone viral across Thailand: young women film themselves self-injecting a "weight-loss pen" — semaglutide, the active ingredient in Novo Nordisk's Ozempic and Wegovy — and caption it *pak pakaa*, "stab the pen." For a generation desperate to get thin but short on time and money for the gym, the jab has become a rite of passage. Thai doctors warn that an entire cohort is now self-administering a potent metabolic drug with little understanding of the needle, the dose, or the risk.

This is not merely a health story. It is a small but telling window into how a global pharmaceutical craze, supercharged by social media and anchored in a "thin equals beautiful" aesthetic, collides with the weak enforcement capacity of a middle-income Southeast Asian state. When a drug meant for Type 2 diabetes is repackaged as a lifestyle shortcut, the state is the last to catch up.

## The numbers: a chasm between the clinic and the shadow market

The economics explain the frenzy. A doctor-prescribed semaglutide course in Thailand runs roughly US$240 to US$360 a month. On the black market, unlicensed sellers offer the same jabs for US$80 to US$90 — "three times cheaper than the clinics," as one trader told *This Week in Asia*. The gap is precisely the incentive that birthed a shadow supply chain of smuggled and counterfeit pens.

By regional standards, Thailand is hardly poor; it is the medical-tourism hub of the region, with private hospitals that draw patients from the Gulf and beyond. Yet the same appetite that fuels its legitimate wellness industry spills into an unregulated market where anyone with a TikTok account can become a distributor. The price spread is the market's verdict on the state's inability to make legitimate care affordable and accessible.

## The regulatory fix: can a prescription rule close the gap?

On 15 September the Thai Food and Drug Administration designated weight-loss pens as a "specially controlled drug," meaning a doctor's prescription is now required at pharmacies. The FDA also promised a Track & Trace system to monitor the drug across the supply chain, insisting the aim is to help genuine patients get "higher quality, safer" medicine rather than to deny them access.

But observers should temper their optimism. Prescription rules work only when paired with enforcement at the point of sale and along the border. Thailand already has a "notorious black market in counterfeit drugs"; a new label on a vial changes little if the clinics, online sellers and pharmacies that flog unlicensed pens face no real cost. As one Bangkok pharmacist put it, this is "one of the most ludicrous industries in the entire country, with almost no oversight." A decree without inspectors is a wish.

## The roots: beauty standards, influencers and the medicalised body

To understand why demand is so elastic, look at the culture. Thailand's beauty standard has long prized thinness, and wellness clinics line every main road in Bangkok, Pattaya and Phuket. Into this fertile ground stepped an army of young content creators, amplifying the virtues of the jab over social media while downplaying the part about medical supervision.

What we are witnessing is the medicalisation of a body ideal: a normal desire to be slim is reframed as a condition requiring a weekly injection, and a pharmaceutical corporation's product becomes the solution to a social anxiety. The danger is not the molecule itself — GLP-1 drugs are genuine breakthroughs for obesity and diabetes — but the speed at which a medical tool is detached from medicine and turned into a consumer accessory, sold by influencers rather than prescribed by doctors.

## The risks: fakes, misuse and an unmonitored needle

The human cost is already visible. Doctors report young people injecting themselves on camera without knowing how to use the needle safely, and warn of serious effects from self-administering unknown, unregulated drugs at unsafe doses. Counterfeit pens — often mishandled, exposed to heat, or simply fake — circulate freely. Because semaglutide needs cold-chain storage, a pen bought from an anonymous seller may be inert at best and dangerous at worst.

"Weight-loss pens should only be considered after you have exhausted other methods," cautions Dr Pitsri Khumanuwong, a social-media adviser with 600,000 followers. Her own platform illustrates the paradox: the same social media that educates also seduces, and the line between informed choice and viral peer pressure has all but dissolved.

## The regional lesson: Southeast Asia's pharmaceutical-governance gap

Thailand's dilemma is not unique. Across Southeast Asia, the diffusion of global pharmaceutical capital — from Novo Nordisk to telehealth platforms — consistently outpaces the regulatory state. Indonesia wrestles with fake antibiotics, the Philippines with unregistered supplements, and the Mekong subregion with cross-border pharmaceutical smuggling. Where institutions are thin, the black market fills the vacuum.

The lesson is uncomfortable for those who celebrate "medical tourism" as a regional triumph: a healthcare system that markets itself to the world must also protect its own young people from the underside of that same market. A prescription rule is a start. But until enforcement, education and affordable access move together, the pen will keep finding its way into untrained hands — and the black market will keep smiling.

## Further reading

**Semaglutide**: A GLP-1 receptor agonist originally developed for Type 2 diabetes and later widely used for weight management because of its pronounced slimming effect; it requires cold-chain storage, and misuse carries health risks.

**GLP-1 receptor agonists**: A class of drugs that suppress appetite and slow gastric emptying by mimicking gut hormones. Hailed as a breakthrough for obesity and diabetes, they have also triggered regulatory concern after being hyped on social media as a "lifestyle shortcut."

**Thai FDA (Food and Drug Administration)**: Thailand's agency responsible for drug and food safety. In September 2026 it classified weight-loss pens as a "specially controlled drug," requiring a prescription and introducing a Track & Trace system.
