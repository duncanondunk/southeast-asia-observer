# 算力的代价：泰国给数据中心"立规矩"背后的水与电


## 从"抢投资"到"管投资"：一个委员会的诞生

据中新社报道，泰国（Thailand）政府网站8月14日发布公报，宣布设立"数据中心经营政策委员会"。该委员会由副总理主持，数字经济与社会部长、内政部长、能源部长任副主席，核心职责是向内阁提出数据中心产业的政策、标准与审批方针。表面看，这是一次普通的机构调整；实质上，它意味着泰国对数据中心的姿态，正从"不设上限地招商引资"转向"带着资源约束招商"。

这一转变并非空穴来风。过去两年，在全球云计算、人工智能与数字转型需求的拉动下，数据中心成为金融、医疗、政务、电商、智能制造与网络安全的基础设施。但它也是一头"电老虎"与"水老虎"——大规模机房需要稳定供电与冷却用水，还会产生热、噪声、废水与土地占用问题。当泰国全社会用电需求攀升、发电增量有限、偏远地区供电能力不足时，数据中心的扩张便可能外溢为民生负担。

## 一组数字：三年42个项目、7500亿泰铢的"算力涌入"

泰国投资促进委员会（BOI）的数据显示，2024至2026年间，全国共有42个数据中心投资项目获投资促进，合计信息科技负载约3400兆瓦，投资总额约7500亿泰铢（约合227亿美元，按发布日中间价约1610亿元人民币）。投资来源并不集中于单一国家：泰国本土企业15个、中国企业10个、美国企业5个，其余来自阿联酋、日本、马来西亚（Malaysia）、中国香港、新加坡、印度、澳大利亚与法国。

更猛烈的还在后面。仅2026年第一季度，BOI收到的投资促进申请约1万亿泰铢，其中数据中心及云端服务达48件、总额约8737亿泰铢（约266亿美元），占整体申请金额近九成。投资者主要来自新加坡、日本、英国与马来西亚。东部经济走廊（EEC）已成为区域数据中心布局的重镇。换句话说，泰国正站在一波"算力涌入"的潮头，而潮水的大小，已经超出了此前的监管框架。

## 电从哪来：当AI工厂撞上"棕色电力"

电力是这场争论的核心。数据中心属高耗能产业，若依赖以天然气等化石燃料发电的"棕色电力"，不仅推高碳排放，还可能把额外成本转嫁到其他用电户头上。为此，国家能源政策委员会已批准为数据中心设立单独的电价类别，反映供电与电网扩张的真实成本，并提高用电保证金额度，以筛选项目成熟度。

更具新意的是"银行履约保证"（Bank Guarantee）机制：投资人申请时必须出具银行保函，若获批后未按计划投资，政府将没收保证金并施以处分。此举直指一个隐忧——企业先占位申请用电容量、却迟迟不开工，甚至转售用电权。对早已核准的16个数据中心项目，多数尚未施工、尚未正式分配电力，政府将重新检视其用电条件，使其符合新的能源管理规范。与此同时，泰国正整合全国电网与水利资料，绘制电力与水资源潜力地图，作为划定数据中心适宜区域的基础。

## 水与热：冷却系统的隐性账单

相比电力，水的问题更隐蔽却同样尖锐。数据中心制冷需要大量用水，若管理不当，可能挤占农业与社区用水，尤其在旱季。新框架要求申请投资优惠的业者，从申请阶段即提交完整用水管理计划；当局正在研究大用量用户的水价、低耗水冷却技术的激励，以及旱季用水管理措施。

环境与社区影响也被纳入审查，标准参照环境影响评估（EIA）的精神，重点检视噪声、社区影响及其他环境因素。火灾风险同样被点名——机房与周边社区的安全，不能只靠运气。委员会职权中"维护国家安全数据主权、防范各类污染、推动碳中和目标"的表述，显示监管者试图把数字基础设施重新定义为一种公共品，而非单纯的商业资产。

## 小国的大算盘：在吸引力与承载力之间

把镜头拉远，泰国的选择具有区域样本意义。新加坡早已因土地与能源瓶颈收紧数据中心准入；马来西亚柔佛凭借毗邻新加坡与充裕电力承接外溢；印尼（Indonesia）则在雅加达（Jakarta）及周边加速布局。泰国想同时吃到AI投资与区域数字枢纽红利，又不愿让普通家庭为算力买单——它的答案，是用一个跨部委的国家级委员会，把"招商质量"置于"招商数量"之前。

这背后是一套新的评估逻辑：投资案能否创造高技能就业、培育本地工程师、拉动中小企业进入供应链？能否避免将负担转嫁民生、规划更高的电价与用水保证金？能否推动绿色数据中心与清洁能源应用？当生成式AI把全球对算力的饥渴推到新高，能够把"算力红利"与"资源代价"放在同一张资产负债表上核算的国家，才可能在下一轮竞赛中走得更稳。

## 结语：数字基础设施也是公共资源

泰国设立数据中心经营政策委员会，未必会减缓投资涌入的速度——它想吸引的，本就是高质量、能承担资源成本、与本地产业形成链接的项目。真正值得观察的，是这套"四支柱"（国家利益、能源安全、水资源、环境影响）审查框架能否从纸面落到审批现场。

对东南亚（Southeast Asia）而言，数据中心的兴起既是机遇也是考题。算力可以成为发展的引擎，也可能成为耗电、耗水与碳排放的黑洞。泰国这一步，是把"发展权"与"环境权"放在同一张谈判桌上的尝试；它能否成功，取决于未来几年执行力，而非公告本身。

## 延伸阅读

**数据中心（Data Centre）**：集中存放服务器与网络设备的设施，为云计算、人工智能与互联网服务提供算力与存储；其运行高度依赖稳定供电与冷却系统，是数字经济时代关键的基础设施。

**东部经济走廊（Eastern Economic Corridor, EEC）**：泰国重点发展的沿海经济走廊，涵盖春武里、罗勇与北柳三府，以高端制造、物流与数字产业为导向，是区域数据中心投资的重要承接地。

**棕色电力（Brown Power）**：指来自化石燃料（如天然气、煤炭）的电力；相较于风电、光伏等清洁能源，其碳排放更高，数据中心若主要依赖棕色电力，将加剧温室气体排放。

# The Price of Compute: What Thailand's New Data-Centre Rules Reveal About Water, Power and Sovereignty


## From "courting investment" to "governing investment": the birth of a committee

According to China News Service, Thailand's government website published an announcement on 14 August establishing a "Data Centre Business Policy Committee." Chaired by a deputy prime minister, with the ministers of digital economy, interior and energy as vice-chairs, its core mandate is to propose data-centre policy, standards and approval guidelines to the cabinet. On the surface, an ordinary institutional tweak; in substance, a shift in posture — from courting data-centre investment without ceiling, to courting it with resource constraints attached.

The shift is not unprovoked. Over the past two years, pulled by global demand for cloud, AI and digital transformation, data centres have become infrastructure for finance, healthcare, government, e-commerce, smart manufacturing and cyber-security. But they are also "power hogs" and "water hogs" — large server farms need stable electricity and cooling water, and generate heat, noise, waste water and land-use pressure. When a country's overall electricity demand is rising, generation headroom is limited and remote areas lack supply, data-centre expansion can spill over into a burden on ordinary households.

## A set of numbers: 42 projects, 750 billion baht of "compute inflow" in three years

Data from the Board of Investment (BOI) shows that between 2024 and 2026, 42 data-centre projects received promotion, with a combined IT load of about 3,400 megawatts and total investment of around 750 billion baht (roughly 22.7 billion U.S. dollars). The capital does not concentrate in a single country: 15 Thai firms, 10 Chinese, 5 American, with the rest from the UAE, Japan, Malaysia, Hong Kong, Singapore, India, Australia and France.

The bigger wave is still coming. In the first quarter of 2026 alone, BOI received applications worth about 1 trillion baht, of which data centres and cloud services accounted for 48 projects totalling some 873.7 billion baht (around 26.6 billion U.S. dollars) — nearly 90 per cent of all applications. Investors came mainly from Singapore, Japan, Britain and Malaysia. The Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC) has become a key landing zone for regional data-centre deployment. In short, Thailand stands at the crest of a "compute inflow" whose scale has outgrown the old regulatory frame.

## Where the power comes from: when AI factories meet "brown power"

Electricity is the heart of the dispute. Data centres are energy-intensive; if they run on "brown power" from fossil fuels such as natural gas, they not only raise carbon emissions but can pass extra costs on to other consumers. To address this, the National Energy Policy Council has approved a separate electricity tariff category for data centres that reflects the true cost of supply and grid expansion, alongside higher security deposits on power connections to screen for project readiness.

More novel is the "bank guarantee" mechanism: applicants must post a bank guarantee, and if they win approval but fail to invest as planned, the state will seize the deposit and impose penalties. This targets a quiet fear — firms reserve power capacity early, then delay construction or even resell the right to use it. For the 16 projects already approved, most not yet built and not yet formally allocated power, the government will re-examine their power terms to fit the new energy rules. At the same time, Thailand is consolidating national grid and water data to draw potential maps guiding where data centres may legitimately go.

## Water and heat: the hidden bill of cooling

Compared with power, water is subtler yet no less sharp. Cooling consumes vast amounts of water; done badly, it can crowd out agricultural and community supply, especially in dry season. The new framework requires firms seeking promotion to submit a full water-management plan from the application stage; authorities are studying water tariffs for large users, incentives for low-water cooling, and dry-season management.

Environmental and community impacts are also under review, echoing the spirit of environmental impact assessment (EIA), with attention to noise, community effects and other factors. Fire risk is named too — the safety of server rooms and surrounding neighbourhoods cannot rest on luck. The committee's stated duties — safeguarding national data sovereignty, preventing pollution, advancing carbon-neutrality — signal an attempt to redefine digital infrastructure as a public good, not merely a commercial asset.

## A small country's big calculation: between attractiveness and carrying capacity

Zoom out, and Thailand's choice is a regional specimen. Singapore long ago tightened data-centre access amid land and energy limits; Malaysia's Johor absorbed spillover thanks to proximity to Singapore and ample power; Indonesia is accelerating deployment around Jakarta. Thailand wants both the AI investment and the regional digital-hub dividend, yet refuses to let ordinary families pay for compute — its answer is a cross-ministerial national committee that puts "quality of investment" ahead of "quantity."

Behind this lies a new logic of evaluation: Does a project create high-skill jobs, cultivate local engineers, pull SMEs into the supply chain? Does it avoid shifting the burden onto households, planning higher tariffs and water deposits? Does it advance green data centres and clean energy? As generative AI pushes global hunger for compute to new highs, the countries that can put "compute dividend" and "resource cost" on the same balance sheet are the ones likely to walk the next race more steadily.

## Closing: digital infrastructure is also a public resource

Thailand's committee will not necessarily slow the inflow of investment — what it wants is precisely high-quality projects that can bear resource costs and link to local industry. What is worth watching is whether the "four-pillar" screening (national interest, energy security, water, environment) moves from paper to the approval front line.

For Southeast Asia, the rise of data centres is both opportunity and exam. Compute can be an engine of development, or a black hole of power, water and carbon. Thailand's step is an attempt to put "right to development" and "right to environment" on the same negotiating table; its success will depend on execution in the coming years, not on the announcement itself.

## Further reading

**Data Centre:** A facility that houses servers and networking equipment, supplying compute and storage for cloud, AI and internet services. Its operation depends heavily on stable power and cooling, making it critical — and resource-hungry — infrastructure of the digital economy.

**Eastern Economic Corridor (EEC):** Thailand's flagship coastal economic zone covering Chonburi, Rayong and Chachoengsao, oriented toward advanced manufacturing, logistics and digital industry, and a major landing zone for regional data-centre investment.

**Brown Power:** Electricity generated from fossil fuels such as natural gas or coal. Compared with wind, solar and other clean sources, it carries higher carbon emissions; a data centre reliant on brown power worsens greenhouse-gas output.
