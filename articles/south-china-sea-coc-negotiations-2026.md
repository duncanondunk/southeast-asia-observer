# 准则约束谁：南海行为准则磋商里未被对齐的前提

南海行为准则的磋商已进行了很多年，外界常见的解释是“条款还不够细”“互信还不够足”。这类说法不算错，却停在表层。若把公开可见的磋商机制摊开看，会发现僵持更像是源于一个更靠前的问题：各方对这份准则究竟要约束谁，并没有形成共识。文本越往细处写，这个未被对齐的前提反而越突出。

这不是修辞上的较真。一份准则要落地，先得回答它管哪些行为、管哪些海域、对谁产生义务、与外部力量及既有法律框架是什么关系。这些问题彼此嵌套：适用范围一变，约束对象就变；约束对象一变，“法律约束力”的含义也跟着变。于是同一段措辞，在不同参与方眼里可能指向完全不同的义务。理解这一点，比追踪又一轮磋商进展更能说明为什么慢。

## 单一磋商文本与共识原则：慢是机制设计的结果

磋商以单一磋商文本为底稿推进，各方在同一份文件上提出增删与措辞修改，逐步收敛分歧；东盟式的协商又高度依赖共识原则，即不靠表决、不靠多数，而是力求无人明确反对。这套安排有其正当性：在立场差异明显的议题上，它避免把任何一方推到必须公开对抗的位置，也给后续履约保留了政治空间。

代价是速度。共识原则意味着每一处实质措辞都可能被重新打开，任何一方的保留都足以让条款停留在方括号里；单一文本则让局部让步很难被隔离，一处关于适用范围的改动会牵动约束对象、争端解决与例外条款。慢因此不是效率低下的偶发结果，而是机制为容纳分歧主动付出的成本。把它读成“谈不动”，会漏掉它其实一直在按自己的逻辑运转。

## 法律约束力之争：一份准则应当有多硬

关于准则是否应具备法律约束力，公开讨论里长期存在不同期待。一方倾向把它写成有明确义务、可援引、可追责的条约性文件；另一方更愿意保留政治承诺的弹性，强调自愿、渐进与不干涉具体主张。两种取向的差别，不在措辞软硬，而在对“违约”的想象：如果一份文件没有被设计成可裁判，约束力靠什么兑现。

这又倒推回对象问题。若准则主要约束各方的行为方式——通报、避让、沟通、危机管控——那么政治承诺加上热线与联络机制或许就够用；若它被期待约束具体的权利主张与执法行动，就需要更硬的义务与更清楚的救济。各方对“管什么”的想象不同，对“有多硬”自然难以一致。约束力之争看似是法律技术问题，实则是范围之争的延长线。

## 适用范围与争议海域界定：连管哪里都未对齐

适用范围是最绕不开、也最容易被一句话带过的分歧。准则要覆盖哪些海域，是否及于各方存在不同主张的区域，地理表述采用何种措辞，都会直接改变义务的落点。用一个宽泛的地理概念，看似为达成文本留了余地，却把“到底管到哪里”留给了日后各自解释；一旦写得具体，又几乎必然触及各方不愿在准则里处理的主张。

更微妙的是，范围与主张被刻意分开处理：许多磋商文本倾向于不界定权利归属、只规范行为，以免把准则变成裁决场所。可行为总发生在具体地点，执法总指向具体海域，一份回避地理界定的规则，在真实场景里仍要回答“这里适不适用”。于是适用范围成了一个被推迟的问题，而它一旦被推迟，约束对象也就跟着悬着——这正是核心论点在文本层面的显形。

## 外部力量介入条款：谁算当事方

关于外部力量介入的讨论，把“约束谁”问得更直白。相关议题涉及域外力量在区域内的活动、联合演习、航行与存在，以及是否应在准则里设置事先通报、限制或排除性条款。支持者认为，若规则只约束区域内各方，实际活动却不限于此，文本的覆盖面就不完整；持保留意见的一方则担心，把第三方写进一份由区域磋商产生的文件，会改变参与各方的构成与文件的性质。

争论的实质仍是主体范围：一份行为准则的“行为者”到底如何界定，是按地理、按身份，还是按活动类型。各方对此没有共识时，即便勉强写下一条中性措辞，执行阶段也会因“谁属于这一条”而再生分歧。这类条款之所以常被留到最后一轮、或干脆以模糊表述收场，不是起草者疏忽，而是它把最根本的分歧摆到了台面上：文件为谁而写。

## 渔业、油气执法与既有法律框架：规则之上还有规则

日常摩擦最集中的，往往是渔业与油气相关的执法活动：渔船作业、登临检查、资源勘探与开发安排。这些场景高频、具体、带有强烈的现场性，也最容易在缺乏共同规则时升级。准则被期待提供的，正是通报、克制、联络与危机沟通的最低程序，把偶发对峙拉回可管理的轨道。

难点在于，各方并非在空白地带立约。《联合国海洋法公约》以及既有的法律解释与裁决，已经构成一套被不同方式援引的框架；准则如何处理与这些既有规则的关系——是并行、是补充、还是刻意不触及——本身就是分歧。多方观察认为，正是这种“规则之上还有规则”的状态，让任何一句看似中性的程序条款都可能被读出权利含义。也正因如此，双边层面的通报热线、海上相遇规则与联络机制，在实践中往往比多边文本更早见效：它们先绕开了“约束谁”，只处理“出事时找谁”。

## 双边管控机制的作用，以及准则究竟约束谁

把前面几节合起来，核心论点就清楚了：谈判的僵持并非因为条款不够精细，而是各方对“准则究竟约束谁”没有共识。约束主体、约束范围、约束行为与约束强度，是同一个问题的四个面；只要主体范围悬着，措辞再精细也只是在未定地基上装修。共识原则、单一文本、约束力之争、适用范围与外部力量条款，看似是并列的议题，其实都是这个前提在不同章节里的投影。

这也解释了双边管控机制为何长期扮演补位角色。两个当事方之间谈通报、谈避让、谈联络，可以暂时不回答“文件对谁有效”这个多边难题，先把具体的风险管住；这类安排务实、可操作，却难以替代一份共同规则所提供的可预期性。对观察者而言，衡量磋商进展的尺度，不宜是又一轮文本缩减了多少方括号，而应是各方是否开始就“谁受约束”形成哪怕最低限度的共同表述。在那之前，慢不是停滞，而是分歧尚未被命名时的正常状态。

## 延伸阅读

**行为准则（Code of conduct）**：由相关各方磋商形成、用以规范行为与管控摩擦的规则文件，其约束力与适用范围取决于各方共识。

**单一磋商文本（Single draft negotiating text）**：各方在同一份草案上逐条增删、逐步收敛分歧的谈判底稿，便于集中处理争议措辞。

**共识原则（Consensus principle）**：以无人明确反对为决策基础、不诉诸表决或多数的协商方式，容纳分歧但显著放缓节奏。

# Whom Does a Code Bind: The Unaligned Premise in South China Sea COC Consultations

Consultations on a South China Sea code of conduct have run for many years, and the usual explanation is that the clauses are not yet detailed enough or trust is not yet deep enough. That is not wrong, but it stays on the surface. Lay out the publicly visible consultation machinery and the deadlock looks rooted in an earlier question: the parties have never agreed on whom the code is actually meant to bind. The finer the text gets, the more this unaligned premise stands out.

This is not a quibble over wording. For a code to work, it must first answer which conduct it governs, which waters it covers, whom it places under obligation, and how it relates to outside actors and existing legal frameworks. These questions nest inside one another: change the scope and the bound subject changes; change the subject and the meaning of legal bindingness changes. The same sentence can therefore point to quite different duties for different participants. Grasping that explains the slowness better than tracking another round of talks.

## A single draft text and the consensus principle: slowness by design

Consultations proceed from a single draft negotiating text, with parties proposing additions, deletions and wording changes on one document and gradually narrowing differences. ASEAN-style consultation also leans heavily on the consensus principle: no vote, no majority, but an effort to leave no party openly opposed. The arrangement has its rationale — on an issue of marked divergence it spares any party a public confrontation and preserves political room for later compliance.

The price is speed. Consensus means every substantive phrase can be reopened, and a single reservation is enough to keep a clause in brackets; a single text makes local concessions hard to isolate, so a change to scope pulls at the bound subject, dispute settlement and exceptions at once. Slowness is therefore not an accidental failure of efficiency but the cost the machinery deliberately pays to contain disagreement. Reading it as a breakdown misses that it is working to its own logic.

## The dispute over legal bindingness: how hard should a code be

Public discussion has long held different expectations about whether the code should be legally binding. One side prefers a treaty-like document with clear duties, citable provisions and accountability; another prefers the flexibility of a political commitment, stressing voluntarism, gradualism and non-interference with specific claims. The difference is not about hard or soft wording but about what a breach would even mean: if a document is not designed to be adjudicated, what delivers its binding force?

That leads back to the question of subjects. If the code mainly governs how parties behave — notification, avoidance, communication, crisis management — a political commitment plus hotlines and liaison may suffice. If it is expected to govern specific claims and enforcement actions, harder duties and clearer remedies are needed. Different imaginations of what is governed make agreement on how hard it should be elusive. The bindingness dispute looks like a legal-technical question but is an extension of the dispute over scope.

## Scope and the definition of disputed waters: not even where is aligned

Scope is the most unavoidable and most easily glossed-over divergence. Which waters the code covers, whether it reaches areas subject to differing claims, and what geographical wording is used all shift where obligations land. A broad geographical phrase leaves room to reach a text but leaves how far it actually reaches to later interpretation; a specific one almost inevitably touches claims parties prefer not to handle inside a code.

More subtly, scope and claims are deliberately separated: many drafts prefer not to define rights but only to regulate conduct, so the code does not become a venue for adjudication. Yet conduct happens somewhere and enforcement points at particular waters, so a rule that avoids geographical definition still has to answer whether it applies here in real situations. Scope becomes a postponed question, and with it the bound subject stays suspended — which is exactly where the central argument surfaces at the level of text.

## Clauses on outside involvement: who counts as a party

Discussion of outside involvement puts whom the code binds most bluntly. The issues concern the activities of extra-regional actors, joint exercises, navigation and presence, and whether the code should set prior-notification, restrictive or exclusionary clauses. Supporters argue that a rule binding only regional parties leaves its coverage incomplete when actual activity is not so limited; those with reservations worry that writing third parties into a document born of regional consultation changes both its participants and its nature.

The substance is still the range of subjects: how are the actors of a code of conduct defined — by geography, by identity, or by type of activity. Without agreement, even a neutral phrase will generate fresh disagreement at the enforcement stage over who falls under it. Such clauses are often left to the final round or settled in vague wording not through drafting negligence but because they place the most fundamental divergence on the table: for whom is this document written?

## Fisheries and energy enforcement and existing legal frameworks: rules upon rules

Day-to-day friction concentrates in enforcement around fisheries and energy: fishing operations, boarding and inspection, resource surveys and development arrangements. These scenes are frequent, concrete and highly charged in the moment, and the most likely to escalate without common rules. What a code is expected to provide is precisely a minimum procedure of notification, restraint, liaison and crisis communication, pulling occasional stand-offs back onto a manageable track.

The difficulty is that parties are not contracting on a blank slate. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, along with existing legal interpretations and rulings, already forms a framework invoked in different ways; how the code relates to it — parallel, supplementary, or deliberately silent — is itself a divergence. Many observers note that this condition of rules upon rules lets even a neutral procedural clause be read for its implications for rights. That is also why bilateral notification hotlines, encounter rules and liaison mechanisms often prove effective earlier than multilateral text: they bypass whom it binds and handle only whom to call when something happens.

## The role of bilateral management, and whom the code ultimately binds

Put the sections together and the argument is clear: the deadlock is not because clauses are insufficiently refined but because the parties have no shared view of whom the code binds. The bound subject, scope, conduct and intensity are four faces of one question; while the subject remains suspended, finer wording only decorates an unsettled foundation. The consensus principle, the single text, the bindingness dispute, scope and clauses on outside actors look like parallel agenda items but are projections of this one premise across different chapters.

That also explains why bilateral management mechanisms have long played a supporting role. Two parties discussing notification, avoidance and liaison can temporarily set aside the multilateral puzzle of to whom the document applies and manage concrete risks first. Such arrangements are practical and workable but cannot replace the predictability a common rule provides. For an observer, progress should be measured not by how many brackets another round removes, but by whether parties begin to form even a minimal shared formulation of who is bound. Until then, slowness is not stagnation but the normal state of a divergence not yet named.

## Further reading

**Code of conduct**: A rules document formed through consultation among the parties concerned to regulate conduct and manage friction, whose binding force and scope depend on their consensus.

**Single draft negotiating text**: A negotiating base on which parties add, delete and refine provisions clause by clause so that disputed wording can be handled in one place.

**Consensus principle**: A method of decision resting on no party expressly objecting, rather than voting or majority rule, which accommodates divergence but markedly slows the pace.
