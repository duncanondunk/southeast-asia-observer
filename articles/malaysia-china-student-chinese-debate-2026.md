# 第四届马中大学生汉语辩论赛落幕：语言如何成为青年外交的桥梁

10月18日，第四届马中大学生汉语辩论赛在马来西亚（Malaysia）世纪大学落下帷幕。赛事由世纪大学孔子学院主办，为中马两国高校学子搭建起语言交流与思想碰撞的线下平台。中国驻马来西亚大使馆参赞赵长涛、马来西亚海南总商会总会长林秋雅、世纪大学校长 Mariani 教授、孔子学院马方院长许玉玲与中方院长马海燕教授，以及中马多所高校代表与参赛师生出席闭幕式。

一场看似普通的校园辩论赛，为何值得专门书写？答案藏在「马中」二字背后的民间温度里。

## 一场辩论赛，何以被称为「青年外交的桥梁」

赵长涛在致辞中说得很直白：汉语辩论赛「不仅是语言竞技的舞台，更是增进两国青年理解与友谊的重要桥梁」。这句话常被当作外交辞令一笔带过，但放在马来西亚这个语境里，分量并不轻。

马来西亚是东盟（ASEAN）中与华语联系最深的成员国之一。这里不仅有庞大的华文教育体系——从华小、独中到大专院校的中文课程——更有延续百年的华人社群记忆。当马来西亚高校学子用中文就一个公共议题展开交锋，语言早已不只是工具，而是一套共享的意义系统：他们争的是论点，靠的却是同一种文化语法。

从这个意义上说，辩论赛确实在扮演桥梁。它把「国家间的青年交流」这个宏大命题，落到了一群具体的年轻人、一个具体的赛场之上。

## 全面回归线下：被疫情打断的面对面交流重启

世纪大学孔子学院中方院长马海燕在回顾本届赛事时，特别提到「全面回归线下带来的突破」。这八个字，是疫情三年跨境流动受限之后，青年交流恢复正常节奏的一个缩影。

线上办赛可以保住形式，却保不住气场。辩论最依赖的，是临场的语感、对手的微表情、观众席的哄笑与掌声——这些当面才能生成的东西，是屏幕无法替代的。当赛事重新回到线下，被压缩的不只是物理距离，还有因长期隔绝而悄然生出的陌生感。

值得注意的是，这已是第四届赛事。一届届办下来，说明它不是临时起意的政治秀，而是扎进两校合作肌理里的长期机制。机制化的交流，比一次性的高光时刻更经得起时间。

## 孔子学院在马来西亚的「正常态」

本次赛事的主办方是世纪大学孔子学院。说起孔子学院，西方舆论场里常伴随质疑甚至关停的声音；但在马来西亚，它呈现出另一种「正常态」。

原因不难理解。马来西亚本就拥有成熟的华文教育生态，孔子学院在这里更像是「锦上添花」而非「平地起高楼」——它对接的是既有的华语需求和华人文化土壤，而不是从零植入。正因如此，它能以相对低调、务实的姿态，扮演中马青年之间的协作者，而非外界想象中的敏感符号。

这也提醒我们：评估孔子学院的海外处境，不能一概而论。在马来西亚这种华语根基深厚、对华关系务实的国家，它反而能找到最自然的功能定位。

## 华人社区与华语：天然的语言桥梁

闭幕式上，马来西亚海南总商会总会长林秋雅的身影，点出了一个常被忽略的角色：华人社团。

马来西亚华人社团——从商会到乡会、从校友会到宗亲会——长期是华语文化传承与中马民间往来的毛细血管。它们既扎根当地、服务华人社区，又天然连接着中国语境。辩论赛能得到商会会长站台，说明这类青年语言活动，从来不只是学校的内部事务，而是整个华人离散网络愿意托举的公共品。

这也解释了为什么马来西亚会成为「马中」语言交流的高地：华人社区提供了现成的语言土壤与组织网络，国家间的青年项目只需顺势接入。

## 语言软实力之外，更需双向的倾听

当然，把辩论赛框定为「软实力」的成功案例，也要警惕单向叙事。语言交流的价值，不在于谁说服了谁，而在于双方是否学会了用对方的逻辑去理解问题。

马来西亚青年用中文辩论，本身就意味着他们掌握了一种进入华人世界主流话语的能力；而中方参与者若能借此听见马来西亚青年对区域议题的真实关切——无论是对自身多元族裔身份的体认，还是对区域秩序的看法——这种「被听见」，或许比奖杯更有长远意义。

语言从来不是单向的输出管道。当两国青年在同一规则下平等交锋，真正沉淀下来的，是彼此都把对方当作值得认真对待的对话者的习惯。这，才是青年外交最朴素的底色。

## 延伸阅读

- **汉语（Mandarin）**：现代标准汉语，是马来西亚华文教育的教学语言，也是本次马中大学生辩论赛的竞赛语言。
- **孔子学院（Confucius Institute）**：由中外高校合作设立的语言文化推广机构；在马来西亚依托既有华文教育生态运作，与西方语境下的争议形象有所不同。
- **马来西亚华文教育（Chinese education in Malaysia）**：从华文小学、独立中学到大专院校的中文教育体系，是东南亚（Southeast Asia）保存最完整的华语教育链条之一。

# Malaysia–China Student Chinese Debate Closes: How Language Becomes Youth Diplomacy

On 18 October, the fourth Malaysia–China University Students' Chinese Debate Competition came to a close at SEGi University in Malaysia. Organised by the SEGi University Confucius Institute, the contest gave students from universities in both countries a physical platform for linguistic exchange and the clash of ideas. Attending the closing ceremony were Zhao Changtao, counsellor at the Chinese Embassy in Malaysia; Lim Chew Yap, president of the Malaysia Hainan General Chamber of Commerce; Professor Mariani, vice-chancellor of SEGi University; Goh Yoke Ling, Malaysian co-director of the Confucius Institute; Professor Ma Haiyan, its Chinese co-director; and representatives from multiple Malaysian and Chinese universities together with the participating students and teachers.

Why bother writing about what looks, on the surface, like an ordinary campus debate? The answer lies in the quiet, grass-roots warmth behind the word "Malaysia–China."

## A debate contest as a "bridge of youth diplomacy"

Zhao Changtao put it plainly in his remarks: the Chinese-language debate is "not merely a stage for linguistic competition, but an important bridge for deepening understanding and friendship between the youth of the two countries." Such lines are easy to dismiss as diplomatic boilerplate, yet in the Malaysian context they carry real weight.

Malaysia is among the ASEAN members with the deepest ties to the Chinese language. It hosts a vast Chinese-medium education system — from vernacular primary schools and independent secondary schools to tertiary Chinese programmes — and a century-old memory of ethnic-Chinese community life. When Malaysian university students argue a public question in Mandarin, language is no longer merely a tool but a shared system of meaning: what they contest are arguments, but what they rely on is the same cultural grammar.

In that sense the debate does function as a bridge. It grounds the grand abstraction of "youth exchange between nations" in a concrete group of young people and a concrete arena.

## Back fully offline: face-to-face exchange resumes after the pandemic

In reviewing the edition, Professor Ma Haiyan, the Chinese co-director of the SEGi Confucius Institute, singled out "the breakthrough brought by the full return to offline." Those words are a microcosm of how youth exchange has regained its normal rhythm after three years of restricted cross-border movement during the pandemic.

Online editions could preserve the form but not the atmosphere. Debate depends most on live linguistic feel, the opponent's micro-expressions, and the audience's laughter and applause — things generated only in person, which a screen cannot substitute. As the contest returned offline, what shrank was not only physical distance but also the unfamiliarity that long isolation had quietly bred.

Notably, this was already the fourth edition. Holding it year after year shows it is no impromptu political pageant but a long-term mechanism embedded in the cooperating universities' fabric. Institutionalised exchange outlasts one-off moments of high visibility.

## The Confucius Institute's "normal state" in Malaysia

The organiser, the SEGi University Confucius Institute, deserves a closer look. In Western discourse, Confucius Institutes are often met with suspicion and even closures; in Malaysia they present a different "normal state."

The reason is not hard to see. Malaysia already possesses a mature Chinese-medium education ecosystem, so the institute is more an addition than a creation from scratch — it plugs into existing Mandarin demand and a Chinese-cultural soil rather than implanting from zero. Precisely for that reason it can act, in a relatively low-key and pragmatic way, as a collaborator between Malaysian and Chinese youth rather than the sensitive symbol imagined elsewhere.

This also reminds us not to generalise about Confucius Institutes overseas. In a country like Malaysia, with deep Mandarin roots and pragmatic China relations, the institute can find its most natural functional niche.

## The ethnic-Chinese community and Mandarin: a natural linguistic bridge

At the closing ceremony, the presence of Lim Chew Yap, president of the Malaysia Hainan General Chamber of Commerce, points to a role often overlooked: the Chinese associations.

Malaysian Chinese associations — chambers of commerce, hometown societies, alumni and clan associations — have long been the capillaries of Chinese-cultural preservation and Malaysia–China grass-roots ties. Rooted locally and serving the Chinese community, they are also naturally connected to the China context. That a chamber president would show up for a youth language contest suggests such activities are never merely internal school affairs but public goods the wider diaspora network is willing to carry.

This also explains why Malaysia became a high ground for Malaysia–China language exchange: the Chinese community supplies ready-made linguistic soil and organisational networks, and state-to-state youth projects merely plug in.

## Beyond language soft power, the need for two-way listening

Of course, framing the debate as a success story of "soft power" also risks a one-directional narrative. The value of linguistic exchange lies not in who persuades whom, but in whether both sides learn to understand problems through the other's logic.

That Malaysian youth debate in Chinese already means they command a capacity to enter the mainstream Chinese-language discourse; and if Chinese participants can, through this, hear Malaysian youth's genuine concerns about regional issues — whether their sense of a plural ethnic identity or their views of the regional order — this being-heard may matter more in the long run than any trophy.

Language was never a one-way output pipe. When the youth of two countries clash as equals under the same rules, what truly settles is the habit of treating each other as interlocutors worthy of serious attention. That, plainly, is the most basic colour of youth diplomacy.

- **Mandarin (Hanyu)**: Modern Standard Chinese, the language of instruction in Malaysia's Chinese-medium education and the competitive language of this Malaysia–China student debate.
- **Confucius Institute**: A language-and-culture promotion body jointly run by Chinese and foreign universities; in Malaysia it operates atop an existing Chinese-education ecosystem, differing from its contested image in the West.
- **Chinese education in Malaysia**: A Chinese-medium education chain — from vernacular primary schools and independent secondary schools to tertiary programmes — among the most complete in Southeast Asia.
