# 举债近700亿新元救经济：小马科斯的政治赌注与财政红线

受建筑业低迷与内需疲软拖累，菲律宾（Philippines）经济今年第二季同比仅增长2.3%，创下2021年以来最低增速；上半年增长率2.6%，也明显低于全年3.5%至4.5%的目标区间。面对失速压力，马尼拉（Manila）开出的药方是"借钱刺激"。8月11日，菲律宾预算与管理部公布数据：明年计划举债3.3万亿比索（约690亿新元，约合3720亿元人民币），比今年修订后的2.73万亿比索借款计划高出五分之一。同一天，总统小马可斯（Ferdinand Marcos Jr.）要求国会批准明年7.2万亿比索（约1500亿新元，约合8100亿元人民币）的国家预算，较上年增长6%。这意味着，借款将占预算的46%。

## 2.3%的警钟：增长失速的真相

数字背后的故事并不复杂。建筑业是菲律宾过去数年增长的重要引擎，如今陷入低迷；家庭消费这一传统支柱也因通胀与就业不确定而转弱。更棘手的是外部冲击的叠加——中东（the Middle East）冲突推高能源与进口成本，国内又接连爆出腐败丑闻，削弱公共投资效率与市场信心。路透社在报道中直言，这是2021年以来最慢的季度表现。

对一个倚重海外劳工汇款与服务业的经济体而言，增速跌至2.3%不仅是统计上的低点，更是就业与民生的现实压力。上半年2.6%的增速距目标尚有缺口，要在下半年显著反弹，单靠既有节奏已显不够，这正是政府转向扩张性财政的直接动因。

## 700亿新元的赌注：钱从哪来、去往何处

拟议的3.3万亿比索借款，主要通过国内融资筹集，显示马尼拉仍优先倚重本币市场、避免过度依赖外债。借钱的目的，按预算与管理部的说法，是对基础设施、教育、卫生、粮食安全与社会保障的战略投资，以支撑"有韧性且具财政责任的经济增长"。

配套措施也同步铺开：政府拟提高对汽水、电子烟与酒类的征税，以填补刺激计划的成本；同时为家庭提供税务减免，并以补贴对冲能源价格飙升。小马可斯在表态中强调，面对地缘政治紧张、持续通胀与能源价格波动，"仍坚定不移地追求具有韧性、同时保持财政责任的经济增长"。这话术本身，已透露出刺激与纪律之间的紧张。

## 债务的底色：66%与还债的近半

真正需要正视的，是债务的存量结构。截至6月，菲律宾未偿债务达19.07万亿比索（约3970亿新元，约合2.14万亿元人民币），约占国内生产总值（GDP）的66%。而明年的新增借款中，近一半、约1.6万亿比索（约333亿新元，约合1800亿元人民币）将直接用于偿还债务。换言之，借来的钱里有相当部分并非投入新增长，而是为旧债"续命"。

拟议支出计划占GDP的21.7%。中国银行首席经济师维拉斯奎兹（Velasquez）的评论较为克制：尽管推进步伐放缓，菲律宾的财政整顿计划依然有效；要让债务占GDP比率持续下行，必须结合更强劲的增长、持续的增收改革与审慎的支出管理。这句话的潜台词是——借钱能买时间，却买不来结构性的好转。

## 财政责任的两难：刺激与红线

摆在马尼拉面前的，是一道典型的发展中国家难题：不刺激，增速难回正轨、民生压力陡增；强刺激，则债务率向更高平台滑去，未来政策空间被透支。小马可斯把赌注压在"改革红利"上——以基建与人力资本的长期投入换可持续增长，用新税与补贴平衡短期的分配阵痛。

但风险在于节奏。若全球不确定性延续、外资与汇款波动，借来的资金能否转化为真实生产率，仍存在疑问。66%的债务率虽未到危机阈值，却已压缩了政策缓冲。对东南亚（Southeast Asia）观察者而言，菲律宾的案例提示了一个更普遍的命题：当增长引擎熄火，财政扩张常是唯一可选项，而真正的考验，从来不在借钱的那一刻，而在偿债与增长能否形成正向循环。

# Borrowing S$69 Billion to Rescue the Economy

Weighed down by a sluggish construction sector and weak domestic demand, the Philippine economy grew just 2.3% year on year in the second quarter — its slowest pace since 2021. The first-half expansion of 2.6% also fell well short of the full-year target band of 3.5% to 4.5%. Manila's prescribed remedy is a borrowing spree. On 11 August, the Department of Budget and Management announced that next year's planned borrowings would reach 3.3 trillion pesos (about S$69 billion, ~US$53 billion), a fifth higher than this year's revised plan of 2.73 trillion pesos. The same day, President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. asked Congress to approve a 2027 national budget of 7.2 trillion pesos (about S$150 billion, ~US$116 billion), up 6% from this year — meaning new debt would cover 46% of the budget.

## The 2.3% warning

The story behind the number is straightforward. Construction, a key growth engine in recent years, has slumped; household consumption, the traditional pillar, weakened under inflation and job uncertainty. Worse, external shocks piled up — the Middle East conflict pushed up energy and import costs, while domestic corruption scandals eroded public-investment efficiency and market confidence. Reuters plainly called it the slowest quarter since 2021.

For an economy leaning on overseas remittances and services, a drop to 2.3% is not merely a statistical low but a real pressure on jobs and livelihoods. With the first half at 2.6%, a sharp second-half rebound under the current rhythm looks unlikely — which is precisely why the government turned to expansionary fiscal policy.

## A S$69 billion bet

The proposed 3.3 trillion pesos would be raised mainly through domestic financing, signalling Manila's preference for local-currency markets over external debt. The purpose, per the budget department, is strategic investment in infrastructure, education, health, food security and social protection to support "resilient and fiscally responsible growth."

Companion measures are rolling out: higher taxes on soda, vaping products and alcohol to fund the stimulus, alongside household tax relief and subsidies to offset surging energy prices. Marcos stressed that amid geopolitical tension, persistent inflation and energy volatility, the government remains "steadfast in pursuing resilient, fiscally responsible economic growth." The phrasing itself betrays the tension between stimulus and discipline.

## The debt backdrop

What demands attention is the stock of debt. As of June, outstanding obligations reached 19.07 trillion pesos (about S$397 billion, ~US$306 billion), roughly 66% of GDP. And of next year's new borrowings, nearly half — about 1.6 trillion pesos (about S$33 billion, ~US$26 billion) — is earmarked simply to repay existing debt. In other words, a sizable share of the new money services old obligations rather than building new growth.

The proposed spending plan equals 21.7% of GDP. Velasquez, chief economist at Bank of China, was measured: though the pace has slowed, the fiscal-consolidation plan remains effective, and bringing the debt-to-GDP ratio down sustainably requires stronger growth, continued revenue reforms and prudent spending. The subtext is clear — borrowing buys time, not structural recovery.

## The fiscal dilemma

Manila faces a classic developing-economy predicament: without stimulus, growth won't recover and livelihood pressures mount; with aggressive stimulus, the debt ratio climbs to a higher plateau and future policy room is pre-empted. Marcos is betting on "reform dividends" — long-term gains from infrastructure and human capital, buffered by new taxes and subsidies against short-term distributional pain.

The risk lies in timing. If global uncertainty persists and remittances or foreign capital wobble, whether borrowed funds translate into real productivity remains an open question. A 66% debt ratio is not yet at a crisis threshold, but it has already compressed the policy buffer. For observers of Southeast Asia, the Philippine case poses a broader proposition: when the growth engine stalls, fiscal expansion is often the only option — and the real test never arrives at the moment of borrowing, but in whether debt service and growth can form a virtuous cycle.
