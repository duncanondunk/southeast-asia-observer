# 东南亚成中国品牌的奢侈品试验场：出海拐点，还是溢价幻觉？

《日经亚洲》（Nikkei Asia）8月12日一篇报道把东南亚称作中国品牌的"奢侈品试验场"。它描摹的画面很生动：曼谷一家购物中心里，一名年轻的印度顾客正在挑选中国品牌"山下有松"的手袋，而他脚上那双亮黄色麂皮球鞋，来自另一个中国初创品牌"PANE"。画面干净，甚至有些诱人。但这究竟是真正的突破，还是中国沿价值链向上攀爬的老故事又翻出一页？本文想把这一趋势本身讲清楚。

## 前提：一个充当试验场的区域

核心说法其实很直白。中国消费品牌——长期习惯给西方大牌做代工——如今想自己掌握设计、品牌与利润。东南亚年轻、追潮流、富裕阶层快速扩张，正好提供了一个低风险的"排练场"，让品牌先在亚洲把全球话术练熟，再去东京、巴黎或纽约。

这个逻辑在它的范围内站得住。当"试验场"用在一个本土市场趋于饱和、国际声誉又落后于制造能力的品牌身上，再合适不过。地理相近、文化相通，降低了试错成本。但把它叫作"奢侈品"试验场，未免高估了这块蛋糕。报道点名的那些名字——香氛、彩妆、小皮具——卖的是可负担价格上的"向往感"，并非爱马仕或香奈儿那种高利润的光环。

## 为何是东南亚，为何是此刻

三股力量在此交汇。其一，区域消费群体年轻、生于数字时代，TikTok（短视频平台）和虾皮购物（电商平台）把产品发布到走红之间的距离压到了极短。其二，地理与文化的亲近，让中国品牌可以借用共通审美——玉色系彩妆、草本香氛——这些符号在亚洲之间流动，比跨越大西洋容易得多。其三，也最常被轻描淡写的一股，是关税逻辑。当华盛顿的关税把部分中国出口商推向把东南亚当作出海第一站时，同一股贸易逆风，悄悄为"建品牌"这种软实力动作提供了补贴。

说到这里，评论该停一停。关税故事是真的，但也容易被夸大。东南亚对中国消费品牌而言，首要不是关税避风港，而是本身就成立的需求中心。把两件事混为一谈，只是给叙事贴金，并没有解释清楚。

## 先行者：手袋、球鞋、彩妆与香氛

《日经亚洲》点出了先锋阵容："山下有松"（手袋）、"PANE"（鞋履）、"花西子"（彩妆）、"观夏"（香氛），以及计划到2030年把海外门店从55家增至100家的香港珠宝商周大福。花西子称2024至2025年间在越南的销售额增长了20倍；观夏的香港门店约六到七成顾客来自城外，包括新加坡和马来西亚。

这些数字并不小。从很小的基数上翻20倍，基数仍然小——但它证明了渠道跑得通。更有意思的信号是渠道本身：中国品牌现在把TikTok和虾皮当作门面，之后才开实体旗舰店。顺序与旧出口模式正好相反，过去是货先堆进外国仓库，名字还没人知道。

## 关税顺风与文化红利

文化红利值得更认真地看。报道提到，中国珠宝商"走向全球"时往往优先亚洲市场，因为文化参照点相通。这不只是图方便，而是一道战略对冲。在本土靠传承叙事、匠艺故事、传统美学建起声望，再把这份声望出口到邻近亚洲，是比在香榭丽舍大道上硬刚路威酩轩更便宜的高端定位路线。

但红利不是护城河。文化亲近帮中国品牌进门，却不保证它们留下。东南亚消费者善变，且对既有品牌忠诚。文化这张牌买来的是注意力，不是忠诚。

## 真正的难题：中国造能否撑起溢价

这是乐观报道往往绕开的问题。奢侈品的定价建立在"产地神话"上，而非工厂质量。几十年来，"中国制造"意味着代工，不意味着原创。中国品牌如今想在设计与生产之间划一道线——等于说"对，它在这里造，但它也在这里构思"。当全球默认仍把中国制造读作代工（OEM）时，这是一桩难卖的买卖。

信号喜忧参半。新加坡的独立腕表零售商已有三分之一货品来自中国品牌，中国酒庄与腕表匠也凭大胆设计赢得小众好评。但小众好评不等于大众溢价。除非某个中国品牌能像法国或意大利老牌那样让人排队等货，否则"奢侈品试验场"这个标签，仍是向往，而非既成事实。

## 对东南亚意味着什么

对区域而言，这股趋势大体是受欢迎的。它丰富了零售供给，加深了围绕社交电商的创作者经济，也给本地消费者提供了西方或日本既有品牌之外的选项。它也在悄悄重新平衡中国与东南亚的关系：几十年来流向是中国工厂供货西方货架；如今是中国品牌在亚洲平台上、以亚洲审美直接向亚洲消费者售卖。

风险在于依赖。如果东南亚主要沦为中国全球品牌的"排练舞台"，它自己的零售生态或许会变成外部品牌资产的输送带，而非本地品牌的建设者。区域应当欢迎这些品牌——同时也要看清楚，它的商场会不会变成别人登顶的展厅。

## 延伸阅读

**代工（OEM）**：原始设备制造，指企业按外部品牌方设计与规格生产、贴上对方商标销售的模式；中国消费品牌的"去代工化"正是要摆脱这一身份。

**文化红利（Cultural Dividend）**：地缘与文化亲近带来的品牌接受度优势；在亚洲市场，共享审美与符号降低中国品牌的进入成本，却难以转化为长期忠诚。

**社交电商（Social Commerce）**：以短视频与直播平台为门面、先聚粉再开实体店的新零售路径；TikTok 与虾皮是东南亚中国品牌出海的主要渠道。

# Southeast Asia as China's Luxury Lab: Why the Region Became the Testing Ground for Chinese Brands Going Global

A *Nikkei Asia* dispatch published on 12 August framed Southeast Asia as the "luxury test lab" for Chinese brands. The image it paints is vivid: inside a Bangkok shopping centre, a young Indian shopper picks out a handbag from Shan Xia You Song, a Chinese label, while his bright-yellow suede sneakers come from PANE, another Chinese startup. The scene is tidy, almost seductive. But is it evidence of a genuine breakthrough, or merely the latest chapter in China's long effort to climb the value chain? This commentary reads the trend on its own terms.

## The premise: a region as a proving ground

The core claim is straightforward. Chinese consumer brands — long comfortable as contract manufacturers for Western houses — now want to own design, brand and margin. Southeast Asia, with its young, trend-following consumers and fast-growing affluent class, offers a low-risk arena to rehearse a global pitch before taking it to Tokyo, Paris or New York.

That logic is sound as far as it goes. A "test lab" is exactly what a brand needs when its domestic market saturates and its international reputation still lags its manufacturing skill. The region's proximity and cultural overlap lower the cost of trial and error. Yet calling it a "luxury" lab overstates the prize. Most of the names cited — fragrance, cosmetics, small leather goods — sell aspiration at accessible prices, not the high-margin veil of a Hermès or a Chanel.

## Why Southeast Asia, and why now

Three forces converge. First, the regional consumer base is young and digitally native; TikTok and Shopee have compressed the distance between a product launch and a viral moment. Second, geographic and cultural closeness lets Chinese brands lean on shared aesthetics — jade-toned cosmetics, herbal fragrances — that travel more easily across Asia than across the Atlantic. Third, and least discussed, is the tariff equation. As Washington's tariffs push some Chinese exporters to treat Southeast Asia as a first overseas stop, the same trade friction quietly subsidises the soft-power move of brand-building.

Here the commentary should pause. The tariff story is real but easily exaggerated. Southeast Asia is not primarily a tariff haven for consumer brands; it is a demand centre in its own right. Confusing the two flatters the narrative without explaining it.

## The pioneers: handbags, sneakers, makeup, fragrance

The *Nikkei* piece names the vanguard: Shan Xia You Song (handbags), PANE (footwear), Florasis (makeup), and To Summer (fragrance), alongside Chow Tai Fook, the Hong Kong jeweller planning to lift overseas stores from 55 to 100 by 2030. Florasis reports a twenty-fold sales jump in Vietnam between 2024 and 2025, and To Summer's Hong Kong store draws 60–70% of its customers from outside the city, including Singapore and Malaysia.

These are not trivial numbers. A twenty-fold rise from a small base is still a small base — but it proves the channel works. The more interesting signal is the channel itself: Chinese brands now treat TikTok and Shopee as the shopfront, and only later open a physical flagship. The sequence is the opposite of the old export model, where goods arrived in foreign warehouses before anyone knew the name.

## The tariff tailwind and the culture dividend

The cultural dividend deserves a harder look. When Chinese jewellers "go global," the report notes, they often prioritise Asian markets because of shared cultural reference points. This is not merely convenience; it is a strategic hedge. Building prestige at home — through heritage storytelling, craft narratives, traditional aesthetics — and exporting that prestige to nearby Asia is a cheaper route to premium positioning than fighting LVMH on the Champs-Élysées.

But a dividend is not a moat. Cultural closeness helps Chinese brands enter; it does not guarantee they stay. Southeast Asian consumers are fickle and brand-loyal to incumbents. The culture card buys attention, not loyalty.

## The hard question: can 'made in China' carry a premium?

This is the question the optimistic coverage tends to dodge. Luxury pricing rests on origin myth, not factory quality. For decades "made in China" signalled contract manufacturing, not authorship. Chinese brands are now trying to separate design from production in the consumer's mind — to say, in effect, "yes it is made here, but it is conceived here too." That is a hard sell when the global default still reads Chinese manufacturing as OEM.

The signs are mixed. Watch retailers in Singapore already stock one-third Chinese-brand products, and Chinese wineries and watchmakers win niche praise for bold design. Yet niche praise is not mass premium. Until a Chinese label commands a waitlist the way a French or Italian house does, the "luxury lab" label remains aspirational rather than earned.

## What it means for Southeast Asia

For the region, the trend is mostly welcome. It diversifies retail offer, deepens the creator economy around social commerce, and gives local consumers alternatives to Western or Japanese incumbents. It also quietly rebalances the China–Southeast Asia relationship: for decades the flow was Chinese factories supplying Western shelves; now Chinese brands sell directly to Asian consumers, on Asian platforms, with Asian aesthetics.

The risk is dependency. If Southeast Asia becomes chiefly a rehearsal stage for Chinese global brands, its own retail ecosystems may serve as feeders to external brand equity rather than builders of local ones. The region should welcome the brands — and watch whether its malls become showrooms for someone else's ascent.

## Further reading

**OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturing)**: the model in which a firm produces to a foreign brand's design and spec, then ships under that brand's label; Chinese consumer brands' "de-OEM" push is precisely an attempt to shed this identity.

**Cultural dividend**: the brand-acceptance advantage that geographic and cultural proximity confers; across Asia, shared aesthetics lower Chinese brands' entry cost, but rarely convert into lasting loyalty.

**Social commerce**: a new-retail path that treats short-video and live-stream platforms as the shopfront — building a fan base before opening a physical store; TikTok and Shopee are the main channels for Chinese brands in Southeast Asia.
