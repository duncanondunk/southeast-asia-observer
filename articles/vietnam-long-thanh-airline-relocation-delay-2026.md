# 隆城机场开门，航司却要缓一缓：越南超级枢纽的最后一公里

据彭博社报道，越南官方媒体援引航空管理部门的一项调查：30多家国际航空公司提出，希望把业务从胡志明市新山一机场（Tan Son Nhat）迁往隆城机场（Long Thanh Airport）的时间往后推。其中24家希望推迟到2027年冬航季，另有10家干脆提出2028年夏航季甚至更晚。越南政府原本指望这座投资336.63万亿越南盾（约170亿新元，约合950亿元人民币）的超级工程在12月投入商业运营，为长期拥堵的新山一机场分流。航司却在最后关头集体“请假”。这到底是航空公司不配合，还是工程进度表本身出了问题？答案藏在“机场建成”与“枢纽可用”之间那道被低估的鸿沟里。

## 30多家航司的集体请假

先把数字说清楚。据越南建设部提交政府的报告，受访的国际航司全部支持最终迁往隆城，认为新机场设施更现代、容量足以承接未来增长；但24家希望从2027年冬航季开始，10家希望到2028年夏航季甚至更晚。为什么要等？航司给出的理由并不矫情：调整航线网络、重新调配飞机与机组、搭建地面服务体系、升级售票系统、设立驻场代表处，这些环节通常需要6到12个月。外国航空监管机构在批准运营前，还可能要实地考察并认证机场安全条件。

换句话说，换机场不是把起飞地点从甲改成乙，而是重排一整套商业合约与操作流程。据联合早报报道，国际货运运营商的准备工作更长，可能需要12个月，涉及货物装卸、清关以及交通衔接的技术评估。航司还点名要求可靠的地面交通、四星级以上的中转酒店和高质量医疗服务。读到这里就明白：这不是航司在讨价还价，而是在告诉项目方——硬件完工只是入场券，运营生态才是正片。

## 建好不等于开得起来

截至2026年10月下旬，隆城机场约86%的关键设施已经建成。数字听着漂亮，但“86%”衡量的是工程，不是运营。机场的运营准备与机场转移（Operational Readiness and Airport Transfer, ORAT）计划包含55个场景，从9月到12月分三阶段推进；从综合试运行到预计商业运营，中间只有约3个月，明显短于国际大型机场的惯例。国际同业通常会留出更长时间做系统检测、流程演练和突发问题整改，而不是把“开门迎客”当成倒计时仪式。

更麻烦的是，这座项目在冲刺阶段遭遇劳动力与建筑材料短缺，还卷入采购丑闻，多名高管因涉嫌违反招标规定被拘留。据联合早报报道，这些困难并非边角料。机场是一个由值机、行李、安检、海关、边检、地勤、油料、维修共同咬合的系统，任何一环掉链子，旅客感受到的就是混乱。把工程进度当作开业标准，本质上是把“楼盖好了”误当成“生意能做了”。这种误判在东南亚大型基建中并不罕见，隆城只是最新的一课。

## 50公里的距离成本

隆城机场位于胡志明市以东约50公里。这50公里不是地图上的一段线，而是一笔每天都在发生的交易成本。国际航司明确提出：市区到机场的交通必须可靠，还要有中转酒店与医疗服务。原因很简单，长途航班的机组需要休息，转机旅客需要过夜，高端商务客对医疗与酒店有刚性要求。没有这些，机场不过是建在同奈省的一片漂亮建筑。

越南并非没有动作。胡志明市—隆城—油曳高速公路把机场与第一郡的距离压缩到约43公里，目标通行时间30分钟；另有13条快速公交线衔接，首添—隆城都市铁路规划全长42公里、设20个站，意图把市区到机场压到20至25分钟。问题在于，轨道仍在前期规划，公交与酒店不是一夜之间长出来的。对比新加坡樟宜与曼谷素万那普，枢纽竞争力从来不是靠跑道数量，而是靠“落地之后多久能进城、转机有多省心”。越南把机场建在了未来，却还没把城市连接到未来。

## 航季表不服从工程进度

这里有一个被政策叙事刻意模糊的事实：航空公司的排班跟着航季走，不跟着工程节点走。全球航空业以冬、夏两个航季切换时刻表，航司要提前半年以上确定机型、运力和航线。政府希望12月开航，但航司最快也要到2027年冬航季才成规模迁入，这不是拖延，而是行业规律的硬约束。

越南机场公司（ACV）提出的三阶段方案，其实承认了这一点：2026年12月至2027年3月鼓励航司按需运营国际航班；2027年3月至10月把长途国际航班全部迁走；2027年10月起再迁其余国际航班，越南航空运营的1000公里以下短途航线除外。为吸引转场，这家机场运营商还计划在2026至2027年给予20%至50%的航空服务费用减免。国内航司已先行一步，越南航空登记每周72班、越捷航空最多294班，均为国内航线。这种“先试后迁、以价换量”的思路是对的；但如果过渡方案没有配上强制性的协同机制与信息披露，费用减免买来的只是航班数量，买不来枢纽秩序。强行赶在准备期结束前转场，换来的是时刻不稳、成本上升和旅客体验下滑，最后还得回头补课。

## 采购丑闻与制度税

336.63万亿越南盾的总投资，约合130亿美元（约合940亿元人民币）。这个数字不仅是钢筋、水泥与设备的价格，也包含一座国家的制度成本。项目卷入招标违规、多名高管被拘，说明在超大型基建中，真正的预算超支常常不是来自材料涨价，而是来自采购摩擦、责任模糊与反复整改。这些成本不会写在工程量清单上，却会体现在工期与融资成本里。

不过，把丑闻直接等同于项目失败，也是另一种偷懒。问责与建设并不矛盾：正因为机场是长期资产，才更需要在动工之初把招标、审计与进度公开透明。对越南而言，真正的考验不是能不能把跑道铺完，而是能不能在铺跑道的同时把规矩立住。否则，下一座超级工程还会重复同样的剧本：开工时万众期待，投产前丑闻缠身，运营时被迫补课。东南亚并不缺宏大的机场蓝图，缺的是能把蓝图兑现为可靠服务的制度能力。

## 区域枢纽不是跑道数出来的

按规划，隆城机场分三期建设，设4条跑道，全部建成后年旅客吞吐量可达1亿人次、货运吞吐量500万吨；一期则是一条4000米跑道和一座年吞吐2500万人次的航站楼。体量足够诱人，新山一机场的拥堵也确实需要出口——该机场2025年底第三航站楼投用后，总设计容量升至5000万人次，但仍长期在饱和边缘运行。

问题是，枢纽地位从来不是跑道数出来的。新加坡樟宜靠的是中转效率、航权网络和口岸服务，曼谷素万那普靠的是腹地与航线密度。越南若只把隆城当成新山一的“泄压阀”，那它得到的只是一座分流机场；若要成为通往美国、欧洲和澳大利亚的长途门户，就必须同步推进航权开放、海关效率、地勤竞争和多式联运。对越南来说，眼下的选择很清楚：继续用工程倒逼运营，让航司在乱局中自己适应；还是把运营就绪设为开航门槛，用分阶段转场把风险摊薄。12月能否开门，其实没那么重要；开馆之后能不能让34家航司安心落下来，才是越南真正要交的答卷。

## 延伸阅读

**运营准备与机场转移（Operational Readiness and Airport Transfer）**：大型机场开业前的系统性演练，涵盖设施、信息系统、人员培训及海关、边检、安保等部门的协同，通常需数月乃至更长时间。

**航季（Flight Season）**：全球航空业按冬、夏两季切换航班时刻，航司须提前数月确定机型与运力，因此新机场转场无法完全服从工程部门的倒计时。

**枢纽机场（Hub Airport）**：以中转衔接能力而非单体吞吐量定义的机场，竞争力取决于航权、口岸效率、地面交通与酒店医疗等配套服务。

# Long Thanh opens, but airlines hit pause: the last mile of Vietnam's mega-hub

According to Bloomberg, Vietnam's official media have cited a civil aviation survey showing that more than 30 international airlines want to postpone their move from Ho Chi Minh City's Tan Son Nhat airport to the new Long Thanh airport. Twenty-four want to wait until the 2027 winter season; another ten are asking for the 2028 summer season or later. The government had hoped to begin commercial operations in December at a project costing 336.63 trillion dong (about S$17 billion, ~US$13 billion), designed to relieve chronic congestion at Tan Son Nhat. Instead, the airlines have collectively asked for more time. Is this airline intransigence, or a flaw in the project's own schedule? The answer lies in the gap between an airport being built and a hub being usable.

## The mass leave request from 34 airlines

Start with the numbers. A report submitted by the Ministry of Construction to the government says all surveyed international airlines ultimately support moving to Long Thanh, citing modern facilities and capacity for future growth. But 24 want to begin in the 2027 winter season, and ten want 2028 or later. Their reasons are not pretexts. Rebuilding a network means reallocating aircraft and crews, setting up ground handling, upgrading ticketing systems and opening resident offices — work that typically takes six to twelve months. Foreign aviation regulators may also need on-site inspections and safety certification before approving operations.

Changing airports is not a matter of rewriting a departure code; it is a wholesale reordering of commercial contracts and operating procedures. Lianhe Zaobao reports that cargo operators need even longer, perhaps twelve months, for technical assessments of freight handling, customs clearance and ground connections. The airlines also specify reliable ground transport, four-star-or-better transit hotels and quality medical services. Read together, these demands say something simple: a finished terminal is an entry ticket, not the main event. The operational ecosystem is the main event.

## Built is not the same as operational

By late October 2026, about 86 per cent of Long Thanh's key facilities were complete. That is an impressive construction figure, but it measures engineering, not operations. The airport's operational readiness and airport transfer (ORAT) programme comprises 55 scenarios rolled out in three phases between September and December. From integrated trials to the expected commercial opening, the window is roughly three months — far shorter than the norm at major international airports, which usually allow more time for systems testing, procedural drills and rectification before welcoming passengers.

The project has also faced labour and material shortages and a procurement scandal, with several executives detained over alleged bidding violations. None of this is peripheral. An airport is a machine in which check-in, baggage, security, customs, immigration, ground handling, fuel and maintenance must mesh. When one cog fails, passengers experience chaos. Treating construction progress as the opening criterion confuses "the building is finished" with "the business can run". This is not a uniquely Vietnamese mistake; it is a recurring lesson across Southeast Asian megaprojects, and Long Thanh is merely the latest classroom.

## The cost of 50 kilometres

Long Thanh sits about 50 kilometres east of Ho Chi Minh City. Those 50 kilometres are not a line on a map but a transaction cost incurred every single day. International airlines have been explicit: transport between the city and the airport must be reliable, with transit hotels and medical services available. The logic is mundane but decisive. Long-haul crews need rest, connecting passengers need somewhere to sleep, and high-end business travellers treat healthcare and hotels as non-negotiable. Without them, Long Thanh is simply an elegant cluster of buildings in Dong Nai province.

Hanoi is not ignoring the problem. The Ho Chi Minh City–Long Thanh–Dau Giay expressway brings the airport to roughly 43 kilometres from District 1, with a target travel time of 30 minutes. Thirteen rapid bus routes are planned, while the 42-kilometre, 20-station Thu Thiem–Long Thanh urban railway aims to cut the journey to 20–25 minutes. Yet the rail link is still at an early planning stage, and bus routes and hotels do not materialise overnight. Compare Changi or Suvarnabhumi: hub competitiveness has never been a function of runway counts. It is a function of how quickly a passenger can reach the city and how painless the transfer feels. Vietnam has built an airport for the future without yet connecting the city to that future.

## Timetables do not obey construction schedules

A fact that policy narratives tend to blur: airline schedules follow flight seasons, not engineering milestones. The industry switches timetables twice a year, in winter and summer, and carriers must lock in aircraft, capacity and routes months ahead. The government wants a December opening; airlines cannot move at scale until the 2027 winter season. That is not obstruction but a hard constraint of the business.

Airports Corporation of Vietnam (ACV) has effectively conceded the point with a three-stage plan. From December 2026 to March 2027, airlines are encouraged to operate international flights at Long Thanh as needed. From March to October 2027, all long-haul international flights move. From October 2027, the remaining international services follow, except Vietnam Airlines' short-haul routes under 1,000 kilometres. To lure carriers, ACV plans fee reductions of 20 to 50 per cent over 2026–27. Domestic airlines have moved first: Vietnam Airlines has registered 72 weekly flights and Vietjet up to 294, all domestic. This "trial before transfer, incentives for volume" approach is sound. But without binding coordination and transparent information, discounted fees buy flight counts, not hub order. Forcing a transfer before readiness would yield unstable schedules, higher costs and a worse passenger experience — and Vietnam would end up retrofitting anyway.

## Procurement scandal and the institutional tax

The 336.63 trillion dong price tag is about US$13 billion. That figure covers more than steel, concrete and equipment; it includes a country's institutional costs. The project has been entangled in bidding violations and detentions, which suggests that on megaprojects, overruns often come less from material prices than from procurement friction, blurred accountability and repeated remediation. Such costs never appear on a bill of quantities, but they show up in delays and financing.

Equating scandal with project failure, however, is its own laziness. Accountability and construction are not opposites. Precisely because an airport is a long-lived asset, tender rules, auditing and progress disclosure need to be established before the first truck arrives. Vietnam's real test is not whether it can finish the runways but whether it can establish rules while laying them. Otherwise the next megaproject will follow the same script: celebration at groundbreaking, scandal near completion, remediation during operations. Southeast Asia does not lack grand airport blueprints. What it lacks is the institutional capacity to turn a blueprint into dependable service.

## A regional hub is not counted in runways

Under the master plan, Long Thanh will have four runways and, at full build-out, handle 100 million passengers and five million tonnes of cargo a year. Phase one delivers a single 4,000-metre runway and a terminal for 25 million passengers. The scale is seductive, and Tan Son Nhat's congestion is real: even after its third terminal opened in late 2025, lifting design capacity to 50 million passengers a year, the airport still runs close to saturation.

But hub status has never been a function of runway counts. Changi competes through transfer efficiency, traffic rights and border services; Suvarnabhumi through its hinterland and route density. If Vietnam treats Long Thanh merely as a pressure valve for Tan Son Nhat, it will get a relief airport and nothing more. If it wants a long-haul gateway to the United States, Europe and Australia, it must also liberalise traffic rights, raise customs efficiency, foster ground-handling competition and build genuine multimodal links. The choice is stark: keep letting engineering force operations and leave airlines to improvise, or make operational readiness the condition for opening and spread risk through phased transfer. Whether the doors open in December is almost beside the point. Whether 34 airlines can settle in without regret is the answer Vietnam actually owes.

## Further reading

**Operational Readiness and Airport Transfer (ORAT)**: The structured rehearsal before a major airport opens, covering facilities, information systems, staff training and coordination among customs, immigration and security agencies; it normally takes months.

**Flight season**: The twice-yearly winter and summer schedule cycles that govern global aviation. Carriers commit aircraft and capacity months in advance, so a new airport's transfer cannot simply follow a construction countdown.

**Hub airport**: An airport defined by transfer connectivity rather than raw throughput, whose competitiveness depends on traffic rights, border efficiency, ground transport, hotels and medical services.
