# 十七名华裔青年，一座自贸港："侨海逐梦"与华裔新生代的认同之问

2026年8月23日，在中国南海（South China Sea）之滨的海南海口，十七名华裔大学生为一趟为期十七天的旅程画上句号。他们来自日本、美国、匈牙利。他们走进本地企业实习，坐上环岛观光巴士，穿行于红树林湿地，也旁听了城际交通产品的设计讨论。这场活动名为"侨海逐梦"，其设定的目标，是让海外华裔青年"近距离感受"海南自贸港的发展脉动。次日清晨，中国新闻网经中国侨网（China Qiao Wang）发布了结营仪式的报道。

这是一则很小的故事。十七名学生，三个国家，一座岛屿。然而它恰好卡在两个更大的问题交汇处：一个据称拥有六千万海外公民的国家，究竟如何看待侨胞中最年轻的那一代；以及，"祖籍国"对一群从未在故土生活过的年轻人，究竟意味着什么。这场研习活动值得细看，不在其规模，而在它揭示了中国面向侨务青年工作的悄然逻辑。

## 十七天的窗口

活动的设计是有意混合的。据报道，它以"实习体验+文化探索"的形式展开，包含省情考察、企业实习与文化体验。这些学生不是游客——他们被安排进企业，被要求弄懂一家中国企业究竟如何运转。一名在日本中央大学就读的学子在海南海汽运输集团实习，跟随带教老师深入一线调研，折服于这个省份如何把交通与文旅编织成一条完整的链条：城际出行产品、环岛旅游公路的观光巴士，以及绿色低碳、智慧出行的长远布局。

他们也不只是宾客。活动引导你去观察、形成判断，并把判断带回家。日本中央大学的苗益诚对记者说，这次实习"让大家看到了海南不同的一面"，这里"不仅有美丽的自然风景，也有丰富的文化和正在不断发展的产业"。他表示，要把所见所闻讲给海外的亲朋好友听。

这句话几乎是整场活动的缩影：看见、吸收、再转述。

## 华裔新生代：既非游客，也非归侨

这些年轻人是谁？主办方所用的标签——"华裔新生代"——精确而关键。他们不是早年的归侨，不是那一代扬帆归国、建设新中国的华侨。他们大多生于海外、持外国护照，并将在异国展开自己的人生。用那个尴尬却有用的说法，他们是"第三文化"的一代：在某种维度上承袭中国文化，在国籍上另属他国，在全球流动中如鱼得水。

这一点之所以重要，是因为它改变了互动的前提。早年的侨务政策（diaspora policy）尚可诉诸爱国心，或诉诸对祖籍国的责任。对一名生于匈牙利或日本的大学在校生，这些音符未必能引发共鸣。这场活动给出的替代方案更柔软，也更务实：一扇窗。在欧洲求学的李政桥说，他对海南的印象此前"更多停留在旅游度假"，直到走进临高金牌港，才发现这里"不仅有阳光沙滩，还有正在崛起的现代产业集群"。他的判断很能说明问题——自贸港的政策红利"正在转化为实实在在的产业动能"，对海外华裔青年而言，"既是了解祖籍国发展的窗口，也是未来参与合作的机遇"。

请注意这里的措辞。主办方并非召唤这些年轻人回家，而是请他们去理解，并或许有朝一日参与合作。这是一套为"注定留在海外"的侨胞量身校准的侨务逻辑——倘若体验能落地，他们便可能成为非正式的桥梁。

## 海南自贸港：被讲述的"发展故事"

为何是海南？这个选择绝非偶然。海南正在建设北京（Beijing）所称"具有世界影响力的中国特色自由贸易港"，按官方表述，对标"世界最高水平开放形态"。对一场侨务青年活动而言，这座岛屿几乎是完美的叙事载体：它同时是一张旅游明信片与一处发展样板，是"绿水青山"（学生们被鼓励去留意的环境线索）与"实实在在的产业动能"并置的地方。

海南省委统战部二级巡视员张建伟在结营仪式上把利害讲得很直白。他说，海南需要"一大批有国际视野、思维、阅历的华裔新生代"来建设自贸港。他承诺为有志来琼发展的华裔新生代打造更优的服务环境，并希望同学们把所见所闻带回去，"向更多人讲述中国故事、海南自贸港故事"，成为中外友好交往的"民间使者"、弘扬中华文化的践行者。

说白了，自贸港被同时当作一个论点与一份邀约：论点是，中国的发展是真实且值得理解的；邀约则是，请侨胞的年轻一代在其中找到自己的位置。

## 从"引资"到"育人"：侨务工作的代际转向

把镜头拉远，这场活动像是本刊此前考察过的一股更大转向的微小注脚——中国侨务治理正从"引资引智"（吸引成熟侨商精英的投资与才能）转向"服务到人"，如今更日益明确地走向"育人"。目标不再只是能开支票的巨贾，或能转移技术的学者，而是那名尚未掌权的学生，那名身份与认同仍在成形中的年轻人。

这里自有其逻辑。1978年后第一代移民渐次老去；商会、宗亲会、华文学校等侨社机构的代际接续，取决于下一代是否愿意接棒。一场把二十岁青年塞进海南某企业实习两周的活动，从某种意义上看，正是对这场接续的一次长线下注。说句实话，它也比笼络自带议程的成熟侨领更便宜、更少政治包袱。

但"育人"的转向也暴露出软肋。对你无法掌控的侨胞做柔性治理，本就艰难。你可以让一个年轻人看红树林、看自贸港，却无法让他心生归属。活动提供的是体验，能否生成认同，决定权在学生手里。

## "民间使者"：期待与限度

整个故事里最耐人寻味的一句，是那句明确的期许：回去讲中国故事。这是一记教科书式的软实力动作，值得冷静地读。

这种做法的长处在于真实。他们不是拿钱的宣传员，而是自愿把部分暑假花在海南的年轻人，且据各方说法，他们确实被其中某些部分真正打动。一句"我在一家运输集团实习，这是我的所见"的同伴讲述，远比任何官方播报更能抵达人心。对那些有时夹在居住国政治与故土情感之间的华侨华人社区而言，一名能说出"它比你以为的更复杂、更有趣"的学生，是一个真正有用的节点。

其限度也同样真实。其一，自愿是一把双刃剑：正是那份让证词可信的自主性，也意味着他们不欠忠诚，且可能不同意。其二，"讲中国故事"模式假定体验本身足够动人；一场让人觉出排练痕迹的定制行程会反噬，尤其面对能嗅出脚本的数位原住民青年。其三，是本刊此前提出过的对称性问题——一个请侨胞担任文化使者的国家，须准备好让这些使者用自己的声音说话，包括异见。无法容纳微妙差别的拓展，恰恰是在悄悄教会不信任。

这一切并不否定这场活动。但它提醒我们：侨务工作最难的部分从来不是发出邀请，而是受邀者之后会怎么做。

## 延伸阅读

- **华人 / 华裔（Huaren / Huayi）**："华人"一般指持有外国公民身份的族裔中国人；"华裔"特指生于海外、有中国血统者。侨务政策中二者常混用，但就公民身份与忠诚问题而言，区分很重要。
- **海南自由贸易港（Hainan Free Trade Port）**：2020年启动的省级试点，目标在2030年代早期建成零关税、投资贸易自由便利的园区，是中国"高水平对外开放"的旗舰工程。
- **侨务（Qiaowu）**：中国对接海外侨胞的制度性机构体系，历史上由侨办与统战系统承担；其重心已从招商引资逐步移向文化与代际侨务拓展。

# Seventeen Young Overseas Chinese, One Free-Trade Island: 'Qiaohai Zhumeng' and the Question of Identity for a New Diaspora Generation

On 23 August 2026, in Haikou, the capital of China's southern island province of Hainan, seventeen ethnic-Chinese university students brought a seventeen-day journey to a close. They had come from Japan, the United States, and Hungary. They had interned at local firms, ridden the ring-island tourist buses, walked through mangrove wetlands, and sat in on the planning of intercity transport products. The programme was called "Qiaohai Zhumeng" — roughly, "dreaming across the seas as qiao" — and its stated aim was to let overseas Chinese youth "feel up close the pulse" of Hainan's free-trade port. China News Service, carried by China Qiaowang, reported the closing ceremony the next morning.

This is a small story. Seventeen students, three countries, one island. Yet it sits at the intersection of two much larger questions: how a state with an estimated sixty million citizens abroad thinks about its diaspora's youngest generation, and what "the motherland" can mean to young people who have never lived there. The camp is worth a closer look not for its scale but for what it reveals about the quiet logic of China's diaspora-youth outreach.

## The 17-day window

The format was deliberately hybrid. According to the report, the activity combined "internship experience" with "cultural exploration" — provincial familiarisation tours, enterprise internships, and cultural immersion. The students were not tourists; they were placed in companies and asked to learn how a Chinese firm actually works. One student interning at the Hainan Haiqi Transport Group followed a mentor into front-line research and came away impressed by how the province weaves transport and tourism into a single chain — intercity travel products, sightseeing buses along the ring-island highway, a green and "smart" mobility roadmap.

Nor were they simply guests. They were nudged to observe, to form a view, and to carry that view home. Miao Yicheng of Chuo University in Japan told the reporter that the internship "let everyone see a different side of Hainan" — not only scenery but "a culture that is rich and an industry that is developing." He said he would tell overseas relatives and friends what he had seen and heard.

That sentence is the whole programme in miniature: see, absorb, and relay.

## The new generation: neither tourists nor returnees

Who are these young people? The label used by the organisers — "华裔新生代," the new generation of Chinese descent — is precise and consequential. These are not the qiaobao of an earlier era, the returned overseas Chinese who sailed home to build a new China. Most were born abroad, hold foreign passports, and will build their lives outside China. They are, in the awkward but useful phrase, a "third culture" generation: culturally Chinese in some register, nationally something else, globally mobile by default.

This matters because it changes the terms of engagement. An earlier diaspora policy could plausibly appeal to patriotism or to a duty to the ancestral land. For a Hungarian- or Japanese-born university student, those chords do not necessarily resonate. What the camp offers instead is something softer and more pragmatic: a window. Li Zhengqiao, a student based in Europe, said his impression of Hainan had been "mostly tourism and vacation" until he visited the Lingao Gold Port development zone and found "not only sunshine and beaches but a modern industrial cluster on the rise." His verdict is telling — the policy dividend, he said, is "being turned into real industrial momentum," which for overseas Chinese youth is "both a window to understand the ancestral country's development and an opportunity to participate in future cooperation."

Note the framing. The organisers are not asking these young people to come home. They are asking them to understand, and perhaps one day to cooperate. That is a diaspora policy calibrated for people who will remain abroad — and who may, if the experience lands, become informal bridges.

## The free-trade port: a 'development story' to be told

Why Hainan? The choice is not accidental. Hainan is building what Beijing calls a "free-trade port with Chinese characteristics and global influence," benchmarked, in the official phrase, against "the world's highest level of openness." For a diaspora-youth programme, the island is an almost perfect narrative vehicle: it is simultaneously a tourist postcard and a development showcase, a place where "green mountains and clear water" (the environmental line the students were encouraged to notice) sit alongside "real industrial momentum."

Zhang Jianwei, a senior official from Hainan's United Front Work Department, framed the stakes directly at the closing ceremony. Hainan, he said, needs "a large cohort of ethnic-Chinese youth with an international vision, mindset, and experience" to build its free-trade port. He promised a better service environment for those who wish to develop on the island, and he asked the students to take what they had seen and "tell the story of China, the story of Hainan's free-trade port" — to become "grassroots envoys" of friendship and practitioners who carry forward Chinese culture.

Read plainly, the free-trade port is being offered as both an argument and an invitation: an argument that China's development is real and worth understanding, and an invitation for the diaspora's young to find a role in it.

## From 'wooing capital' to 'nurturing people': the generational turn in diaspora work

Step back, and the camp looks like one small instance of a broader shift this newsletter has examined before — the move in China's diaspora governance from "wooing capital" (attracting investment and talent from established émigré business elites) toward "serving people" and now, increasingly, "nurturing people." The target is no longer only the tycoon who can write a cheque or the scholar who can transfer technology. It is the student, the not-yet-powerful, the person whose loyalty and identity are still being formed.

There is a logic to this. The first generation of post-1978 emigrants is ageing; the succession of diaspora institutions — chambers of commerce, clan associations, language schools — depends on whether the next generation shows up. A camp that puts a 20-year-old in a Hainan firm for two weeks is, among other things, a long bet on that succession. It is also, frankly, cheaper and less politically loaded than courting established community leaders who may carry their own agendas.

But the turn toward "nurturing" also exposes a vulnerability. Soft governance of a diaspora you do not control is hard. You can show a young person a mangrove and a free-trade port; you cannot make them feel belonging. The camp provides the experience. Whether it produces identity is up to the student.

## 'Grassroots envoys': expectations and their limits

The most revealing line of the whole story is the explicit ask: go home and tell China's story. It is a textbook soft-power move, and it deserves a clear-eyed read.

The strength of the approach is its authenticity. These are not paid propagandists; they are young people who chose to spend part of a summer in Hainan and who, by all accounts, found parts of it genuinely impressive. A peer-to-peer account — "I interned at a transport group and here is what I saw" — travels better than any official broadcast. For overseas Chinese communities that sometimes feel caught between host-country politics and homeland sentiment, a student who can say "it's more complex and more interesting than you think" is a genuinely useful node.

The limits are equally real. First, voluntariness cuts both ways: the same autonomy that makes their testimony credible also means they owe no loyalty and may disagree. Second, the "tell the story" model assumes the experience is compelling on its own terms; a curated programme that feels staged will backfire, especially with digital-native youth who can smell a script. Third, there is a symmetry problem this newsletter has raised before — a state that asks its diaspora to be cultural ambassadors should be prepared for those ambassadors to speak with their own voices, including dissent. Outreach that cannot tolerate nuance is outreach that quietly teaches distrust.

None of this invalidates the camp. But it is a reminder that the hardest part of diaspora work is not the invitation. It is what the invitee does afterwards.

## Further reading

- **Huaren / Huayi (华人 / 华裔)**: "Huaren" generally denotes ethnic Chinese who hold foreign citizenship; "huayi" (华裔) specifically marks people of Chinese descent born abroad. The two are often used interchangeably in diaspora policy, but the distinction matters for questions of citizenship and loyalty.
- **Hainan Free Trade Port (海南自由贸易港)**: A provincial-level pilot launched in 2020 to become a zero-tariff, free-flowing trade and investment zone by the early 2030s; it is a centrepiece of China's "high-level opening-up."
- **Qiaowu (侨务)**: China's institutional apparatus for engaging the Chinese diaspora, historically housed in overseas Chinese affairs offices and the United Front system; its emphasis has shifted over time from investment attraction to cultural and generational outreach.
