# 胡志明市低排放区推迟半年：绿色转型“慢半拍”，千万摩托车的绿色账单

## 数字：一座被尾气“锁喉”的超级都市

新胡志明市（2026年7月1日并入巴地—头顿省与平阳省后）人口已达1400万，却背负着逾100万辆汽车与730万辆摩托车的“移动污染源”。据市政建设部门披露，交通运输贡献了全市88%的氮氧化物、99%的一氧化碳、79%的二氧化硫与88%的颗粒物排放；平均细颗粒物（PM2.5）浓度高达31微克/立方米，是世界卫生组织建议限值的6倍。这组数字并非抽象的环保指标——它意味着每年因空气污染死亡的人数，已经超过交通事故致死人数。更要命的是经济代价：相关估算显示，空气污染每年造成逾3万亿越南盾（约8.1亿元人民币）的损失，相当于全市地区生产总值的0.25%。一座以“摩托车上的城市”闻名的东南亚经济引擎，正被自己的尾气锁喉。我们不妨直说：当出行工具本身就是污染源，所谓“绿色转型”便不再是可选项，而是生存题。

## 延期背后：被规划“慢放”的绿色转型

《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》9月6日报道，胡志明市将低排放区的落地推迟了半年。这并非孤例式的技术拖延，而是一场被规划“慢放”的绿色转型。低排放区（Low-Emission Zone，简称LEZ）的设想并不激进：先在市中心以15座桥梁、20条主干道为界的核心区，以及沿如社路（Rung Sac）一带划出禁限区，逐步限制不达排放标准的汽柴油车进入；公交车、出租车、网约车与货车则被列为首批“换电”对象，目标2030年前将全市公交车队转为电动或清洁能源。时间轴从2026年的试点，一路铺到2032年全市域最严标准。问题在于，原定2026年初启动的方案已被推迟，而回望旧方案，连“2027年中起试办”如今也成了修订后的新基准。一个耐人寻味的问题是：当污染代价以“每年超过交通事故死亡”的速度累积，规划者为何仍有底气把治理节奏往后挪？答案或许藏在“给居民和企业留出换车时间”这句体面的说辞里——它既是对民生成本的敬畏，也未必不是对既得利益与执行难度的妥协。

## 千万摩托与“电车焦虑”：绿色账单谁来买单

绿色转型最锋利的刃，砍在730万辆摩托车身上。摩托车是越南都市平民的腿，是外卖骑手、摩托出租车司机的饭碗；要他们在几年内换上电动摩托或达标车，等于让最脆弱的一群人先替全城的空气买单。规划已列出激励：用于营运服务的电动车全免注册费、电动汽车养路费减半、换牌费减半。但“减半”与“全免”的前提，是你先买得起一辆新车。对月收入有限的骑手而言，一辆合规电摩的价差，可能就是半个月的饭钱。这恰是许多发展中国家绿色政策的通病：减排目标写在文件里，成本却落在最没议价能力的肩膀上。胡志明市若真想让低排放区落地而不沦为“富人区特权”，就必须回答一个朴素的问题——绿色账单，到底该由谁付？

## 丰田的游说：混动能否写进低排放区

在这场政策博弈中，日本车企已先一步下注。据《日经亚洲》报道，丰田越南（日本丰田子公司）在9月6日越南—日本地方合作论坛（顺化）上，向越南总理提请：在未来的低排放区中，将“自充电混动车（HEV）”与纯电动车（BEV）并列认定为优先通行车辆。丰田的理由很直白——混动无需充电基建、不改变驾驶习惯，还能在断电或自然灾害时照常行驶，更契合越南国情；它同时建议将特别消费税从按排量计改为按二氧化碳排放计。这不是单纯的环保倡议，而是一次精明的产业链卡位：丰田已宣布在越南投资3.6亿美元（约25.6亿元人民币），扩产并准备量产自充电混动等电动车型。日系车在越南燃油车市场根基深厚，如今借“绿色”之名争取过渡期红利，既延缓纯电对燃油车的替代，又为自身混动路线争取政策护城河。对河内而言，这是一道两难：拒绝混动，等于把转型成本全压给消费者；放行混动，则低排放区的“低排放”成色要打折扣。

## 双城记：河内先行，胡志明掉队

有意思的是，在越南的绿色赛道上，胡志明市并非领跑者。河内早在今年6月便宣布，计划将汽油动力网约车摩托车逐步排除出低排放区，先从自愿做起，2027年1月起全面禁止。两相比较，河内动得更早、更硬，胡志明市则因体量更大、摩托更密、利益更杂而显得步履蹒跚。这并非简单的“谁更环保”之争，而是两座超级城市治理能力的分野：河内用行政命令抢跑，胡志明市用分阶段路线图求稳。谁的路径更可持续，尚难定论；但可以确定的是，当双城各自划定低排放区，越南的绿色转型将不再是笼统的国家口号，而会被拆成一场场可量化、可问责的城市实验。对东南亚而言，这或许比任何一份区域宣言都更实在——因为空气，从不会在国境线上止步。

## 结语：低排放区不是终点，而是治理的试金石

归根结底，低排放区不是绿色转型的终点，而是城市治理能力的一块试金石。胡志明市推迟半年，给了居民喘息，也给了污染继续累积的窗口；它暴露的不是规划的失败，而是一个更根本的难题——当“绿色”遇上“公平”，当减排指令撞上千万辆摩托车赖以谋生的车轮，治理者必须在理想与现实之间找到那条窄路。越南的教训对整片东南亚都有参照意义：从雅加达的雾霾到曼谷的尾气，从马尼拉的电摩洪流到湄公河畔的燃油船，低排放区迟早会从河内、胡志明的试点，走向更多城市的议事桌。真正的考验，从来不是划出一条禁行线，而是让这条线划得下去、收得上来、且不让最普通的人为之付出生计代价。胡志明市这半年的“慢半拍”，值得整片区域记取。

## 延伸阅读

**低排放区（Low-Emission Zone, LEZ）**：在城市特定区域限制高排放车辆通行的交通与环境政策；胡志明市与河内均为越南首批试点，是东南亚城市绿色转型的前沿实验。

**电动汽车（Electric Vehicle, EV）**：以电力驱动、零尾气排放的车辆；越南正以公交、出租车与网约车为首批电动化对象，但私家车与摩托车的转换成本仍是落地瓶颈。

**日本—越南产业链协作（Japan–Vietnam Supply Chain Cooperation）**：日系车企借低排放区政策争取混动过渡红利，是东南亚绿色转型中“外资利益与本土减排目标”博弈的典型样本。

# Ho Chi Minh City Hits Pause on Low-Emission Zones: A Six-Month Delay in the Green Transition, and the Dilemma of Millions of Motorbikes

## The numbers: a megacity choking on its own exhaust

The newly enlarged Ho Chi Minh City — formed on 1 July 2026 by merging the former Ba Ria–Vung Tau and Binh Duong provinces — is home to 14 million people, yet it carries a "mobile source of pollution" of more than 1 million cars and 7.3 million motorcycles. According to the municipal Department of Construction, transport accounts for 88% of the city's nitrogen oxides, 99% of its carbon monoxide, 79% of its sulphur dioxide and 88% of particulate matter. Average PM2.5 stands at 31 micrograms per cubic metre — six times the World Health Organization's recommended limit. These are not abstract environmental metrics. They mean that air-pollution-related deaths in the city now outnumber those from traffic accidents every year. The economic cost is starker still: estimates put the annual loss from air pollution at over VND3 trillion (about US$114 million), equivalent to 0.25% of the city's GRDP. A Southeast Asian growth engine long famous as a "city on two wheels" is being strangled by its own exhaust. To state it plainly: when the means of getting around is itself the source of pollution, a "green transition" stops being optional and becomes a matter of survival.

## Behind the delay: a green transition put on slow motion

Nikkei Asia reported on 6 September that Ho Chi Minh City has delayed the rollout of its low-emission zone by six months. This is not a one-off technical slip; it is a green transition placed on slow motion by planning. The idea of a Low-Emission Zone (LEZ) is not radical. It would first draw restricted zones in the core area bounded by 15 bridges and 20 main roads, plus a stretch along Rung Sac Road, and gradually keep out petrol and diesel vehicles that fail emission standards. Buses, taxis, ride-hailing vehicles and trucks are named as the first batch to "go electric," with the goal of converting the entire city bus fleet to electric or clean-fuel vehicles by 2030. The timeline runs from a 2026 pilot all the way to the strictest citywide standard in 2032. The catch is that the scheme originally slated to start in early 2026 has already been pushed back, and what was once "mid-2027 pilot" is now the revised baseline. One cannot help but ask: when the cost of pollution accumulates at a pace that outruns traffic fatalities, why do planners still feel entitled to move the governance clock backward? The answer may lie in the polite formula of "giving residents and businesses time to switch vehicles" — a gesture of respect for household costs that is, just as plausibly, a compromise with vested interests and implementation difficulty.

## Millions of motorbikes and "EV anxiety": who pays the green bill

The sharpest edge of the green transition falls on the 7.3 million motorcycles. The motorbike is the legs of Vietnam's urban poor and the livelihood of delivery riders and moto-taxi drivers; asking them to switch to electric or compliant bikes within a few years is asking the most vulnerable to pay first for the city's air. The plan lists incentives — a full registration-fee waiver for electric vehicles used in transport services, a 50% cut in road-maintenance fees for electric cars, and a 50% reduction in plate-change fees. But the premise of "half" or "full waiver" is that you can first afford a new vehicle. For a rider on a thin monthly income, the price gap of a compliant e-motorbike can be half a month's meals. This is the chronic ailment of green policy in many developing countries: the emission target is written into the document, but the cost lands on the shoulders with the least bargaining power. If HCMC truly wants its LEZ to land without becoming a "privilege of the affluent districts," it must answer a plain question — who, exactly, should foot the green bill?

## Toyota's lobby: can hybrids earn a place in the low-emission zone

In this policy contest, Japanese carmakers have already placed their bets. Nikkei Asia reports that Toyota Vietnam, a subsidiary of Toyota Motor, urged the Vietnamese prime minister at the Vietnam–Japan Local Cooperation Forum in Hue on 6 September to recognise self-charging hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs) alongside battery electric vehicles (BEVs) as priority vehicles in future LEZs. Toyota's reasoning is blunt: hybrids need no charging infrastructure, change no driving habits, and keep running during power outages or natural disasters, making them better suited to Vietnam's conditions. It also proposed shifting the special consumption tax from engine displacement to CO2 emissions. This is no mere environmental plea; it is a shrewd move to stake out the supply chain. Toyota has announced a US$360 million investment in Vietnam to expand capacity and prepare mass production of self-charging hybrids and other EV models. With deep roots in Vietnam's petrol-car market, Japan's carmakers are now using the "green" label to secure a transitional dividend — slowing the substitution of petrol by pure electric while carving a policy moat for their hybrid line. For Hanoi the dilemma is real: reject hybrids and the transition cost falls entirely on consumers; admit them and the "low-emission" credentials of the zone take a discount.

## A tale of two cities: Hanoi moves first, HCMC lags

Interestingly, in Vietnam's green race HCMC is not the front-runner. Hanoi announced as early as June that it would phase petrol-powered ride-hailing motorbikes out of its LEZ, starting voluntarily and moving to a full ban from January 2027. Side by side, Hanoi moved earlier and harder, while HCMC — larger in scale, denser in motorbikes, more tangled in interests — appears to limp along. This is not simply a contest of "who is greener," but a divergence in the governing capacity of two megacities: Hanoi seizes the lead with administrative orders, HCMC seeks stability with a phased roadmap. Which path is more durable remains open; what is certain is that as the two cities each draw their LEZs, Vietnam's green transition ceases to be a vague national slogan and is broken into quantifiable, accountable urban experiments. For Southeast Asia this may be more tangible than any regional declaration — because air never stops at a border.

## The bottom line: the LEZ is not the finish line but a test of governance

In the end, the low-emission zone is not the destination of the green transition but a touchstone of urban governance. HCMC's six-month delay gives residents breathing room and also a window for pollution to keep accumulating; what it exposes is not a failure of planning but a more fundamental difficulty — when "green" meets "fair," when an emission order collides with the wheels millions of motorbikes depend on for a living, the governor must find the narrow path between ideal and reality. Vietnam's lesson resonates across Southeast Asia: from Jakarta's haze to Bangkok's tailpipes, from Manila's e-motorbike tides to the fuel boats on the Mekong, the LEZ will sooner or later move from the pilots in Hanoi and HCMC onto the agenda of more cities. The real test was never to draw a restriction line, but to make that line enforceable, reversible, and not paid for in the livelihoods of ordinary people. HCMC's half-beat of "slow motion" is worth the whole region taking to heart.

## Further reading

**Low-Emission Zone (LEZ)**: A transport and environmental policy that restricts high-emission vehicles within designated urban areas; HCMC and Hanoi are among Vietnam's first pilots and sit at the frontier of Southeast Asian cities' green transition.

**Electric Vehicle (EV)**: A vehicle powered by electricity with zero tailpipe emissions; Vietnam is electrifying buses, taxis and ride-hailing fleets first, yet the conversion cost for private cars and motorbikes remains the binding constraint.

**Japan–Vietnam Supply Chain Cooperation**: Japanese carmakers leveraging LEZ policy to secure a hybrid-transition dividend, a typical case of the tug-of-war between foreign commercial interests and a host country's emission-reduction goals in Southeast Asia's green shift.
