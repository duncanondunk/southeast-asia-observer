# 日越推进核能合作：越南押注日本小型模块化反应堆

## 事件：苏林时隔11年访日，核能备忘录落地

11月9日，正在日本访问的越南国家主席苏林与首相高市早苗举行会谈，双方签署核能合作备忘录，计划在2040年后于越南建设新一代核反应堆。这不只是一次例行的国事访问——这是自2015年阮富仲访日以来，越南国家主席时隔11年再度踏上日本国土。一句「东南亚首个采用日本核能技术的国家」，把河内与东京的战略绑定，写进了区域能源版图的长期坐标。

据联合早报报道，苏林应日本政府邀请，自星期天起对日本进行为期四天的访问。高市早苗在会谈中高调重申「自由开放印太（FOIP）」理念，明言希望与越南共同应对区域挑战。把核能写进联合声明，本身就是这一框架落子的具体信号。

## 日本的算盘：核能出口这张「国家名片」要重新擦亮

日本核电在2011年福岛事故后几乎成了「出口禁区」，安倍晋三当年高调推销的「核电外交」雷声大、雨点小。如今高市早苗政府把核能合作写进与越南的联合声明，背后是清晰的产业逻辑：国内机组重启缓慢、海外订单寥寥，日本核电产业链急需一针强心剂。

拿下越南这个「东南亚第一单」，既为日立、东芝、三菱重工等厂商打开东盟市场，也把核能包装成「自由开放印太」框架下的安全资产。说白了，东京要的不是一座反应堆的利润，而是一张重新擦亮的「国家名片」——证明日本仍能在高端能源与战略基建上，与中韩争夺东盟的话语权。

## 越南的能源焦虑：从2016年弃核到2040年重启

越南并非核电新兵。2009年，河内立项在宁顺省建设两座核电厂，原定由俄罗斯承建宁顺一号、日本承建宁顺二号，原计划投资约100亿美元（约720亿元人民币），后因成本攀升至约180亿美元（约1296亿元人民币）、叠加福岛事故后的安全疑虑，于2016年叫停。

讽刺的是，此番选定新建的两座核电厂，恰恰落在庆和省与原宁顺省——当年弃核的旧址，如今成了重启的起点。制造业（三星、苹果供应链、富士康）涌入带来的电力饥渴，让这个煤电占比近半的国家急于寻找稳定基荷电源。越南提出的「2045年跻身高收入国家」目标，更离不开一场能源结构的彻底换血。

## 技术路线：押注小型模块化反应堆

越南选定的是日本研发的新一代小型模块化反应堆（SMR）。与传统百万千瓦级大堆相比，SMR单堆功率较小、可工厂预制、模块化部署，被多国视为兼顾安全性与灵活性的核电复兴路径。

但现实很骨感：全球SMR商业化普遍延期、成本超支，美国相关项目便是前车之鉴。越南押注的，更像是一张2040年后的远期期权，而非眼前的供电方案。在技术尚未在本土跑通的当下，这份备忘录的象征意义，远大于其工程确定性。

## 安保与关键矿产：核能之外的战略绑定

核能只是清单上的一项。联合声明同时确认在安保领域推进合作，日本将通过「政府安全保障能力强化支援（OSA）」机制，向越南提供用于联合国维和行动的装备；双方还将在关键矿产领域加快具体合作。

能源、安保、矿产——三张牌叠在一起，指向同一个战略坐标：在中美之间，越南用「竹式外交（bamboo diplomacy）」借力日本，平衡与最大贸易伙伴中国之间的张力。核能合作不过是这条主轴上一颗最显眼的棋子。

## 中国的阴影：最大贸易伙伴与主权争议并存

报道点得很直白：中国虽是越南最大的贸易伙伴，但双方在南沙群岛等地存在主权争议，日本与越南因此在部分战略利益上拥有共同关切。这层「阴影」，才是核能备忘录真正的底色。

越南对华贸易依存度高（2024年双边贸易额约2000亿美元，约1.44万亿人民币），却在南海议题上与东京呼应。这种「经济上靠中国、安全上靠日本」的走钢丝，正是河内长期的外交常态。核能合作不会改写这一常态，却会让它在可见的未来更加显性。

## 现实门槛：成本、时间与公众接受度

把愿景变成电，路还很长。2040年后的时间表意味着至少十余年的审批、选址、建设与公众沟通；越南公众对核电的记忆仍停留在2016年「说停就停」的反复；SMR在日本的本土商业化都尚未跑通，遑论出口落地。

更现实的是，同一次会谈还签下了燃气-蒸汽联合循环（GTCC）电厂贷款协议、推动液化天然气（LNG）项目——相比遥不可及的核反应堆，这些化石电源才是越南未来十年的真实供电主力。核能的回归，注定是一场慢变量，而非解近渴的甘霖。

## 延伸阅读

**小型模块化反应堆（Small Modular Reactor, SMR）**：单堆功率通常小于300兆瓦、可工厂预制模块化部署的新一代反应堆，被多国视为兼顾安全性与灵活性的核电复兴路径。

**自由开放印太（Free and Open Indo-Pacific, FOIP）**：日本倡导、强调航行自由与法治的地区秩序构想，越南以「竹式外交」在南海议题上与之呼应。

**政府安全保障能力强化支援（Official Security Assistance, OSA）**：日本2023年设立的框架，向「志同道合」国家无偿提供防卫装备与基建，已涵盖菲律宾、马来西亚等。

# Japan–Vietnam Advance Nuclear Ties: Vietnam Bets on Japanese SMRs

## The event: To Lam's Tokyo visit and the nuclear MoU

On November 9, Vietnamese President To Lam, on a visit to Japan, held talks with Prime Minister Takaichi Sanae. The two signed a memorandum on nuclear cooperation, planning to build a new-generation reactor in Vietnam after 2040. This was no routine state visit: it was the first trip to Japan by a Vietnamese president in 11 years, since Nguyen Phu Trong's visit in 2015. The phrase "the first Southeast Asian country to adopt Japanese nuclear technology" inscribes Hanoi's and Tokyo's strategic alignment onto the long-term map of the region's energy geography.

According to Lianhe Zaobao, To Lam was invited by the Japanese government for a four-day visit beginning that Sunday. Takaichi openly reaffirmed the "Free and Open Indo-Pacific (FOIP)" vision and said Tokyo wants to tackle regional challenges together with Hanoi. Writing nuclear energy into the joint statement is itself a concrete signal of that framework in action.

## Japan's calculus: polishing the nuclear export brand again

After the 2011 Fukushima disaster, Japanese nuclear power became almost a no-go zone for exports; the "nuclear diplomacy" touted by Shinzo Abe generated far more noise than orders. Now the Takaichi government has written nuclear cooperation into the joint statement with Vietnam, driven by a clear industrial logic: with domestic reactor restarts slow and overseas orders scarce, Japan's nuclear supply chain needs a shot in the arm.

Securing Vietnam as the "first Southeast Asian customer" opens the ASEAN market for Hitachi, Toshiba and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, while repackaging nuclear energy as a security asset under the "Free and Open Indo-Pacific" framework. In plain terms, Tokyo is after not the profit of a single reactor but a re-polished "national brand" — proof that Japan can still compete with China and South Korea for influence over ASEAN's high-end energy and strategic infrastructure.

## Vietnam's energy anxiety: from the 2016 cancellation to a 2040 restart

Vietnam is no newcomer to nuclear power. In 2009, Hanoi approved two nuclear plants in Ninh Thuan province — Ninh Thuan 1 to be built by Russia, Ninh Thuan 2 by Japan — with an original outlay of about US$10 billion (about RMB72 billion), later ballooning to some US$18 billion (about RMB129.6 billion) before the plan was shelved in 2016 over cost overruns and post-Fukushima safety fears.

The irony is sharp: the two new sites now chosen — Khanh Hoa and the original Ninh Thuan province — sit exactly where the abandoned plants once stood. With manufacturers (Samsung, Apple suppliers, Foxconn) flooding in and stoking an electricity hunger, a country still reliant on coal for nearly half its power is desperate for stable baseload capacity. Vietnam's stated goal of reaching high-income status by 2045 depends on nothing less than a thorough overhaul of its energy mix.

## The technology bet: small modular reactors

What Vietnam selected is Japan's new-generation small modular reactor (SMR). Unlike traditional gigawatt-scale reactors, SMRs have lower per-unit capacity, can be factory-built and deployed modularly, and are promoted by many countries as a safer, more flexible path to a nuclear revival.

The reality is sobering: global SMR commercialization has been widely delayed and over budget, and related U.S. projects are a cautionary tale. What Vietnam is betting on looks less like an imminent supply solution and more like a long-dated option expiring after 2040. With the technology not yet proven at home in Japan, the memorandum's symbolic weight far exceeds its engineering certainty.

## Security and critical minerals: the strategic bind beyond nuclear

Nuclear energy is only one item on the list. The joint statement also confirms cooperation in security, with Japan to provide equipment for UN peacekeeping to Vietnam through the Official Security Assistance (OSA) framework, and the two sides to accelerate cooperation in critical minerals.

Energy, security, minerals — stacked together, they point to one strategic coordinate: between the United States and China, Vietnam uses its "bamboo diplomacy" to leverage Japan and balance the tension with its largest trading partner, China. Nuclear cooperation is merely the most visible pawn on that main axis.

## The China shadow: largest trade partner alongside a sovereignty dispute

The reporting puts it bluntly: although China is Vietnam's largest trading partner, the two have a sovereignty dispute in the Spratly (Nansha) Islands, and Japan and Vietnam thus share certain strategic interests. This "shadow" is the real subtext of the nuclear MoU.

Vietnam's economic dependence on China (two-way trade reached about US$200 billion in 2024) sits awkwardly with its alignment with Tokyo on the South China Sea. That tightrope — "economy leaning on China, security leaning on Japan" — is Hanoi's long-standing diplomatic norm. Nuclear cooperation will not rewrite that norm; it will only make it more visible for the foreseeable future.

## The real hurdles: cost, time and public acceptance

Turning the vision into electrons is a long road. A post-2040 timeline means at least a decade-plus of licensing, siting, construction and public consultation; Vietnamese public memory of nuclear power still lingers on the abrupt 2016 halt; and SMRs have not even cleared commercialization at home in Japan, let alone for export.

More practically, the same talks also signed a loan for a gas-steam combined cycle (GTCC) plant and pushed forward LNG projects — compared with the distant reactor, these fossil sources are Vietnam's real power backbone for the next decade. The return of nuclear power is destined to be a slow variable, not a timely remedy.

## Further reading

**Small Modular Reactor (SMR):** reactors typically under 300 MW that can be factory-built and deployed modularly, promoted as a safer and more flexible path to a nuclear revival.

**Free and Open Indo-Pacific (FOIP):** Japan's regional order vision stressing freedom of navigation and the rule of law, echoed by Vietnam on South China Sea issues.

**Official Security Assistance (OSA):** Japan's 2023 framework to provide defence equipment and infrastructure to like-minded partners, already covering the Philippines and Malaysia.
