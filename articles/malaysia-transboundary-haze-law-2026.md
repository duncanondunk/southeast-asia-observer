# 借鉴新加坡立法：马来西亚拟向跨境烟霾"动真格"

9月21日，吉隆坡的天空再度灰蒙蒙一片，法新社的镜头里，地标建筑在烟霾中若隐若现。马来西亚天然资源及环境永续部近日证实，正研究制定一部专门应对跨境烟霾的法律，并明确表态要"参考新加坡采取的措施"。这不是马来西亚第一次动念头，却可能是离落地最近的一次。

## 烟霾又来了：吉隆坡的灰幕

马来西亚与新加坡，是印度尼西亚野火所产生的跨境烟霾最主要的受害国。今年印尼再度爆发严重的森林与泥炭地火灾，火点逼近新建首都努山塔拉，两国已连续数个星期被烟霾笼罩。据印尼官方数据，过火面积已超过二十万公顷——这已不是偶然的天气现象，而是每年旱季准时上演的区域性公害。

说到底，烟霾从来不是"天灾"。它是苏门答腊与加里曼丹油棕和纸浆种植园在旱季放火清地、火势蔓延至泥炭层的产物，随风飘过国界，年复一年地敲打邻国的肺。问题是：东盟国家喊了快三十年"共同的天空"，可这片天空，至今没有一道真正管用的"共同法律"。

## 新加坡的"作业"：2014年那部法

马来西亚环境部环境管理组秘书普巴迪在9月24日晚的马新社节目《议论空间》中说得很直白：环境部长亚瑟古律已指示成立跨部门特别委员会，研究加强应对跨境烟霾的对策，"我们正在研究新加坡现有的机制，以制定专门应对烟霾问题的法令。不过，要制定这样的法令并非易事。"

新加坡的"机制"指的就是2014年通过的《跨境烟霾污染法令》。这部法律的厉害之处在于它的域外效力：只要某家企业的行为导致新加坡本地空气污染，无论火点是否在新加坡境内，新加坡都能追究其责任。过去十年，新加坡据此对多家印尼油棕与纸浆企业发出制止令，并要求其披露供应链信息——这是整个东南亚唯一一部对跨境烟霾拥有"牙齿"的硬法。

## 十年三停：马来西亚为何迟迟不敢立法

马来西亚并非没想过抄这份"作业"。事实上，它多次探讨类似立法，却在2023年底突然停止起草跨境烟霾法案。当时的官方理由，是执行上存在困难。

可困难到底在哪？说穿了，不是技术，而是政治。马来西亚和印尼同属东盟，彼此有大量相互投资与人员往来；一旦马来西亚的法律把手伸到印尼境内的企业，等于公开质疑邻国的治理能力与司法管辖权。在"不干涉内政"被奉为区域交往金科玉律的东南亚，这种域外追责几乎等同于外交冒犯。于是，立法一事一拖再拖——不是不能，是不敢。

## 主权与机密的绳索：搜证难的真相

普巴迪提到的"并非易事"，点破的正是搜证环节。要证明某起境外火灾与某家企业的清地行为有关，必须调取火源国的土地权属、企业许可与卫星监测数据；这些信息往往被当事国视为主权与商业机密。马来西亚政府当年也承认，相关立法"在执行时有困难，尤其是搜证时可能触及邻国主权和机密，恐影响双边关系"，但强调"对相关立法仍持开放态度"。

这里还有一层少有人提的尴尬：马来西亚本国也有企业在印尼经营种植园。若域外追责真成常态，吉隆坡未必只是原告，也可能成为被告。法律的绳索一旦套上别国的脖子，回头也会勒到自己。

## 科学外交与永续烟霾基金：老难题的新解法

除了立法，马来西亚这次在巴厘岛第27次亚细安跨境烟霾污染技术工作组会议上，还抛出了"科学外交"与"永续烟霾基金"两套思路。据普巴迪透露，设立永续烟霾基金的建议已获印尼、新加坡、文莱、泰国与东帝汶五国支持，目标是确保本区域有充足资金采购应对野火与烟霾所需的设备物资；该议题将交由亚细安秘书处研究，并计划提交12月在越南举行的亚细安系列会议，供全部十一个成员国深入讨论。

马来西亚还提议建立更开放、透明的数据共享机制，实时互通烟霾与露天焚烧信息，让各国更快采取灭火与应对行动。

基金与数据共享当然必要，但它们更像是"补丁"，而非"解药"。真正决定烟霾能否终结的，仍是印尼境内的地面执法与对涉事企业的追责——而那恰恰是东盟最不愿触碰的软肋。把希望寄托在资金和透明度上，固然体面，却可能再次绕开了最难的问题。

## 季风将至：窗口期与治理短板

马来西亚环境局执法组主任罗斯里祖提醒，当局会持续加强露天焚烧执法——环境局每年接获的污染投诉中，近八成与露天焚烧有关。他还预计，10月中转入东北季风后，跨境烟霾影响可能随风向改变而减弱，因此眼下更有必要加强监测，确保马来西亚本国火灾不会在接下来成为烟霾的源头。

这最后一点，恰恰点中了常被忽视的真相：马来西亚自己也不是无辜的旁观者。当八成污染投诉源于本土露天焚烧，光指着印尼的火点说事，多少有些站着说话不腰疼。要真正治理烟霾，吉隆坡既要敢抄新加坡的"硬法"作业，也得把自己的后院收拾干净。

## 延伸阅读

**跨境烟霾污染（Transboundary Haze Pollution）**：指一国境内露天焚烧或林火产生的颗粒物随风跨境扩散、使他国空气质量恶化的现象；东南亚每年旱季由印尼苏门答腊与加里曼丹泥炭地火点引发，1997年曾笼罩大半个区域。

**《跨境烟霾污染法令》（Transboundary Haze Pollution Act）**：新加坡2014年通过、具域外效力的法律，可追究导致新加坡空气污染的境外企业责任，被视为区域治理的"硬法"标杆，却因触及主权长期未被邻国效仿。

**亚细安跨境烟霾污染协定（ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution）**：2002年签署的软法框架，以"不干涉内政"与自愿合作为基调，因缺乏强制追责而执行乏力，是烟霾年年重演的制度根源之一。

# Borrowing Singapore's Law: Why Malaysia Is Finally Tackling Transboundary Haze

On September 21, Kuala Lumpur's skyline disappeared behind a grey shroud. In photographs carried by Agence France-Presse, the city's landmarks loomed faintly through the haze. This week Malaysia's Ministry of Natural Resources and Environmental Sustainability confirmed it is studying a dedicated law against transboundary haze and, tellingly, said it would "reference the measures adopted by Singapore." Malaysia has flirted with this idea before; this time, it may be closer to landing than ever.

## The haze is back: Kuala Lumpur under a grey shroud

Malaysia and Singapore are the two countries most exposed to the transboundary haze drifting from Indonesia's wildfires. This year Indonesia's forest and peatland fires flared again violently, with hotspots approaching the new capital Nusantara, and both neighbours have been blanketed for weeks. Official Indonesian figures put the burned area at over 200,000 hectares. This is no freak weather event; it is a seasonal regional public nuisance, on schedule.

To be blunt, haze was never a natural disaster. It is the product of plantations in Sumatra and Kalimantan clearing land with fire during the dry season, the flames spreading into carbon-rich peat, and the smoke riding the wind across borders to choke neighbours' lungs year after year. The puzzle is simple: ASEAN has invoked a "shared sky" for nearly three decades, yet that sky still has no law that actually binds.

## Singapore's homework: the 2014 law

Pubadi, secretary of the Environment Management Division at Malaysia's environment ministry, was candid on the Bernama programme "Ruang Wacana" on the evening of September 24: Environment Minister Nik Nazmi has instructed a cross-agency committee to study stronger responses, and "we are studying Singapore's existing mechanism to formulate a law specifically addressing haze. But formulating such a law is not easy."

Singapore's "mechanism" is its Transboundary Haze Pollution Act of 2014. Its bite lies in extraterritorial reach: any company whose conduct causes air pollution in Singapore can be held liable, regardless of where the fire burns. Over the past decade Singapore has used it to issue stop-work orders against Indonesian palm-oil and pulp companies and to demand disclosure of their supply chains — the only hard law in Southeast Asia that gives transboundary haze real teeth.

## Three stops in a decade: why Malaysia hesitated

Malaysia has considered copying that homework before. It explored similar legislation repeatedly, then abruptly halted drafting its transboundary haze bill at the end of 2023, citing practical difficulties.

But where exactly is the difficulty? Stripped bare, it is not technical — it is political. Malaysia and Indonesia are both ASEAN members with deep mutual investment and people-to-people ties; once Malaysian law reaches into Indonesian companies, it publicly questions a neighbour's governance and jurisdiction. In a region where non-interference is gospel, such extraterritorial accountability borders on diplomatic offence. So the bill stalled — not because it could not be written, but because it dared not be.

## The rope of sovereignty and secrecy: the real difficulty of evidence

What Pubadi called "not easy" exposes the evidence problem. Proving a foreign fire traces to a company's land-clearing requires land titles, permits and satellite data from the source country — information routinely treated as sovereignty and commercial secrets. Malaysia's government admitted as much in 2023, noting the law "would be difficult to enforce, especially as evidence-gathering might touch neighbouring countries' sovereignty and confidentiality and affect bilateral relations," while stressing it "remains open" to such legislation.

There is a further awkwardness few mention: Malaysian firms also operate plantations in Indonesia. If extraterritorial liability became the norm, Kuala Lumpur would not always be the plaintiff — it could be the defendant. The rope of law, once looped around a neighbour's neck, can tighten on one's own.

## Scientific diplomacy and a sustainable haze fund: an old problem, a new fix

Beyond legislation, Malaysia floated two more ideas at the 27th ASEAN Transboundary Haze Pollution Technical Working Group meeting in Bali: "scientific diplomacy" and a "sustainable haze fund." According to Pubadi, the fund proposal — meant to secure regional financing for equipment and supplies against wildfires and haze — won backing from five countries: Indonesia, Singapore, Brunei, Thailand and Timor-Leste. The matter goes to the ASEAN Secretariat and is slated for the ASEAN meetings in Vietnam this December, where all eleven members can debate it in depth.

Malaysia also proposed a more open, transparent mechanism to share real-time data on haze and open burning, so countries can respond faster.

Funds and data-sharing are necessary, but they are patches, not cure. What ultimately decides whether haze ends is ground-level enforcement in Indonesia and accountability for the companies involved — precisely the soft spot ASEAN least wants to touch. Pinning hope on money and transparency is civil, but it may again dodge the hardest question.

## The monsoon is coming: the window and the gaps

Rosli Zu, director of the environment department's enforcement group, warned that authorities will keep tightening action against open burning — nearly 80% of the pollution complaints the department receives each year relate to it. He expects the transboundary impact to ease after the northeast monsoon arrives around mid-October and shifts the wind, which makes sharpening domestic monitoring now all the more urgent, so Malaysian fires do not become the next source.

That last point hits an often-ignored truth: Malaysia is not an innocent bystander either. When four in five pollution complaints stem from local open burning, pointing only at Indonesia's hotspots rings a little hollow. To truly govern haze, Kuala Lumpur must dare to copy Singapore's hard-law homework — and also clean up its own backyard.

## Further reading

**Transboundary haze pollution**: a phenomenon in which particulates from open burning or wildfires in one country drift across borders on the wind and degrade another country's air quality; in Southeast Asia it recurs each dry season from peatland hotspots in Sumatra and Kalimantan and in 1997 blanketed much of the region.

**Transboundary Haze Pollution Act (Singapore, 2014)**: a law with extraterritorial effect that holds foreign companies liable for air pollution in Singapore; seen as the region's benchmark "hard law" yet long unimitated by neighbours because it touches sovereignty.

**ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution (2002)**: a soft-law framework built on non-interference and voluntary cooperation; its lack of compulsory enforcement makes it weak, and is one institutional root of haze's annual return.
