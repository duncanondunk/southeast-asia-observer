# 马来西亚重启舰载反舰导弹采购：挪威毁约后的马六甲安全账

马来西亚与挪威一笔价值28亿美元（约201.6亿元人民币）的舰载反舰导弹系统军购协议，今年早些时候因奥斯陆修订出口管制法规而告吹。国防部长莫哈末卡立10月3日在柔佛州哥打丁宜证实，政府正评估来自三个国家的替代方案，拟另拨7亿至10亿令吉（约11.2亿至16亿元人民币）重新采购。一桩看似技术性的军购纠纷，实则撕开了东南亚中等国家在大国军备供应体系中的脆弱位置。

## 背景：挪威毁约留下的防空缺口

马来西亚皇家海军的濒海战斗舰（Littoral Combat Ship，简称LCS）项目本应搭载挪威提供的舰载反舰导弹系统，如今协议破裂，已下水或建造中的舰体顿时面临"有舰无弹"的尴尬。挪威方面以修订出口管制法规、限制向非最紧密伙伴出售部分装备为由，终止交付导弹及发射器组件。首相安华为此严厉谴责，警告此举将严重损害西方防务供应商的信誉，并指示军方停止向挪威采购弹药及防务资产，还向厂商发出索赔通知，追讨超过10亿令吉（约16亿元人民币）的损失。

## 数字：7亿至10亿令吉的替代账单

替代方案并非小数目。马来西亚拟拨出7亿至10亿令吉（约11.2亿至16亿元人民币）为新舰采购一套全新舰载反舰导弹系统。莫哈末卡立坦言，导弹系统"不是在展示厅买车"，从下订到交付往往要三四年，因此必须尽早决策，而不能干等挪威退款。这意味着即便索赔成功，马方也要先垫付一笔新预算，时间与经济成本双双叠加。

## 兼容困局：法国战斗管理系统成关键变量

更复杂的是兼容性问题。舰上现有的战斗管理系统由一家法国公司供应，新导弹能否与这套系统顺畅对接，是评估替代方案时的关键。换言之，马来西亚的采购空间已被前期装备锁定：即便有国家愿意低价供货，若无法接入既有的指挥控制链路，也只是"装上去打不响"。这恰是中等国家军备"路径依赖"的生动写照——早年分散采购留下的拼盘，今天反过来卡住了自主选择的余地。

## 美国递来橄榄枝：替代方案的权力逻辑

今年5月，美国方面表示愿意为马来西亚的濒海战斗舰计划提供替代反舰导弹系统。华盛顿的表态并非单纯的商业善意，而是借挪威退场之机，把自身防务产品推进吉隆坡的采购清单。对马来西亚而言，这既是止损，也是新的依赖：在大国博弈的棋盘上，军备供应从来不只是买卖，而是将买方逐步嵌入卖方的安全与经济网络。安华政府口中的"信誉受损"，背后正是西方防务体系信任松动的征兆。

## 马六甲咽喉：海军现代化与海上安全焦虑

把视野拉回地缘：马来西亚扼守马六甲海峡这一全球航运咽喉，海上安全直接牵动能源与贸易命脉。濒海战斗舰本是其海军现代化、守护航道的核心资产，如今"有舰无弹"暴露的不仅是装备缺口，更是战略层面的被动。在南海周边声索方军事活动频密、海上摩擦常态化的背景下，马来西亚亟需补齐近海防御短板，却偏偏在关键节点被供应国"卡脖子"，足见中小国家军备自主之难。

## 评析：西方信誉受损与东南亚防务自主的悖论

这桩纠纷给东南亚上了一课：把国家安全寄托于远方的防务供应商，随时可能因对方的国内法规或地缘取舍而落空。挪威的退出让"西方可靠"叙事出现裂缝，而美国顺势补位，又让依赖从一端转向另一端。对吉隆坡来说，真正的出路或许不在于换一个卖家，而在于借区域合作与国产能力，逐步把安全主动权握回自己手里。马六甲的风浪不会等人，采购清单上的每一行，写下的都是中等国家的安全焦虑。

## 延伸阅读

**出口管制（Export Control）**：供应国以国内法规限制敏感装备外售的机制，本案例中挪威据此终止对马交付，凸显军备贸易的政治属性。

**濒海战斗舰（Littoral Combat Ship）**：马来西亚皇家海军主力水面舰艇建造计划，本应集成舰载反舰导弹系统，因供应中断陷入"有舰无弹"。

**马六甲海峡（Strait of Malacca）**：扼守印度洋与太平洋航运要冲，是全球最繁忙水道之一，其安全态势直接关系马来西亚与区域国家的经济命脉。

# Malaysia Reopens Naval Missile Procurement After Norway's Cancellation: The Security Calculus of the Malacca Strait

A US$2.8 billion (about RMB 20.2 billion) contract for a shipborne anti-ship missile system, signed between Malaysia and Norway, collapsed earlier this year after Oslo revised its export-control legislation. On 3 October, Defence Minister Mohammed Khaled confirmed in Kota Tinggi, Johor, that the government is weighing alternative proposals from three countries and may set aside a fresh 700 million–1 billion ringgit (about S$219 million–S$313 million, roughly US$155 million–US$222 million) to restart procurement. What looks on the surface like a technical procurement dispute in fact exposes the structural vulnerability of a mid-sized Southeast Asian state inside the great-power arms-supply system.

## The backdrop: Oslo's cancellation leaves a gap

The Royal Malaysian Navy's Littoral Combat Ship (LCS) programme was meant to carry the Norwegian-supplied shipborne anti-ship missile system. With the agreement torn up, hulls already launched or under construction now face the awkward reality of "ships without missiles." Norway terminated delivery of the missiles and launcher components, citing revised export-control rules that restrict the sale of certain equipment to partners outside its closest circle. Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim responded with a sharp condemnation, warning that the move would seriously damage the credibility of Western defence suppliers. Kuala Lumpur instructed the military to stop buying Norwegian ammunition and defence assets, and served a claim notice on the manufacturer seeking more than 1 billion ringgit (about RMB 1.6 billion, roughly US$222 million) in losses.

## The numbers: a 700 million–1 billion ringgit replacement bill

The replacement will not come cheap. Malaysia plans to allocate 700 million–1 billion ringgit (about S$219 million–S$313 million, roughly US$155 million–US$222 million) for an entirely new shipborne anti-ship missile system for the new frigates. Mohammed Khaled was candid that a missile system is "not a car bought from a showroom": from order to delivery typically takes three to four years, so a decision cannot wait until Norway refunds the money. This means that even if the claim succeeds, Kuala Lumpur must front a new budget first, stacking time and economic costs on top of each other.

## The compatibility puzzle: the French combat-management system

The complication runs deeper than money. The ship's existing combat-management system is supplied by a French firm, and whether a new missile can integrate smoothly with that system is the decisive factor in evaluating alternatives. In other words, Malaysia's procurement space is already locked in by earlier equipment choices: even if a country offers a low price, a weapon that cannot plug into the existing command-and-control chain is merely "mounted but unable to fire." This is a vivid illustration of the "path dependence" of mid-sized states' arms purchases — a patchwork assembled years earlier now reaches back to constrain today's freedom of choice.

## Washington's olive branch: the power logic of alternatives

In May, the United States indicated its willingness to provide an alternative anti-ship missile system for Malaysia's LCS programme. Washington's gesture is hardly pure commercial goodwill; it is an opportunity created by Norway's exit to push its own defence products onto Kuala Lumpur's shopping list. For Malaysia, this is both damage control and a new dependency: on the chessboard of great-power competition, arms supply was never merely a transaction but a mechanism for gradually embedding the buyer into the seller's security and economic networks. The "damaged credibility" Anwar speaks of is, in truth, a symptom of fraying trust in the Western defence architecture.

## The Malacca chokepoint: naval modernisation and maritime anxiety

Zoom out to geography. Malaysia guards the Strait of Malacca, a global shipping chokepoint whose security bears directly on energy and trade lifelines. The LCS was meant to be the centrepiece of naval modernisation and sea-lane protection, yet the "ships without missiles" predicament exposes not just an equipment gap but a strategic passivity. As military activity by claimants around the South China Sea intensifies and maritime friction becomes routine, Malaysia urgently needs to close its coastal-defence shortfall — only to be "strangled" by a supplier at the precise moment it matters most, underscoring how hard defence autonomy is for smaller states.

## Assessment: damaged Western credibility and the paradox of defence autonomy

The dispute is a lesson for Southeast Asia: entrusting national security to distant defence suppliers can fail at any time over the supplier's own domestic law or geopolitical calculus. Norway's withdrawal opened a crack in the "the West is reliable" narrative, while America's swift fill-in merely shifts the dependency from one end to the other. For Kuala Lumpur, the real way out may lie not in swapping one seller for another but in gradually reclaiming the initiative through regional cooperation and indigenous capability. The strait's waters will not wait; every line on the procurement list is, in the end, a mid-sized state's written-down security anxiety.

## Further reading

**Export Control**: A mechanism by which supplier states restrict the export of sensitive equipment through domestic law. In this case Norway invoked it to halt delivery to Malaysia, exposing the inherently political character of the arms trade.

**Littoral Combat Ship (LCS)**: The Royal Malaysian Navy's principal surface-combatant build programme, intended to integrate the shipborne anti-ship missile system but left "hulls without missiles" by the supply interruption.

**Strait of Malacca**: The maritime chokepoint between the Indian and Pacific oceans and one of the world's busiest waterways; its security situation bears directly on the economic lifelines of Malaysia and the wider region.
