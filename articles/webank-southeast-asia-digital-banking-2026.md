# 微众出海：当中国金融科技开始给东南亚"卖水"

据《南华早报》（South China Morning Post）11月2日报道，总部位于香港的微众银行（WeBank）科技服务公司——腾讯（Tencent）参股的这家中国互联网银行旗下的金融科技单元——去年在东南亚录得数千万美元营收（约合数亿元人民币），增长来自当地对数字银行基础设施的需求上升。公司高管称，东南亚（Southeast Asia）在技术与服务的供需之间有一道"巨大的鸿沟"，正推动其在印度尼西亚（Indonesia）、马来西亚（Malaysia）和泰国（Thailand）快速增长。

这则不大起眼的商业消息，其实戳中了中国金融科技"出海"的一个关键转向：从过去在海外直接放贷、做消费金融，转向给当地银行"卖基建"——卖系统、卖风控、卖人工智能（AI）能力。换句话说，微众不想再当那个亲手下场的"放贷者"，而想做站在所有银行身后的"卖水人"。

## 从"放贷者"到"卖水人"：微众的出海逻辑

中国金融科技出海，并不新鲜。早几年，无论是 Grab 旗下的 Atome，还是 Sea 旗下的 SeaMoney，走的都是"持牌消费金融 + 先买后付"的路子：自己进场借钱给当地人。这条路增长快，争议也大——高利率、过度授信、债务陷阱的批评从未停过。

微众的路径不同。它并没有在东南亚申请一张张消费金融牌照去直接放贷，而是把在中国市场打磨了十余年的分布式核心系统、线上信贷引擎和人工智能风控，打包成"数字银行基础设施"，卖给或赋能当地的金融机构。在泰国，它与当地 SCB X 金融集团、韩国的 KakaoBank 组成三方联盟，共建一家虚拟银行（virtual bank）；在印尼和马来西亚，它更多以技术输出的方式切入。

这一招更聪明，也更稳。自己放贷，要扛坏账、扛监管、扛舆论压力；卖基建，则是收"过路费"——风险留在当地银行的表上，微众赚的是技术和服务费。在各国对跨境消费金融日益警惕的当下，"卖水"比"下水"安全得多。

## 数字：被长期忽视的"金融洼地"

微众看中的，是东南亚（Southeast Asia）那道长期存在的"金融鸿沟"。这里人口超过6.8亿，却仍有数以亿计的成年人没有银行账户或得不到充分服务；传统银行依赖物理网点，成本高、覆盖稀，大量小微企业和低收入群体被挡在门外。

这道鸿沟的另一面，是巨大的需求。据行业估算，东南亚数字银行与移动金融服务的总规模正以每年两位数的速度扩张，年轻人口、高手机渗透率与滞后的传统金融供给之间，形成了肉眼可见的落差。微众高管口中的"巨大的鸿沟"，说的正是供给和需求之间这道还没被填平的缝。

腾讯（Tencent）系的微众在中国市场已是服务数亿用户、数千万小微企业的数字银行标杆，如今把它在国内跑通的"低成本、广覆盖、AI 驱动"的打法搬到东南亚，本质上是把一套已经验证过的产能，输出到一片需求旺盛却供给不足的市场。

## 泰国样板：中韩泰三方合建虚拟银行

最有看头的，是泰国的那桩合作。2026年1月，泰国 SCB X 金融集团与韩国 KakaoBank、微众银行科技服务公司三方签署协议，要在泰国推一家全数字化的虚拟银行。SCB X 出本地市场经验和运营能力，KakaoBank 出"手机优先"数字银行的实战经验，微众出底层数字银行基础设施与人工智能能力。

泰国监管层正在推进虚拟银行牌照框架，目标正是提升金融普惠（financial inclusion）、激活市场创新。这个三方联盟把"本地经验 + 韩国产品经验 + 中国技术底座"捏在一起，意图很明显：用一家从一开始就内嵌人工智能的"AI 原生银行"，去补上泰国长期存在的金融服务可及性缺口。

值得注意的是，微众在这里的角色是"技术底座"而非"品牌前台"。它不背负获客与坏账的直接压力，却把自家的系统、模型和人工智能嵌进了泰国未来数字银行的血管里。这种"借船出海"，比单枪匹马去拿牌照要省力，也更难被贴上"外来掠夺者"的标签。

## 普惠叙事下的隐忧：数据主权与技术依赖

当然，普惠金融（financial inclusion）的叙事之下，藏着两组值得警惕的问题。

第一组是数据主权。当一国的核心银行系统、风控模型和人工智能越来越依赖某一家外国技术供应商，金融数据的流向、模型的解释权、系统的自主可控性，都会成为绕不开的命题。对东南亚（Southeast Asia）国家而言，金融基础设施的"自主"与"安全"，从来不只是商业问题。

第二组是技术依赖与锁定效应。微众的优势在于成熟、便宜、可快速部署；但一旦当地银行深度绑定这套中国技术栈，后续的迁移成本、议价能力、乃至地缘波动时的系统韧性，都要打问号。历史上，从支付到云再到电信，技术依赖如何反噬主权，东南亚并不陌生。

这并不是说微众的进入是坏事——竞争能压低价格、提升服务；但把"卖水人"请进门的同时，东道国也该想清楚：水管在自己手里，还是在水厂手里。

## 不只是生意：金融科技的"出海"逻辑

把视野拉高一层，微众在东南亚的落子，是中国金融科技"出海"逻辑的一次缩影。过去十年，中国互联网公司把电商、支付、短视频的模式输出到东南亚；如今，连最敏感的金融基础设施，也开始以"技术赋能"的面孔南下。

这背后有两股推力：对内，国内金融科技市场趋于饱和、监管趋严，增长见顶的玩家必须向外找增量；对外，东南亚（Southeast Asia）的数字化进程方兴未艾，又恰好处在"中国模式"与"西方模式"之间左右观望的中间地带。微众这类"卖基建"的打法，恰好卡在这个缝隙里——它比直接放贷更低调，比纯硬件出口更贴近数据，是一种更柔软、也更持久的影响力投射。

对东南亚而言，这既是补齐金融短板的机遇，也是一道治理考题：如何在借力外来技术实现普惠的同时，守住数据主权、避免技术锁定。这道题，不会因为一家银行的进入而有标准答案，却会随每一笔数字交易的完成而愈发紧迫。

## 延伸阅读

**虚拟银行（virtual bank）**：不设实体网点、主要通过互联网和手机提供零售银行服务的持牌机构，近年在新加坡、马来西亚、中国香港等地获发牌照，泰国也在推进相关框架，目标多为提升金融普惠。

**普惠金融（financial inclusion）**：以可负担的成本，向此前被传统金融排除在外的低收入群体、小微企业和偏远地区提供支付、信贷、保险等基础金融服务。

**金融科技（fintech）**：运用人工智能、大数据、分布式系统等新技术重构支付、信贷、风控等金融服务的产业形态，是中国企业近年来"出海"东南亚的重点赛道之一。

# WeBank goes south: when Chinese fintech starts selling water in Southeast Asia

According to the South China Morning Post on 2 November, Hong Kong-based WeBank Technology Services — the fintech arm of Tencent-backed Chinese online lender WeBank — recorded tens of millions of dollars in revenue last year from rising demand for digital banking infrastructure across Southeast Asia. A senior executive said there is a "huge gap" between the supply of and demand for technology in the region, driving rapid growth in Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand.

This modest business item actually captures a key pivot in how Chinese fintech is going global: away from directly lending and running consumer finance overseas, and toward selling "infrastructure" to local banks — systems, risk controls, artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities. In other words, WeBank no longer wants to be the lender in the arena; it wants to be the "water seller" standing behind every bank.

## From lender to "water seller": WeBank's going-out logic

Chinese fintech going abroad is hardly new. A few years back, whether Grab's Atome or Sea's SeaMoney, the playbook was "licensed consumer finance plus buy-now-pay-later": show up yourself and lend to locals. That route grows fast but draws no less controversy — criticism over high interest rates, over-indebtedness and debt traps has never stopped.

WeBank's path is different. Rather than applying for consumer-finance licences country by country to lend directly, it packages the distributed core system, online credit engine and AI risk control honed in China over more than a decade into "digital banking infrastructure" and sells or enables it for local financial institutions. In Thailand it forms a three-way alliance with local group SCB X and South Korea's KakaoBank to build a virtual bank; in Indonesia and Malaysia it enters more through technology export.

This move is smarter and steadier. Lend yourself, and you carry the bad loans, the regulation, the reputational risk. Sell infrastructure, and you collect "toll fees" — the risk stays on the local bank's balance sheet while WeBank earns technology and service fees. At a time when countries are increasingly wary of cross-border consumer finance, "selling water" is far safer than "getting into the water."

## The numbers: a long-ignored "financial lowlands"

What WeBank is eyeing is the long-standing "financial gap" in Southeast Asia. The region is home to more than 680 million people, yet hundreds of millions of adults remain unbanked or underserved; traditional banks rely on physical branches that are costly and thinly spread, leaving vast numbers of micro-enterprises and low-income groups outside the door.

On the other side of that gap lies enormous demand. Industry estimates put the total size of Southeast Asia's digital-banking and mobile-financial services expanding at double-digit annual rates, with a visible gulf between a young population and high phone penetration on one side and lagging traditional financial supply on the other. The "huge gap" WeBank's executive cites is precisely the seam between supply and demand that has yet to be filled.

Tencent's WeBank is already a digital-banking benchmark in China, serving hundreds of millions of users and tens of millions of micro-enterprises. Porting the "low-cost, wide-coverage, AI-driven" formula proven at home to a market that is hungry for supply but short on it is, at bottom, exporting a validated capacity to where it is most needed.

## The Thai template: a China–Korea–Thailand virtual bank

The most interesting piece is the Thai deal. In January 2026, Thailand's SCB X financial group signed with South Korea's KakaoBank and WeBank Technology Services to launch a fully digital virtual bank in Thailand. SCB X contributes local-market expertise and operating scale; KakaoBank brings its track record running a mobile-first digital bank; WeBank supplies the underlying digital-banking infrastructure and AI capabilities.

Thai regulators are advancing a virtual-bank licensing framework, aimed precisely at lifting financial inclusion and energising market innovation. The three-way alliance fuses "local know-how + Korean product experience + Chinese technology base" with a clear intent: use an "AI-native bank" embedded with artificial intelligence from day one to close Thailand's long-standing financial-access gap.

Notably, WeBank's role here is "technology base," not "brand front." It does not bear the direct pressure of customer acquisition or bad loans, yet it has woven its systems, models and AI into the veins of Thailand's future digital bank. This "borrowing a boat to go to sea" is easier than going solo for a licence, and far harder to label as a "foreign predator."

## Worries beneath the inclusion narrative: data sovereignty and dependency

Of course, beneath the financial-inclusion narrative sit two sets of concerns worth vigilance.

The first is data sovereignty. When a country's core banking system, risk models and AI grow dependent on a single foreign technology supplier, the flow of financial data, the interpretability of models and the autonomy and controllability of the system all become unavoidable questions. For Southeast Asian countries, the "autonomy" and "security" of financial infrastructure have never been merely commercial matters.

The second is technological dependency and lock-in. WeBank's edge is maturity, cheapness and fast deployment; but once local banks are deeply bound to this Chinese tech stack, the subsequent migration cost, bargaining power, and even system resilience under geopolitical swings all come into question. From payments to cloud to telecom, Southeast Asia is hardly a stranger to how technological dependency can boomerang on sovereignty.

None of this means WeBank's entry is bad — competition presses prices down and services up; but while inviting the "water seller" in, host countries should also ask plainly: are the pipes in their own hands, or in the factory's?

## More than business: the logic of fintech going out

Zoom out, and WeBank's Southeast Asian moves are a microcosm of how Chinese fintech is going global. Over the past decade, Chinese internet firms exported e-commerce, payments and short-video models to Southeast Asia; today even the most sensitive financial infrastructure is heading south under the face of "technology empowerment."

Two forces drive this. Domestically, China's fintech market is saturating and tightening under regulation; players that have hit a growth ceiling must look outward for increment. Externally, Southeast Asia's digitalisation is still in its early innings and sits precisely in the middle ground between "Chinese" and "Western" models, watching both ways. A "sell-infrastructure" approach like WeBank's slots neatly into that crevice — lower-profile than direct lending, closer to data than pure hardware export, a softer and more durable form of influence projection.

For Southeast Asia, this is both an opportunity to close financial gaps and a governance exam: how to achieve inclusion by leveraging foreign technology while safeguarding data sovereignty and avoiding technological lock-in. That question will not be answered by the entry of one bank, yet it grows more urgent with every digital transaction completed.

## Further reading

**Virtual bank**: A licensed institution that operates without physical branches and delivers retail banking mainly over the internet and mobile phones. Such licences have been granted in Singapore, Malaysia and Hong Kong, and Thailand is advancing its own framework, mostly aimed at boosting financial inclusion.

**Financial inclusion**: Providing previously excluded low-income groups, micro-enterprises and remote areas with basic financial services — payments, credit, insurance — at an affordable cost.

**Fintech**: An industry that uses new technologies such as artificial intelligence, big data and distributed systems to rebuild payments, credit and risk control; a key track for Chinese firms expanding into Southeast Asia in recent years.
