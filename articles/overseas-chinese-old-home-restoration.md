# 海外侨胞助老侨宅焕新颜 留住“看得见的乡愁”

侨宅是海外侨胞乡愁所系。近年来，一些旅居比利时、美国、加拿大的华侨华人回乡，参与老侨宅的保护与修缮，让百年砖木建筑重新融入当下生活。一座座侨宅的活化，既是家族记忆的接力和侨乡文化的延续，也折射出“以用促保”的遗产保护新思路。

## 侨宅：一砖一瓦里的乡愁

据中国新闻社报道，长期关注历史建筑保护的比利时华侨吴宇华（Wu Yuhua）表示，侨宅的价值“不止于一砖一瓦，更在于让侨胞乡思有落脚之地”。岭南地区的侨房多为砖木结构，长期闲置后，木质构件普遍受到霉菌、白蚁侵蚀。如何让这些濒朽的老宅“活”下来，成为侨乡面临的共同课题。

## “最小干预”：让百年侨房活起来

在广东中山石岐（Shiqi）街道龙母庙街92号，吴宇华团队修缮一座百年侨房时，遵循“最小干预”原则，修复了木质楼板、楼梯与彩绘玻璃等原有构件，并采用轻量化加固工艺，使建筑重新投入使用。这种做法的要义，是在不破坏历史信息的前提下延长建筑寿命，而非推倒重建。

## 从老宅到新业态：活化不止于修缮

修缮只是第一步。吴宇华认为，长期且适度的使用才能形成良性保护机制。龙母庙街3号侨房引入古着店业态，在保留原有格局基础上增添服装展示、咖啡休闲等功能；一处七间相连的侨房，则被改造为具有南洋风情的商业综合体。在广东江门台山市（Taishan）官步村，美国华人李旻禧（Li Minxi）将侨宅“翘庐”活化成特色民宿“翘庐1922”，既还原雕花砖瓦，又融入开平（Kaiping）腐乳、新会（Xinhui）陈皮等侨乡风味，推出腐乳咖啡、陈皮拿铁。一位印尼老华侨到访后，萌生了回乡改造祖屋的念头。

## 跨代接力：侨宅里的家族记忆

在福建晋江（Jinjiang）灵源街道灵水社区，由吴垂奎（Wu Chuikui）大厝及话桑别墅（Huasang Villa）改造而成的话桑文化馆，由吴垂奎的孙女、移居加拿大的吴淑惠（Wu Shuhui）全程参与修缮。从墙体修复到展陈设计，她将收藏的邮票陈列其中，把祖宅打造成集文化展览与家风展示于一体的公共空间。这座1936年兴建的祖宅，承载着一个家族跨海谋生又归乡守护的记忆。

一座侨宅的修复，留住的不只是建筑，更是“看得见”的乡愁。当侨胞乡思有了落脚之地，侨乡文化也随之在代际之间悄然延续。

## 延伸阅读

**侨乡（Qiaoxiang）**：特指近代以来大批居民出国谋生、在海外形成庞大华侨华人族群的中国沿海县乡，以广东、福建最为典型，是侨宅、侨汇与侨批文化的原生地。

**最小干预原则（Principle of Minimal Intervention）**：遗产保护中主张在保留建筑原有历史信息与材质的前提下进行必要修缮，避免过度修复破坏真实性的准则。

**活化利用（Adaptive Reuse）**：在保护历史建筑价值的基础上，赋予其符合当代需求的新功能，如民宿、展馆、商业空间，以使用促保护。

# Overseas Chinese Breathe New Life into Old Emigrant Homes, Preserving a “Visible Nostalgia”

Qiaozhai — the homes left behind by emigrants — anchor the nostalgia of overseas Chinese. In recent years, ethnic Chinese living in Belgium, the United States and Canada have returned to their ancestral towns to help protect and restore such buildings, reintegrating century-old brick-and-timber houses into present-day life. The revitalisation of these homes is at once a relay of family memory and a continuation of qiaoxiang culture, and it also reflects a new “conservation through use” approach to heritage.

## Qiaozhai: Nostalgia Encoded in Brick and Tile

According to China News Service, Wu Yuhua, a Belgian overseas Chinese devoted to historic-building conservation, argues that the value of a qiaozhai “lies not only in its bricks and tiles, but in giving the emigrants’ homesickness a place to land.” In southern Lingnan, most qiaozhai are brick-wood structures; left vacant for years, their timber components have been widely eroded by mould and termites. How to keep these decaying homes alive has become a shared challenge for emigrant hometowns.

## “Minimal Intervention”: Bringing Centennial Homes Back to Life

At No. 92 Longmu Temple Street in Shiqi, Zhongshan, Guangdong, Wu’s team followed a “minimal intervention” principle when restoring a century-old qiaozhai — repairing the original timber floors, staircase and stained glass, and applying lightweight reinforcement so the building could be used again. The essence is to extend a building’s life without destroying its historical information, rather than demolishing and rebuilding.

## From Old House to New Use: Revitalisation Beyond Repair

Repair is only the first step. Wu believes a benign preservation mechanism requires long-term, moderate use. At No. 3 Longmu Temple Street, a vintage-store format was introduced, adding garment display and café functions while keeping the original layout; a cluster of seven adjoining qiaozhai was transformed into a commercial complex with a Nanyang (Southeast Asian) flavour. In Guanbu village, Taishan, Guangdong, the American Chinese Li Minxi turned the qiaozhai “Qiaolu” into a boutique homestay, “Qiaolu 1922” — restoring carved bricks and tiles while weaving in qiaoxiang tastes such as Kaiping fermented tofu and Xinhui dried tangerine peel, even serving “fermented-tofu coffee” and “tangerine-peel latte.” An elderly Indonesian Chinese visitor was inspired to renovate his own ancestral home.

## A Cross-Generational Relay: Family Memory in the Qiaozhai

In Lingshui community, Jinjiang, Fujian, the Huasang Culture Hall was converted from the mansion of Wu Chuikui and the adjacent Huasang Villa. The restoration was driven throughout by Wu Shuhui, Wu Chuikui’s granddaughter who emigrated to Canada. From wall repair to exhibition design, she displayed her stamp collection and turned the ancestral home into a public space for cultural exhibitions and family-ethos display. Built in 1936, the house carries one family’s memory of crossing the seas to make a living and then returning to guard it.

Restoring a qiaozhai preserves more than a building; it preserves a “visible” nostalgia. When emigrants’ longing finds a place to land, qiaoxiang culture quietly continues across generations.

## Further reading

**Qiaoxiang**: A term for the coastal counties and towns of China — most typically in Guangdong and Fujian — from which large numbers emigrated, giving rise to the native culture of qiaozhai, remittances and qiaopi (migrant letters).

**Principle of Minimal Intervention**: A heritage-conservation tenet that necessary repairs should preserve a building’s original historical information and materials, avoiding over-restoration that destroys authenticity.

**Adaptive Reuse**: Giving a historic building a contemporary function — a homestay, museum or commercial space — that protects its value while keeping it in use.
