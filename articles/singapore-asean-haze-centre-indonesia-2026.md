# 新加坡要在印尼建「烟霾中心」：一纸承诺能解开东盟24年的死结吗？

## 六国门槛：一个拖了24年的中心

据《南华早报》（South China Morning Post）报道，新加坡可持续发展与环境部长傅海燕（Grace Fu）11月3日在国会答询时表示，新加坡明年接任东盟轮值主席国后，将与成员国加强《东盟跨境烟霾污染协定》（AATHP）的执行，并与东盟一道，在印尼设立「东盟跨境烟霾污染控制协调中心」。

一句话点破关键：这个中心只有在六个东盟成员国批准其成立协议后才会生效。六个，而东盟有10个成员国。换句话说，任何一个成员国的刻意拖延，都足以让中心停摆。这是最低共识设计的典型样本——它保证没人被落下，也保证没人能被真正追责。《东盟跨境烟霾污染协定》2002年首次签署，到今年已经24年，区域烟霾却依旧年年造访。一个中心的承诺，来得不早。

## 从「静默外交」到「断气」

新加坡近年来对雅加达采取的是所谓「静默外交」。但烟霾不是外交辞令能吹散的。上个月，印尼境内野火引发的区域烟霾把两国关系推到紧绷：印尼一度警告穆斯林朝圣者避免经新加坡转机，并削减了对这个城市国家的天然气供应。

把这些动作放在一起看，你会发现它们几乎与环保无关。断气是能源牌，朝圣警告是宗教与民意牌，两张牌都打在新加坡最脆弱的地方——一个几乎没有战略纵深、能源与中转高度依赖邻国的城邦。据《南华早报》报道，印尼前官员甚至在没有证据的情况下声称，新加坡主权基金介入了明晟（MSCI）对印尼股票的降级警告。点评一句：当环境议题被拿来当作外交报复的筹码，烟霾就不再是烟霾，而成了谈判桌上的筹码。

## 「我们送的是氧气」：当邻国互相嘲讽

据《南华早报》另一篇报道，面对邻国指责，印尼官员抛出「我们送的是氧气」的说法，被马来西亚网民群嘲。印尼通讯与数字事务部长穆特亚（Meutya Hafid）则呼吁各方冷静，并说了一句值得记下的话：新加坡「也面临风险」——她指的是有前新加坡议员暗示城邦可以切断邻国互联网接入的言论。

这里有一个常被忽略的事实：跨境烟霾是双向的伤害，不是单向的施舍。印尼是主要火源国，但空气没有护照，新加坡的资本与技术同样没有。双方互相放狠话，最终受损的是区域的空气、区域的信任与区域的投资环境。用嘴仗代替治理，是这条新闻里最不划算的一笔账。

## 泥炭地与刀耕火种：问题的根不在新加坡

傅海燕在国会里说得很清楚：土地与森林火灾主要由人为活动引起，例如泥炭地排水与刀耕火种式的清地。她还提到可持续的泥炭地管理与作物焚烧指引，作为从源头治理的方向。

这才是问题的硬核。泥炭地一旦排水，就变得极易燃烧，燃烧时释放的碳与烟雾远超普通森林火灾。而刀耕火种背后，是种植园经济与小农生计的博弈——谁来做、为谁做、谁买单。协调中心能改善监测与协调，却动不了土地使用的激励结构。把中心设在印尼是对的方向：排放者在源头就近管理。但如果中心只是又一个开会的地方，它改变不了火。

## 2027主席国：把议程设置权变成治理能力

新加坡明年接任东盟轮值主席国，给了它一个难得的窗口。观察东盟的人都懂一个常识：主席国并不掌握超国家权力，却掌握议程设置权——开什么会、先谈什么、把什么写进联合声明。傅海燕的表态，本质上是新加坡提前把「烟霾治理」钉在明年的议程板上。

但要警惕一种「议程治理」的错觉：把一件事写进议程，与把一件事做成，是两回事。过去24年，东盟在烟霾问题上不缺声明、不缺协议、不缺路线图，缺的是火点发生的那一刻，究竟谁有能力叫停它。新加坡能带来的是它一贯的精密治理与数据能力；它带不来的，是印尼国内的土地政治。这两者之间的落差，决定了中心的分量。

## 从街头到会场：烟霾的政治账

据报道，印尼的烟霾危机已经成为学生表达对普拉博沃（Prabowo）政府不满的集结号，印尼大学生与学生组织联盟在首都多点发起抗议。点评一句：当空气变成政治，任何技术性方案都必须先回答一个政治问题——政府究竟对谁的空气负责？

新加坡气象部门说，随着西南季风减弱、11月降雨增加，境内烟霾已明显缓解。这当然是好消息，却也暗含危险：烟霾一散，紧迫感也随之消散。本世纪的区域治理反复证明一个规律——危机到来时承诺最多，危机离开时落实最少。六个成员国的批准书，能不能在风停雨停之前凑齐，才是这张考卷真正的题目。

## 延伸阅读

**跨境烟霾（Transboundary haze）**：污染物跨越国界流动造成的空气污染，是典型的区域公地问题，根源在于泥炭地排水与刀耕火种等土地使用方式。

**东盟方式（The ASEAN Way）**：以协商、不干涉内政与最低共识为基础的区域决策模式，长于包容，短在执行力依赖最不情愿的成员。

**公地悲剧（Tragedy of the commons）**：个体各自追求短期利益、导致共享资源退化的经典困境，跨境烟霾与泥炭地治理都是现实案例。

# Singapore's haze centre in Indonesia: can one pledge unlock ASEAN's 24-year deadlock?

## The six-country threshold

According to the South China Morning Post, Singapore's Minister for Sustainability and the Environment, Grace Fu, told Parliament on November 3 that, when the city-state takes over the ASEAN chairmanship next year, it will work with member states to strengthen implementation of the ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution (AATHP) and to establish the ASEAN Coordinating Centre for Transboundary Haze Pollution Control in Indonesia.

Here is the line that matters: the centre will only come into force after six ASEAN members ratify its founding agreement. Six, out of ten. In practical terms, a single determined delay is enough to stall it. This is lowest-common-denominator design by another name — it ensures no one is left behind, and it ensures no one can be held to account. The AATHP was first signed in 2002; twenty-four years on, regional haze still arrives like clockwork. A promise to build a centre is not a moment too soon.

## From quiet diplomacy to gas cut-offs

Singapore has in recent years practised what observers call "quiet diplomacy" toward Jakarta. But haze is not dispersed by diplomatic phrasing. Last month, wildfires in Indonesia blanketed the region and pushed relations to the breaking point: Jakarta warned Muslim pilgrims against transiting Singapore and cut gas supplies to the city-state.

Read those moves together and they have almost nothing to do with environmental protection. The gas cut is an energy lever; the pilgrim warning plays the religious and public-opinion card. Both land on Singapore's softest spot — a city-state with almost no strategic depth, dependent on its neighbours for energy and transit. According to the SCMP, a former Indonesian official even claimed, without evidence, that Singapore's sovereign wealth funds played a role in MSCI's warning that it might downgrade Indonesian stocks. One line of comment is in order: when the environment becomes an instrument of diplomatic retaliation, haze is no longer haze; it is a chip on the negotiating table.

## "We send you oxygen": when neighbours trade jabs

In a separate SCMP report, Indonesian officials defended themselves against neighbours' criticism by saying they "send oxygen" — a line mercilessly mocked by Malaysian netizens. Indonesia's Communication and Digital Affairs Minister, Meutya Hafid, urged calm and made a remark worth remembering: Singapore is "also at risk" — a reference to a former Singaporean nominated member of parliament who suggested the city-state could disrupt its neighbour's internet access.

The overlooked fact is that transboundary haze is a two-way injury, not a one-way handout. Indonesia is the main source of the fires, but air carries no passport, and neither does Singapore's capital or technology. Trading threats serves only to damage the region's air, its trust and its investment climate. Substituting a war of words for governance is the worst bargain in this story.

## Peatland and slash-and-burn: the roots are far from Singapore

Fu was explicit in Parliament: land and forest fires are mainly caused by human activity, such as draining peatland and slash-and-burn land clearance. She also pointed to sustainable peatland management and guidelines on crop burning as ways to tackle the problem at source.

That is the hard core of the issue. Once peatland is drained it becomes highly flammable, and when it burns it releases far more carbon and smoke than an ordinary forest fire. Behind slash-and-burn lies a contest between plantation economics and smallholder livelihoods — who does the burning, for whom, and who pays. A coordinating centre can improve monitoring and coordination; it cannot change the incentives embedded in land use. Locating the centre in Indonesia is the right direction: the polluter manages close to the source. But if the centre becomes just another venue for meetings, it will not change the fires.

## The 2027 chairmanship: turning agenda-setting into governance

Singapore's assumption of the ASEAN chairmanship next year gives it a rare window. Those who follow ASEAN know the rule: the chair holds no supranational power, but it does hold agenda-setting power — which meetings to convene, what to discuss first, what goes into the joint communiqué. Fu's pledge is, in essence, Singapore pinning haze governance to next year's agenda board early.

Yet there is a familiar illusion here: putting an issue on the agenda is not the same as resolving it. For twenty-four years ASEAN has not lacked declarations, agreements or roadmaps on haze. What it has lacked is someone who can actually stop a fire at the moment it starts. What Singapore brings is its characteristic precision in governance and data; what it cannot bring is Indonesia's domestic land politics. The gap between the two will determine how much the centre actually weighs.

## From the street to the summit: the political ledger

According to SCMP coverage, Indonesia's haze crisis has become a rallying point for student anger at the Prabowo government, with demonstrations organised by University of Indonesia students and a coalition of campus groups in several parts of the capital. One observation follows: once air becomes political, every technical solution must first answer a political question — whose air, exactly, is the government accountable for?

Singapore's meteorological service says the haze has eased markedly as the southwest monsoon weakens and November rains arrive. That is good news, and also a danger: when the smoke clears, the urgency clears with it. Regional governance this century keeps repeating one lesson — promises are loudest when a crisis arrives and quietest when it leaves. Whether the six ratifications can be secured before the wind and rain do their work is the real examination.

## Further reading

**Transboundary haze**: air pollution carried across borders, a classic regional commons problem rooted in land-use practices such as peat drainage and slash-and-burn clearing.

**The ASEAN Way**: a regional decision-making model based on consultation, non-interference and lowest-common-denominator consensus — inclusive, but dependent for enforcement on the least willing member.

**Tragedy of the commons**: the classic dilemma in which individuals pursuing short-term gain deplete a shared resource; cross-border haze and peatland management are both real-world cases.
