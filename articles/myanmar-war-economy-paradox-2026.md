# 缅甸战时经济怪象：官方经济缩水2%，仰光房价却年年疯涨

据《日经亚洲（Nikkei Asia）》报道，缅甸截至2026年3月底的会计年度经济萎缩2%，进口管制、外汇短缺与持续内战拖累企业；但仰光房地产从业者称，2021至2026年当地房价每年上涨约40%至60%。一个经济体在官方统计里往下塌，资产价格却往上冲——这不是复苏的信号，而是战争经济的典型症候。

## 一组刺眼的反差

如果把缅甸的宏观数据摊开看，最先撞进眼里的不是增长，而是一对彼此矛盾的曲线。一边是国内生产总值（GDP）连续收缩：世界银行估算，2025/26财年（截至2026年3月）实际GDP下滑2.0%，亚洲开发银行（ADB）也确认2025年经济收缩2.2%。另一边则是房价与硬资产的逆势狂奔。仰光的地产中介告诉《日经亚洲》，政变以来的五年里，当地住宅与商业物业价格每年跳涨四成到六成。

同一片土地上，官方的“缩水”和民间的“膨胀”同时发生。这本身就说明，传统的经济指标已经无法描述缅甸正在发生的事。当一个国家的统计口径还在数工厂与农田，真正的财富却悄悄涌进了砖头和黄金，你就知道，这台机器运转的逻辑已经变了。

## 数字：官方经济在塌方

收缩是结构性的，不是周期性的。世界银行指出，地震、持续冲突与长期结构性问题三重叠加，企业的运转能力虽略有回升，但电力不稳、治安风险与重建资金有限三道坎几乎同时压顶。亚行预计整体亚太2026年增长5.0%，而缅甸即便在“反弹”情景下，2026年也只被预估增长2.4%——在东盟国家里几乎垫底。

通胀更是一把钝刀。亚行估算缅甸2026年通胀率平均高达24%，世界银行则认为短期通胀仍将维持在20%以上。这意味着，即便名义收入没有下降，普通家庭的购买力也在被一点点啃掉。官方财政也撑得很紧：2026/27财年赤字预计达GDP的5%，公共债务维持在GDP的60%以上。

## 三重汇率：财富的隐形抽水机

缅甸今天并存着一套“三重汇率”：央行参考汇率约2100缅元兑1美元，银行间交易平台实际汇率约3658缅元兑1美元，而在仰光的民间黑市，汇率一度逼近4000缅元兑1美元（约合2.9万元人民币兑1美元）。同一张钞票，三种身价。

这套扭曲的汇率体系，本质上是向持有外币、靠近权力的人悄悄输送利益，同时把持有缅元的普通储户反复收割。进口成本被层层抬高，外资企业的财务报表被汇率差吞噬，而能够拿到官方低价美元的人，转手就是一笔横财。一个经济体的定价权一旦变成寻租工具，真正做实业的人只会越来越沉默。

## 房价为何逆势狂奔

既然实体经济在塌，为什么房子反而更贵？答案不在需求，而在“无处可逃的储蓄”。内战、制裁与货币贬值三重不确定之下，缅元信用不断磨损，银行存款随时可能被管制、被贬值、被冻结。于是，资本本能地扑向最古老、最笨重、也最不容易被没收的避风港——土地与房产。

当所有人都在抢同一种“保险”，价格当然会脱离租金、脱离收入、脱离一切基本面。仰光核心地段地价较政变前翻了数倍，房子的金融属性彻底压过了居住属性。这哪里是房地产市场，分明是一场对纸币的不信任投票。

## 谁在买房：战时资产的避风港

更值得玩味的是买房的人。在正规外资大举撤离、外国直接投资（FDI）锐减的背景下，撑起这笔交易的，并非国际资本，而更多是本土权贵、军方关联企业，以及把海外积蓄搬回来的离散资本。缅甸的华商群体历来是当地商业网络的中坚，战时他们同样面临资产缩水与汇兑锁死的两难，把资金转回境内的硬资产，成了一种“不折腾就等贬值”的自保。

当财富向少数能穿越管制的人集中，房价的上涨就不再是繁荣的证明，而是分配恶化的刻度。

## 贫困的另一面

资产价格在狂欢，另一头的民生却在下沉。世界银行估算，受地震、冲突与物价三重挤压，缅甸贫困率已攀升至约31%，约1700万人生活在温饱线以下；城市贫困率约21.3%，农村更高达34.9%。亚行预计2026年通胀约24%，粮食与能源价格首当其冲。

这正是战争经济最刺骨的地方：同一套货币贬值，既把权贵的砖头炒成了黄金，也把底层家庭的餐桌抽空了。通胀从来不是中性的一刀切，它精准地劈开了社会的阶层。

## 离散资本与侨汇的暗线

在这条资产狂欢的暗线里，离散社群与侨汇是不该被忽略的角色。缅甸的华侨华人长期深耕贸易、零售与中小制造，是民间资本的重要载体。当本币失稳、资金出海受限，他们寄回的侨汇与搬回的离散资本，相当一部分没有进入生产，而是沉淀为境内的房产与黄金——既是保值，也是乱世里一种低成本的身份锚定。

这也提醒我们，看待东南亚的“资本回流”，不能只看账面。很多时候，回流的不是信心，而是避险。

## 这究竟是复苏，还是转移

所以，回到开头那组反差：GDP缩水2%、房价年涨四成到六成，到底意味着什么？我的判断是，它描述的不是复苏，而是财富在战火中的“空间转移”——从受损的实体部门，挪到了少数能锁定的硬资产；从无权者的存款账户，挪到了有权者的地产簿。

缅甸真正的难题，从来不是某一年增长几个百分点，而是当统计在缩水、资产在膨胀、贫困在加深三者并存时，这个国家还有没有一套能把财富重新导向生产与民生的机制。如果没有，那么仰光那些年年上涨的房价，终将成为战争最沉默、也最昂贵的纪念碑。

## 延伸阅读

**战争经济（War Economy）**：指在经济体被武装冲突、制裁与治理真空重塑后，资源与财富配置以“存续与避险”而非“增长与效率”为主导的一种非正常经济形态，常见于内战或长期动荡地区。

**三重汇率（Multiple Exchange Rates）**：一国同时存在官方、银行间与黑市等多种汇率，往往是外汇管制与资本错配的产物，会系统性补贴特权群体、侵蚀普通储户，并扭曲贸易与投资的真实成本。

**离散资本（Diaspora Capital）**：指侨居海外的族群将积蓄、侨汇或投资回流原籍国的资本，在动荡经济体中常表现为“避险型回流”，即不进入实业生产，而沉淀为房产、黄金等硬资产。

# Myanmar's War-Economy Paradox: Official Output Shrinks 2%, Yet Yangon Property Prices Soar

Nikkei Asia reported that Myanmar's economy contracted 2 per cent in the fiscal year ended March 2026, dragged down by import controls, foreign-exchange shortages and the continuing civil war. Yet property agents in Yangon told the outlet that local home and commercial prices had climbed roughly 40 to 60 per cent every year between 2021 and 2026. An economy that contracts on paper while its assets inflate in practice is not showing a recovery signal — it is displaying the classic symptom of a war economy.

## The jarring contradiction

Lay Myanmar's macro figures side by side and the first thing that hits you is not growth but a pair of mutually contradictory curves. On one side, gross domestic product (GDP) keeps shrinking: the World Bank estimates real GDP fell 2.0 per cent in FY2025/26 (to March 2026), and the Asian Development Bank (ADB) confirms a 2.2 per cent contraction in 2025. On the other side, property and hard assets run against the tide. Yangon estate agents told Nikkei Asia that residential and commercial prices jumped 40 to 60 per cent annually across the five years since the coup.

On the same soil, the official "shrinkage" and the popular "inflation" of asset values occur at once. That alone tells you the conventional indicators no longer describe what is happening in Myanmar. When a country's statistics still count factories and farms while real wealth quietly flows into bricks and gold, you know the machine is running on a different logic.

## The numbers: an economy caving in

The contraction is structural, not cyclical. The World Bank points to a triple overlay of earthquake, persistent conflict and long-term structural problems: corporate operating capacity has recovered slightly, yet unstable electricity, security risks and limited reconstruction funds bear down together. The ADB projects 5.0 per cent growth for the broader Asia-Pacific in 2026, while Myanmar — even in a "rebound" scenario — is forecast to expand only 2.4 per cent, near the bottom of the ASEAN group.

Inflation is the blunt instrument. The ADB estimates Myanmar's average inflation at a punishing 24 per cent in 2026; the World Bank expects it to stay above 20 per cent in the near term. That means even where nominal incomes hold, households' purchasing power is being gnawed away. Public finances are also stretched: the FY2026/27 deficit is projected at 5 per cent of GDP, with public debt above 60 per cent of GDP.

## The triple exchange rate: an invisible drain

Myanmar today runs a "triple exchange rate": a central-bank reference rate of about 2,100 kyat to the dollar, an interbank platform rate near 3,658 kyat, and a Yangon street rate that has pushed toward 4,000 kyat. One note, three valuations.

This distorted system quietly channels benefit to those holding foreign currency and close to power, while repeatedly harvesting ordinary savers who hold kyat. Import costs are layered upward, foreign-investor balance sheets are eaten by the rate gap, and whoever can obtain cheap official dollars pockets an instant windfall. Once a currency's pricing power becomes a rent-seeking tool, those who actually make things only grow quieter.

## Why property defies gravity

If the real economy is caving in, why do houses get dearer? The answer is not demand but "savings with nowhere to flee". Amid the triple uncertainty of civil war, sanctions and currency depreciation, trust in the kyat keeps eroding; bank deposits can be controlled, devalued or frozen at any moment. Capital instinctively pounces on the oldest, heaviest and hardest-to-confiscate haven — land and property.

When everyone rushes the same "insurance", prices detach from rents, incomes and every fundamental. Prime Yangon land now commands several times its pre-coup price; the financial attribute of housing has completely overwhelmed its use value. This is no property market — it is a vote of no confidence in paper money.

## Who is buying: a haven in a wartime asset

More telling is who is buying. With formal foreign capital withdrawing en masse and foreign direct investment (FDI) shrinking sharply, the transactions are not propped up by international money but largely by local elites, military-linked firms and diaspora capital repatriated from abroad. Myanmar's ethnic-Chinese business community has long formed the backbone of local commercial networks; in wartime they too face the dilemma of asset erosion and locked exchanges, and channelling funds back into domestic hard assets became a self-protection of "do nothing and watch it depreciate".

When wealth concentrates among the few who can cross the controls, a rising property price ceases to prove prosperity and becomes a gauge of worsening distribution.

## The other face of poverty

Asset prices revel while livelihoods sink at the other end. The World Bank estimates that, squeezed by earthquake, conflict and prices, Myanmar's poverty rate has climbed to about 31 per cent, with some 17 million people below the subsistence line; urban poverty is about 21.3 per cent and rural poverty as high as 34.9 per cent. The ADB expects around 24 per cent inflation in 2026, with food and energy leading the rise.

This is the cruelest edge of a war economy: the same currency depreciation turns the elite's bricks into gold while emptying the poor family's table. Inflation is never a neutral blanket cut; it precisely splits society along class lines.

## The diaspora capital undercurrent

Along the undercurrent of this asset frenzy, the diaspora and remittances are roles that should not be ignored. Myanmar's overseas Chinese have long been rooted in trade, retail and small manufacturing, a key carrier of private capital. As the local currency destabilises and capital outflow is restricted, the remittances they send back and the diaspora capital they repatriate — a large share, rather than entering production — settle into domestic property and gold: both a store of value and, in turbulent times, a low-cost anchor of identity.

This also reminds us that Southeast Asia's "capital return" should not be read by the ledger alone. Often what returns is not confidence but hedging.

## Recovery, or merely transfer?

So, back to the opening contradiction: a 2 per cent GDP shrink alongside 40 to 60 per cent annual house-price gains — what does it mean? My reading is that it describes not a recovery but a "spatial transfer" of wealth amid war — from damaged real sectors to a few lockable hard assets, from the powerless saver's account to the powerful's property ledger.

Myanmar's real difficulty was never a few percentage points of annual growth; it is whether, with statistics shrinking, assets inflating and poverty deepening all at once, the country still has a mechanism to redirect wealth back toward production and livelihoods. If not, then those ever-rising Yangon prices will stand as the war's most silent — and most expensive — monument.

## Further reading

**War Economy**: An abnormal economic form in which, after an economy is reshaped by armed conflict, sanctions and governance vacuum, the allocation of resources and wealth is driven by "survival and hedging" rather than "growth and efficiency" — common in civil-war or chronically unstable regions.

**Multiple Exchange Rates**: When a country simultaneously runs official, interbank and black-market rates, usually a product of exchange controls and capital misallocation; it systematically subsidises privileged groups, erodes ordinary savers and distorts the true cost of trade and investment.

**Diaspora Capital**: Capital that overseas communities send back as savings, remittances or investment to their country of origin; in unstable economies it often appears as "hedging repatriation" — not entering real production but settling into hard assets such as property and gold.
