# 1462名外国人涉诈被捕：马来西亚的“窝点”警报

马来西亚警方今年前五个月逮捕1462名涉嫌商业罪案与网络诈骗的外国人，其中993人来自中国大陆，占比近七成。这个数字已远超2024年全年的1006人，直逼2025年全年的1663人——而今年只过了五个月。数字背后，是一条正在南移的诈骗产业链，也是一道摆在吉隆坡与北京面前的跨境治理考题。

## 数字：两年翻近三倍的曲线

据《中国报》报道，马来西亚武吉阿曼商业罪案调查局总监鲁斯迪10月20日披露：涉商业罪案与网络诈骗被捕的外国人，2024年为1006人，2025年增至1663人，增幅65.3%；今年仅前五个月就已达1462人。照此速度，2026年全年数字可能逼近3000人，约为2024年的三倍。

国籍构成更值得细看：中国大陆籍993人居首，其后依次为印尼85人、越南49人、缅甸42人、孟加拉39人、中国台湾37人。中国大陆籍占比约68%，超过其余所有国籍之和。

有人会问：为什么又是马来西亚？为什么又是中国籍？

## 免签的另一面：游客与骗子同门而入

答案部分藏在入境记录里。1462名落网者中，955人持社交访问准证入境，另有7人持旅游准证——绝大多数人是合法入境、逾期滞留，再转入诈骗活动。

自2023年底中马互免签证以来，人员往来确实升温，旅游与商务各得其利。但硬币的另一面同样清晰：面向游客打开的便利通道，也被诈骗团伙当成了通勤班车。社交访问准证本意是探亲访友，如今却成了涉案外籍人员最常见的入境凭证。政策设计者大概没有料到，免签的红利与免签的漏洞，会以这种方式同时兑现。

## 从“过境站”到“窝点”的滑落

这并非孤例。据马来西亚媒体报道，今年5月，吉隆坡警方突击首都地区46个单位，逮捕187人，七成是中国籍，起获资产总值5768万令吉（约9300万元人民币）；7月，柔佛警方在森林城市捣毁32个住宅单位内的诈骗窝点，335人被捕，其中309人是中国籍；彭亨州一处废弃度假村也被揭发为诈骗据点，98人落网，88人是中国籍。

更值得警惕的是迁徙路线：彭亨移民局调查显示，该团伙此前已在柬埔寨、越南辗转经营约9个月，专挑偏僻停业的度假村落脚。这条路线说明，随着柬埔寨、缅甸的诈骗园区在区域压力下收缩，团伙正在寻找下一个落脚点。鲁斯迪警告要确保马来西亚不沦为国际诈骗团伙的运作窝点或中转站——这更像是在描述一个正在发生的进程，而不是遥远的假设。

## 加害者，还是被拐的劳动者？

彭亨移民局总监扎卡利亚的说法值得留意：当局仍在厘清被捕者的真实身份——他们究竟是诈骗集团成员，还是被虚假招聘吸引、最终被迫在呼叫中心劳动的受害者。

这一区分绝非细节。柬埔寨与缅甸的经验反复证明，诈骗园区里既有骨干，也有被没收护照、遭受暴力、以业绩论生死的被拐劳工。马来西亚的落网者中，两种人恐怕都有。若不加甄别一律按罪犯处理，既可能冤枉受害者，也会让真正的人口贩运网络脱罪；反之，若一律按受害者放行，则等于为产业链开了后门。甄别机制——而不是抓捕数字——才是这场治理的真正难点。

## 侨社声誉的无形代价

还有一层成本难以计价。马来西亚有约670万华人，是海外华社历史最悠久、最成体系的社群之一。每一条“中国人涉诈被捕”的新闻，都在为这个社群平添无谓的嫌疑。绝大多数旅居马来西亚的华侨华人奉公守法，与诈骗产业毫无瓜葛，却不得不承受外界的异样眼光。

把犯罪归咎于族群，是廉价的叙事；但无视涉案者的国籍分布，也无助于理解问题的来龙去脉。侨社有理由要求更精准的表述——问题在于诈骗产业，不在于华人身份。

## 治理靠一国之力远远不够

鲁斯迪承诺加强执法、情报搜集与跨机构合作。方向没错，但诈骗产业链跨越国境，单靠一国的突击与抓捕，只能驱赶而不能根除——彭亨案例已经演示了团伙如何在柬埔寨、越南、马来西亚之间流转。

真正的解药至少包括三味：一是区域内实时共享涉案人员与窝点情报，让团伙无处可搬；二是落网者甄别与遣返协作机制，把受害者送回家，把骨干送上法庭；三是源头治理，斩断“高薪海外工作”的虚假招聘链条。中国与东盟国家近年联合打击跨境电诈已有相当积累，马来西亚若想避免沦为下一站，此刻正是接入这套区域机制的时机。

## 延伸阅读

- **电信诈骗（Telecom fraud）**：以电话、网络社交及虚假投资平台实施远程欺诈的犯罪形态，近年在东南亚形成产业化、跨境化的园区经济。
- **人口贩运（Human trafficking）**：以欺骗、胁迫手段招募并剥削劳动者的犯罪。东南亚诈骗园区大量涉案者实为被拐劳工，甄别受害者与加害者成为执法关键。
- **跨境治理（Cross-border governance）**：主权国家就跨境犯罪、人员流动等议题开展的多边协调机制，涵盖情报共享、联合执法与遣返合作。

# 1,462 Foreigners Arrested for Scams in Malaysia: The 'Hub' Alarm Is Already Ringing

In the first five months of this year, Malaysian police arrested 1,462 foreigners for commercial crimes and online scams. 993 of them — nearly seventy percent — came from mainland China. The figure has already blown past the 1,006 arrests recorded in all of 2024 and is closing in on 2025's full-year total of 1,663, with seven months of the year still uncounted. Behind the numbers lies a scam industry migrating southward — and a cross-border governance test now sitting on the desks of both Kuala Lumpur and Beijing.

## The numbers: a curve that keeps climbing

According to China Press, Rusdi, director of the Commercial Crime Investigation Department at Bukit Aman, disclosed on October 20 that arrests of foreign nationals for commercial crimes and online scams rose from 1,006 in 2024 to 1,663 in 2025 — a 65.3 percent jump. This year's first five months alone have already produced 1,462 arrests. If the pace holds, the 2026 full-year figure could approach 3,000, roughly triple the 2024 level.

The nationality breakdown deserves a closer look: 993 mainland Chinese lead the list, followed by 85 Indonesians, 49 Vietnamese, 42 Myanmar nationals, 39 Bangladeshis and 37 from Taiwan. Mainland Chinese account for about 68 percent — more than all other nationalities combined.

Which raises the obvious questions: why Malaysia, and why Chinese nationals?

## The other side of visa-free entry: tourists and scammers through the same door

Part of the answer sits in the immigration records. Of the 1,462 arrested, 955 entered on social visit passes and another seven on tourist passes. In other words, most arrived legally, overstayed, and drifted into scam operations.

Since China and Malaysia introduced mutual visa-free travel in late 2023, people flows have recovered, to the benefit of tourism and business alike. But the other side of the coin is equally clear: a door opened for tourists has doubled as a commuter route for scam syndicates. The social visit pass was designed for family visits; it has become the most common entry credential among arrested foreign suspects. Policymakers probably did not anticipate that the dividend and the loophole of visa-free travel would cash in simultaneously.

## From transit point to scam hub

These are not isolated cases. Malaysian media report that in May, Kuala Lumpur police raided 46 premises in the capital and arrested 187 people, seventy percent of them Chinese nationals, seizing assets worth 57.68 million ringgit (about US$13 million). In July, Johor police dismantled scam operations across 32 residential units in Forest City, arresting 335 people, 309 of them Chinese. An abandoned resort in Pahang was also exposed as a scam site, with 98 arrests, 88 of them Chinese nationals.

The migration route is the more telling detail. Pahang immigration officials found that the syndicate there had operated in Cambodia and Vietnam for roughly nine months before moving in, deliberately choosing remote, closed-down resorts. The route tells a story: as scam compounds in Cambodia and Myanmar shrink under regional pressure, the syndicates are shopping for the next base. Rusdi's pledge to ensure Malaysia does not become an operational hub or transit point for international scam syndicates reads less like a warning about a distant scenario and more like a description of a process already underway.

## Perpetrators, or trafficked workers?

One remark from Pahang immigration director Zakaria deserves attention: authorities are still determining whether those arrested are syndicate members or workers lured by fake job offers and forced to toil in call centers.

That distinction is anything but a technicality. Cambodia and Myanmar have repeatedly shown that scam compounds contain both hardened operators and trafficked workers stripped of their passports, subjected to violence, and graded by sales quotas. Among those arrested in Malaysia, both categories are likely present. Processing everyone as criminals risks punishing victims and letting trafficking networks off the hook; processing everyone as victims would open a back door for the industry itself. The screening mechanism — not the arrest tally — is the real difficulty of this fight.

## The invisible cost to diaspora reputation

There is also a cost that defies accounting. Malaysia is home to roughly 6.7 million ethnic Chinese, one of the oldest and most established overseas Chinese communities in the world. Every "Chinese nationals arrested for scams" headline adds unearned suspicion to that community. The overwhelming majority of overseas Chinese in Malaysia are law-abiding and have nothing to do with the scam economy, yet they bear the awkward glances all the same.

Blaming an ethnic group for crime is a cheap narrative — but ignoring the nationality distribution of the arrested does not help anyone understand how the problem came about. The community is entitled to more precise framing: the problem is the scam industry, not Chinese identity.

## No single country can police this alone

Rusdi has pledged stronger enforcement, intelligence gathering and cross-agency cooperation. The direction is right, but the scam supply chain runs across borders; one country's raids can displace syndicates, not eliminate them — the Pahang case has already demonstrated how gangs rotate between Cambodia, Vietnam and Malaysia.

A real remedy needs at least three ingredients: real-time regional sharing of intelligence on suspects and compound locations, so syndicates have nowhere to move; joint screening and repatriation protocols that send victims home and ringleaders to court; and source-country action to cut off the fake "high-paying overseas jobs" recruitment pipeline. China and ASEAN states have accumulated considerable experience in joint crackdowns on cross-border telecom fraud. If Malaysia wants to avoid becoming the next stop, now is the moment to plug into that regional machinery.

## Further reading

- **Telecom fraud**: Remote fraud via phone calls, social platforms and fake investment schemes, which has evolved into an industrialized, cross-border compound economy in Southeast Asia.
- **Human trafficking**: Recruiting and exploiting workers through deception and coercion. Many caught in Southeast Asian scam compounds are trafficked laborers; distinguishing victims from perpetrators is a key enforcement challenge.
- **Cross-border governance**: Multilateral coordination among sovereign states on transnational crime and migration, covering intelligence sharing, joint enforcement and repatriation cooperation.
