# 印尼镍矿出口禁令与全球电动车产业链

> 从原矿到电池：雅加达的产业升级野心与代价。

2020 年 1 月，印尼正式实施镍矿原矿出口禁令。彼时几乎没有人预料到，这个看似激进的决定，会在五年后重塑全球电动车电池上游格局。

## 禁令的逻辑：不做原材料供应商

印尼握有全球最大的镍储量——超过 2100 万吨，占世界已探明储量的近四分之一。长久以来，雅加达扮演的是"挖矿—出口"的原材料供应商角色，利润微薄、就业粗糙。

> 佐科政府的判断很简单：与其出口原矿让别人赚深加工的钱，不如把价值链留在国内。

禁令的核心诉求，是**倒逼外资在印尼本土建厂**，把冶炼、前驱体、正极材料直至电芯组装的环节搬到镍矿边上。这一招，确实奏效了。

## 谁来建厂：中资主导的格局

禁令落地后，青山控股、宁德时代、华友钴业等中资企业率先涌入苏拉威西与马鲁古群岛，构建起从镍冶炼到电池材料的完整产能。据统计，印尼已建与在建的镍冶炼产能超过 200 万吨/年，绝大多数为中资背景。

| 阶段 | 主要玩家 | 产能规模 |
|------|----------|----------|
| 镍冶炼 | 青山、德龙 | 200 万吨/年级别 |
| 前驱体 | 中伟、华友 | 数十万吨级 |
| 正极/电芯 | 宁德、LG新能源 | 起步阶段 |

## 代价：环保与社会的两难

繁荣的另一面是争议。苏拉威西的镍冶炼园区，多次被环保组织指控破坏红树林与海洋生态；2023 年的数起冶炼厂劳工事故，更把工作条件问题推到国际视野。

**RKEF 工艺的高耗能**——每生产一吨镍铁，要消耗约 40 兆瓦时电力——让印尼的低碳叙事显得尴尬。雅加达承诺的"绿色镍"，在现实中更多停留在口号阶段。

## 外资的犹豫

并非所有玩家都买账。2025 年，部分日韩电池企业因环保合规成本与政策不确定性，放缓了在印尼的扩产节奏。欧盟也在 WTO 提起对禁令的挑战，认为其扭曲了全球原材料市场。

> 禁令重塑了电池上游，但雅加达尚未证明，它能把这个优势真正延伸到价值链的高端。

## 结语

印尼的镍矿禁令，是资源型国家产业升级的一次大胆实验。它证明了一个朴素的道理——**原材料不是命运，把价值链往下游延伸才有议价权**。

但实验的下半场，是环保合规、是技术自主、是与外资的长期博弈。雅加达的野心能否兑现，未来五年见分晓。

# Indonesia's Nickel Export Ban and the Global EV Battery Chain

> From raw ore to battery: Jakarta's industrial-upgrading ambition and its costs.

In January 2020, Indonesia formally imposed a ban on raw nickel ore exports. Few at the time foresaw that this seemingly drastic decision would, within five years, reshape the upstream of the global EV battery industry.

## The Logic of the Ban: No Longer a Raw-Material Supplier

Indonesia holds the world's largest nickel reserves — over 21 million tonnes, nearly a quarter of proven global reserves. For a long time, Jakarta played the "mine-and-export" role of a raw-material supplier, with thin margins and rough employment.

> The Jokowi government's reasoning was simple: rather than export raw ore and let others capture the deep-processing margins, keep the value chain at home.

The core demand of the ban was to **force foreign capital to build plants on Indonesian soil**, moving smelting, precursors, cathode materials, and even cell assembly to the mine's doorstep. It worked.

## Who Builds: A Chinese-Led Landscape

After the ban took effect, Chinese firms — Tsingshan, CATL, Huayou Cobalt — led the rush into Sulawesi and the Maluku Islands, building integrated capacity from nickel smelting to battery materials. Indonesia's built and under-construction nickel smelting capacity now exceeds 2 million tonnes per year, the bulk of it Chinese-backed.

| Stage | Key Players | Scale |
|------|----------|----------|
| Nickel smelting | Tsingshan, Delong | ~2m t/year class |
| Precursors | CNGR, Huayou | Tens of thousands of tonnes |
| Cathode / cells | CATL, LG Energy Solution | Nascent |

## The Cost: Environment and Society in Tension

The flip side of the boom is controversy. Sulawesi's nickel parks have been repeatedly accused by environmental groups of destroying mangroves and marine ecology; a string of smelter labor incidents in 2023 pushed working conditions into the international spotlight.

**The energy intensity of the RKEF process** — producing a tonne of ferronickel consumes roughly 40 MWh of electricity — makes Indonesia's low-carbon narrative awkward. Jakarta's promised "green nickel" remains, in practice, mostly a slogan.

## Foreign Capital's Hesitation

Not everyone is convinced. In 2025, several Japanese and Korean battery makers slowed their Indonesia expansion, citing environmental compliance costs and policy uncertainty. The EU also challenged the ban at the WTO, arguing it distorts global raw-material markets.

> The ban reshaped the battery upstream, but Jakarta has yet to prove it can extend that advantage to the high end of the value chain.

## Conclusion

Indonesia's nickel ban is a bold experiment in resource-state industrial upgrading. It proves a plain truth — **raw materials are not destiny; only by extending the value chain downstream does a country gain pricing power.**

But the second half of the experiment is environmental compliance, technological autonomy, and a long contest with foreign capital. Whether Jakarta's ambition pays off will be decided in the next five years.
