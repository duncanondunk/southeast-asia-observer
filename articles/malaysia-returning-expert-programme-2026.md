# 马来西亚（Malaysia）专才回流计划推行15年 仅吸引5234人回国

10月23日，马来西亚人力资源部（KESUMA）在国会一份书面答复中，披露了一组足以令任何寄望于本国侨民（散居海外国民）的政府警醒的数字：自2011年推出"专才回流计划"（Returning Expert Programme，简称REP）以来，全国共接获1万2850份申请，批准8093份，实际回国工作的人数却只有5234人。十五年努力，最终回国的申请者还不到四成。这项本为逆转人才外流而设的计划，用自己的账面成绩，成了马来西亚人才流失有多难止住的一面镜子。

## 数字：15年、12850份申请、5234人回国

这笔账目，是在回应反对党国民联盟（PN）议员沙哈里祖基奈因（Setiu 选区）的质询时公开的，它在每一个环节都透出"漏水"的迹象。1万2850名申请人中，8093人获批——63%的批准率单看并不难看。真正的流失发生在终点线之前：截至今年8月，获批者中真正回流并入职的只有5234人，占全部申请人的40.73%，也就是说，拿到回国许可的人里，约五分之二最终并未成行。说直白些，政府铺好了欢迎毯、办完了文书，却眼睁睁看着多数合资格国民转身走向了相反的方向。

## 计划的初衷：把外流专才拉回来

REP由人力资源部下属的"人才机构"（TalentCorp）负责落实，设计的本意是为旅居海外的马来西亚高技能国民搭建一座归桥。它开出的条件相当优厚：回国后连续五年可享15%的单一税率；购买一辆本地组装车可豁免最高10万令吉（约16万元人民币）的消费税；外国配偶与未成年子女还可申请永久居留权。逻辑很顺——用税务与移民优惠，把医生、工程师和技术人才拉回来，为马来西亚的创新经济播下种子。对于一个长期向新加坡、澳大利亚和西方"失血"的国家而言，这项计划被包装成摆脱人才外流的出口匝道。

## 为什么留不住人：薪资、政治与生活

那么，为何成效不彰？分析人士把矛头指向那些并不光鲜的基本面。薪资差距仍是头号拦路虎：一名马来西亚工程师或软件开发者在新加坡的薪水，可能是在本国同岗位的二到三倍，而五年减税根本补不上这道鸿沟。除了钱，政治氛围与整体生活质量——学校、医疗、交通、治理——才是举家权衡是否回流时的真正砝码。15%的单一税率固然是善意姿态，却无法掩盖一种持续十年的感受：在本土，那份社会契约正在磨损。REP把人才回流当作一道文书题，但对多数移民而言，它是一道人生题。

## 官僚迷宫：繁琐流程劝退精英

这项计划也一直是自己最大的敌人。国会答复本身承认，首相安华已指示人力资源部审查该计划，包括申请耗时、批准与拒绝的数量。多年来，批评者——其中一些人甚至主张直接关闭人才机构——一直指其申请流程过分繁琐，令许多海外精英干脆放弃。要求申请人在仍旅居海外时就递交材料，要辗转于移民、税务、内政多个部门，还要在好处兑现之前，先为外国配偶铺好永久居留之路：阻力是真实存在的。马来西亚实际上是在要求本国最善流动的国民，为了回家而先去爬一座官僚迷宫。

## 一张带着族群面孔的侨民图景

这里还藏着令人不安的族群底色。马来西亚的人才外流长期带着族群维度：华裔马来西亚人在移民群体中占比偏高，动因既有机会的牵引，也有部分人对偏袒"土著"（马来人与原住民）的固有帮助政策的疏离感。REP希望吸引的许多专才——以及许多默默拒绝回流的人——正是华裔。对于一个华侨华人既为经济引擎、又常成政治焦点的国家，一项连本国公民都赢不回来的回流计划，指向的是一个更深的归属之问。毕竟，人才总是流向它感到被需要的地方。

## 横向比较：邻国怎样抢人才

马来西亚并非唯一在争取本国侨民的国家，却被对手抢了先机。新加坡把区域人才池当作战略资源，用高薪与一座没有摩擦的城邦，把马来西亚想留住的同一批人吸走。中国式的"千人计划"类项目，以及东南亚（Southeast Asia）邻国推出的创业与研究签证，比拼的是速度与确定感，而非五年税务假期。教训很清楚：留人要在上游赢得——在培养人的大学、雇用人的实验室、让人安居的城市——而不是手持一份表格在边境守株。马来西亚的REP，把力气用错了管道的位置。

## 结语：小修小补难解结构性难题

今年8月启动的"心系归侨"（MyHeart）计划及其"全球联系"（Global Connect）分支，聚焦人工智能、医疗与马新战略合作等领域，是值得欢迎的修补。但它们只是在一个结构性问题的边缘做文章。十五年、5234人回国，这个计划并非偶然失败，而是要求人们回到他们当初有充分理由离开的环境时，必然得到的结果。如果吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）真想赢回人才，就得在与他们离开时同样的那些维度上竞争——薪资、制度，以及一种"在家乡的未来比在国外更明亮"的安静信心。在此之前，REP的账面，只会继续数着那些说了"不"的人。

## 延伸阅读

**专才回流计划（Returning Expert Programme）**：马来西亚由人才机构（TalentCorp）自2011年起运行的计划，以税务、车辆消费税与居留优惠吸引旅居海外的高技能国民回流。

**人才外流（Brain drain）**：高技能专业人才从一国移出，是马来西亚因与新加坡及西方薪资差距而长期面对的难题。

**人才机构（TalentCorp）**：隶属人力资源部的政府机构，负责落实马来西亚的人才吸引与留用战略。

# Malaysia's 15-Year Talent Repatriation Plan Has Lured Back Just 5,234 Experts

On October 23, Malaysia's Human Resources Ministry (KESUMA) revealed in a written parliamentary reply a number that should worry any government betting on its own diaspora: since the Returning Expert Programme (REP) was launched in 2011, the country has received 12,850 applications, approved 8,093, and seen only 5,234 professionals actually come home to work. Fifteen years of effort, and barely four in ten applicants have returned. The scheme meant to reverse Malaysia's brain drain has, by its own ledger, become a measure of how hard that drain is to stop.

## The numbers: 15 years, 12,850 applications, 5,234 returns

The figures, released in answer to a question from opposition lawmaker Shaharizukirnain Abd Kadir (PN–Setiu), tell a story of attrition at every stage. Of 12,850 who applied, 8,093 were approved — a 63 percent approval rate that looks healthy on paper. But the real leakage is at the finish line: only 5,234 of those approved actually relocated and took up work by August this year. That is 40.73 percent of all applicants, and roughly two-fifths of those cleared to return never did. Put bluntly, the government built a welcome mat, processed the paperwork, and then watched a majority of qualified Malaysians walk the other way.

## The original intent: luring back the diaspora's expertise

The REP, run by TalentCorp under the Human Resources Ministry, was designed to be a bridge for highly skilled Malaysians abroad. Its incentives are substantial: an optional 15 percent flat tax rate on employment income for five consecutive years; exemption of excise duty up to 100,000 ringgit (about US$24,000) on one locally assembled car; and the chance for foreign spouses and minor children to apply for permanent residency. The logic was sound — use tax and immigration perks to pull back doctors, engineers, and technologists who could seed Malaysia's innovation economy. For a country that has long hemorrhaged talent to Singapore, Australia, and the West, the programme was sold as the off-ramp from brain drain.

## Why it fails to retain: pay, politics, and life

So why does it underperform? Analysts point to the unglamorous basics. Salary gaps remain the single largest deterrent: a Malaysian engineer or software developer in Singapore can earn two to three times what the same role pays at home, and that gap is not closed by a five-year tax discount. Beyond money, political climate and overall quality of life — schools, healthcare, traffic, governance — shape the calculus of families weighing a return. A 15 percent flat tax is a welcome gesture, but it cannot paper over a decade-long sense that the social contract at home is fraying. The REP treats talent return as a paperwork problem when, for most migrants, it is a life-problem.

## The bureaucratic maze: red tape that deters elites

The scheme has also been its own worst enemy. The parliamentary reply itself acknowledges that Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim has ordered a review of the programme, including how long applications take and how many are approved or rejected. For years, critics — some of whom have called for TalentCorp to be shut down entirely — argued that the application process was so cumbersome that many overseas elites simply gave up. Requiring applicants to file while still abroad, navigating immigration, tax, and home-affairs ministries, and securing permanent-residency pathways for foreign spouses, all before a single ringgit of incentive is realised: the friction is real. Malaysia is effectively asking its most mobile citizens to climb a bureaucracy in order to come home.

## A diaspora with an ethnic face

There is an unsettling demographic underside. Malaysia's brain drain has long carried an ethnic dimension: ethnic Chinese Malaysians are disproportionately represented among those who emigrate, driven by a mix of opportunity and, for some, discomfort with entrenched affirmative-action policies favouring the Bumiputera majority. Many of the professionals the REP hopes to attract — and many of those who quietly decline — are ethnic Chinese. For a country where the Overseas Chinese community is both an economic engine and a political flashpoint, a repatriation programme that cannot win back its own citizens speaks to a deeper question of belonging. Talent, after all, flows toward where it feels wanted.

## Benchmarking: how neighbours compete for talent

Malaysia is not alone in courting its diaspora, but it is being outmanoeuvred. Singapore treats the regional talent pool as a strategic resource, offering salaries and a frictionless city-state to draw the same Malaysians Malaysia wants back. China's "Thousand Talents" style programmes, and indeed Southeast Asian neighbours' startup and research visas, compete on speed and certainty rather than on five-year tax holidays. The lesson is that retention is won upstream — in the universities that train people, the labs that employ them, and the cities that let them live well — not at the border with a form in hand. Malaysia's REP spends its energy at the wrong end of the pipeline.

## Conclusion: tinkering won't fix a structural problem

The new "MyHeart" initiative and its Global Connect arm, launched in August with a focus on AI, health, and Malaysia–Singapore strategic priorities, are welcome refinements. But they tinker at the margins of a structural problem. Fifteen years and 5,234 returns is not a programme that has failed by accident; it is the predictable result of asking people to return to conditions they left for good reasons. If Kuala Lumpur wants its talent back, it will need to compete on the things that made them leave — pay, institutions, and the quiet confidence that the future at home is brighter than the one abroad. Until then, the REP's ledger will keep counting the ones who said no.
