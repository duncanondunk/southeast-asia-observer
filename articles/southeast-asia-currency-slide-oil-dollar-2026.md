# 东南亚货币遭油价与强美元夹击：印尼盾年内贬逾7%领跌

中东局势骤然紧张，国际油价重新站上每桶100美元（约720元人民币）关口，东南亚货币正被卷入新一轮风暴。布伦特原油一周之内上涨16.4%，西得克萨斯中质原油更飙涨19.2%，创下自2022年2月俄乌冲突以来最猛的单周涨幅；与此同时，美元指数本周有望上涨1.4%，录得2024年11月以来最强单周表现。据日经亚洲援引三菱日联金融集团新加坡全球市场研究部高级货币分析师劳埃德·陈（Lloyd Chan）的判断，亚洲汇率的异动，本质上是全球风险情绪脆弱、能源价格攀升与地缘政治风险居高不下的共同写照。换言之，东南亚货币正同时承受三重压力：避险资金回流美元、进口能源成本骤升、以及本就薄弱的财政与经常账户更显脆弱。

## 三重夹击：油价、美元与避险

油价与美元同时走强，对东南亚是一记组合拳。区域内绝大多数经济体是能源净进口国，布伦特与西得克萨斯中质原油的单周涨幅意味着进口账单迅速膨胀；而美元走强则让以美元计价的债务、原材料与燃料采购变得更加昂贵。对印尼、菲律宾、泰国等国而言，油价每上涨1美元，财政补贴就得多掏一分钱。财政空间原本就有限的国家，等于在收入尚未恢复时被强迫追加支出。这正是为什么市场情绪会突然收紧——交易员看到的不是单一冲击，而是通胀、赤字与资本外流的三重共振。

## 数字：印尼盾今年已贬值逾7%

把镜头拉到区域内部，分化一目了然。印尼盾是今年以来东南亚表现最差的货币，兑美元累计贬值超过7%。9月30日前后，印尼盾一度触及约17535兑1美元的四个月高位，但好景不长——进入10月，随着全球债券收益率继续走高，印尼盾再度承压。更具反差的是基本面：印尼8月录得355亿美元（约256亿元人民币）的贸易顺差，远超市场预期约6.3亿美元（约45亿元人民币），通胀却加速至3.28%，已逼近印尼央行1.5%至3.5%目标区间的上沿。贸易顺差未能转化为汇率支撑，恰恰说明市场担心的不是当下贸易，而是能源账单与财政纪律。

## 印尼：最脆弱的一环

为什么是印尼？三个因素叠加。第一，尽管印尼是产油国，其原油却高度依赖从中东进口，中东依存度约20%至30%，加之国家战略石油储备仅约20天，油价每涨一分，进口成本就实打实地抬升。第二，为平抑国内油价，政府以财政补贴兜底，能源补贴支出激增，把原本约2.68%的财政赤字率推高至3%以上，突破法定红线。第三，市场用脚投票：惠誉与穆迪已在2026年先后将印尼评级展望下调至负面。印尼央行今年5至6月累计加息100个基点，但抛售美元、消耗外储的防御，只延缓了跌势，没能逆转资本外流。

## 马来西亚与菲律宾：难以独善其身

印尼之外，没有谁真正「免疫」。10月首个交易日，马来西亚林吉特、菲律宾比索兑美元普遍回落0.1%至0.3%。马来西亚虽然财政相对稳健、科技出口韧性较强，却同样受困于全球收益率上行与美元走强；菲律宾比索则长期受原油几乎全部依赖进口之苦，油价上涨直接转化为输入型通胀与家庭账单压力。区域内唯一的亮色是新加坡——其海峡时报指数在多数市场下挫时逆势微涨，但分析师将其更多解读为「前期涨多后的获利了结与重定价」，而非结构性脱钩。

## 加息还是死守：央行的两难

摆在东南亚央行面前的，是一道没有好选项的选择题。若跟随加息以吸引外资、守住本币，就要承受借贷成本上升、抑制刚有起色的内需；若按兵不动，本币贬值又会推高进口物价，进一步侵蚀民众购买力。华侨银行（OCBC）的经济学家判断，印尼央行很可能在11月或12月再加息25个基点。但更深层的悖论在于：加息本身无法解决能源依赖与财政透支这两个结构性病灶，只能买来时间。对能源净进口国而言，强美元与高油价是外部冲击，而财政纪律松弛与产业短板，才是本币长期承压的内因。

## 油价破百的连锁反应

油价重新站上每桶100美元（约720元人民币），对东南亚的冲击远不止于汇率。加油站排起长队、电费补贴捉襟见肘、物流成本向食品价格传导——这些都会被通胀数据一一记录。更值得注意的是，中东冲突的外溢正在改写区域的安全与财政叙事：泰国、菲律宾等高度依赖中东原油的经济体，不得不重启或扩大燃料补贴与紧急借贷，中期债务风险随之抬头。能源安全，从一句政策口号，变成了迫在眉睫的预算窟窿。

## 区域韧性分化

把视野放宽，东南亚并非铁板一块。越南凭借制造业与外资流入，上半年经济增速超过8%，本币相对抗跌；新加坡、马来西亚受益于人工智能产业链与结构性改革的叙事，被机构列为相对看好的货币。但印尼、菲律宾这类能源净进口、财政余量有限的经济体，则在大国利率周期与地缘冲突的夹缝中左右挨耳光。这种分化提示我们：所谓「东南亚崛起」，内部温差极大，一场油价与美元的外部冲击，足以把区域内部的强弱格局重新排序。

## 结语：风暴远未结束

这场汇率风暴，与其说是偶然，不如说是多重长期病灶的集中暴露。强美元或许会随美联储政策预期而回落，但中东局势的走向、能源价格的黏性、以及区域内财政纪律的差异，都不会在几周内消失。对普通民众而言，本币贬值最直接的含义是：进口商品更贵、出行成本更高、生活账单更重。对决策者而言，真正的考题不是如何「托住」汇率，而是如何在补贴、赤字与改革之间，找到一条不那么痛苦的平衡之路。东南亚的货币之锚，仍在风浪中摇晃。

## 延伸阅读

**印尼盾（Indonesian Rupiah）**：东南亚最大经济体的本币，2026年因油价与强美元叠加成为区域表现最差货币之一，年内贬值逾7%。
**能源补贴（Fuel Subsidy）**：印尼等产油国为平抑国内油价而由财政兜底的支出，油价上涨时迅速膨胀，是推高财政赤字的核心变量。
**经常账户（Current Account）**：衡量一国贸易与投资收益收支的账户；能源净进口国逆差扩大，会直接削弱本币的支撑基础。

# Southeast Asian currencies caught between oil and the dollar: rupiah down over 7%

Middle East tensions have flared and international oil is back above $100 a barrel, pulling Southeast Asian currencies into a fresh storm. Brent crude jumped 16.4% in a single week and West Texas Intermediate surged 19.2%, the steepest weekly rise since the Russia–Ukraine conflict in February 2022. At the same time, the US dollar index is on track for a 1.4% weekly gain, its strongest since November 2024. As Lloyd Chan, senior currency analyst at MUFG Global Markets Research in Singapore, put it in remarks carried by Nikkei Asia, the latest Asia FX moves "underscore fragile global sentiment and rising global growth uncertainty as energy prices climb and geopolitical risks remain elevated." Southeast Asian currencies are being squeezed from three directions at once: safe-haven flows back to the dollar, a sudden jump in imported energy costs, and already-fragile fiscal and current-account positions made even more exposed.

## The triple squeeze: oil, the dollar, and safe-haven flows

Rising oil prices and a stronger dollar are a double blow. Most economies in the region are net energy importers, so the weekly gains in Brent and WTI mean import bills are swelling fast; a stronger dollar makes dollar-denominated debt, raw materials and fuel purchases more expensive. For Indonesia, the Philippines and Thailand, every extra dollar on the oil price forces governments to spend more on subsidies. For states whose fiscal room is already limited, this is an unplanned outlay before revenues have recovered. That is why sentiment has tightened so quickly: traders are not pricing one isolated shock, but a triple resonance of inflation, deficits and capital flight.

## The numbers: the rupiah is down over 7% this year

Zoom in and the divergence is stark. The Indonesian rupiah is the worst-performing currency in Southeast Asia in 2026, having weakened more than 7% against the US dollar year-to-date. Around 30 September it briefly touched a near four-month high of about 17,535 to the dollar, but the reprieve was short-lived; as global bond yields climbed into October, the rupiah came under renewed pressure. The contrast with fundamentals is telling: Indonesia posted a US$3.55 billion trade surplus in August, far above the roughly US$630 million forecast, yet inflation accelerated to 3.28%, near the top of Bank Indonesia's 1.5%–3.5% target band. A swelling trade surplus that fails to support the currency says the market is pricing not today's trade but tomorrow's energy bill and fiscal discipline.

## Indonesia: the weakest link

Why Indonesia? Three forces converge. First, although Indonesia is an oil producer, it depends heavily on Middle East imports for crude, with a Middle East dependency of roughly 20%–30% and a strategic reserve of only about 20 days; every rise in oil prices directly lifts import costs. Second, to keep domestic fuel prices low the government backstops the bill with fiscal subsidies, and surging subsidy spending pushed the fiscal deficit from around 2.68% of GDP toward and above the statutory 3% ceiling. Third, markets voted with their feet: Fitch and Moody's both cut Indonesia's credit outlook to negative in 2026. Bank Indonesia raised rates by a combined 100 basis points over May and June, but selling dollars and burning reserves only delayed, rather than reversed, capital outflows.

## Malaysia and the Philippines: no safe harbor

Beyond Indonesia, no one is truly immune. On the first trading day of October, the Malaysian ringgit and Philippine peso both slipped 0.1%–0.3% against the dollar. Malaysia, for all its relatively sound fiscal position and resilient tech exports, is still caught between rising global yields and a stronger dollar; the Philippine peso, which relies almost entirely on imported crude, translates every oil spike straight into imported inflation and household bills. The lone bright spot was Singapore, whose Straits Times Index edged up while most regional benchmarks fell — but analysts read that more as profit-taking and repricing after a strong run than as structural decoupling.

## Hike or defend: the central bank dilemma

Southeast Asian central banks face a choice with no good option. Hiking to attract foreign capital and defend the currency means higher borrowing costs and a hit to a still-fragile domestic recovery; standing pat lets depreciation feed imported inflation and erode household purchasing power. Economists at OCBC expect Bank Indonesia to deliver another 25-basis-point increase in either November or December. The deeper paradox is that rate hikes cannot cure the structural ills of energy dependency and fiscal overreach — they only buy time. For energy net-importers, a strong dollar and $100 oil are external shocks; lax fiscal discipline and industrial shortcomings are the domestic causes of sustained currency pressure.

## The ripple effects of $100 oil

Oil back above $100 a barrel sends shocks well beyond exchange rates. Queues at petrol stations, stretched electricity subsidies, and logistics costs feeding into food prices — all of it shows up in the inflation data. More tellingly, the spillover from the Middle East conflict is rewriting the region's security and fiscal narrative: economies like Thailand and the Philippines, heavily dependent on Middle Eastern crude, are forced to restart or expand fuel subsidies and emergency borrowing, raising medium-term debt risks. Energy security has moved from a policy slogan to an immediate hole in the budget.

## Diverging resilience across the region

Step back and Southeast Asia is no monolith. Vietnam, riding manufacturing and foreign-investment inflows, grew more than 8% in the first half and its currency held up relatively well; Singapore and Malaysia, buoyed by the AI supply chain and reform narratives, are flagged by institutions as relatively favoured. But energy net-importers with thin fiscal buffers, such as Indonesia and the Philippines, are squeezed from both sides by the global rate cycle and geopolitical shocks. This divergence is a reminder that the much-touted "rise of Southeast Asia" hides wide internal temperature gaps — and one oil-and-dollar shock is enough to re-rank the regional pecking order.

## Conclusion: the storm is far from over

This currency storm is less an accident than a concentrated exposure of long-standing vulnerabilities. A stronger dollar may ease as Federal Reserve expectations shift, but the trajectory of the Middle East, the stickiness of energy prices, and the gap in regional fiscal discipline will not vanish in weeks. For ordinary people, currency depreciation means dearer imports, costlier travel, heavier household bills. For policymakers, the real test is not how to "hold up" the exchange rate but how to find a less painful balance among subsidies, deficits and reform. Southeast Asia's monetary anchor is still rocking in the waves.

**Current account**: The record of a country's trade and investment income; for energy net-importers, a widening deficit directly weakens the foundation that supports the currency.
