# 美国制裁东南亚诈骗网络：长臂伸进园区之后

据《南华早报》报道，美国财政部海外资产控制办公室（OFAC）于11月19日宣布制裁三名个人和两家公司，理由是它们与东南亚诈骗园区存在关联。名单里既有与太子集团（Prince Group）创办人陈志有关的柬埔寨商人赵辉，也有缅甸克伦民主仁爱军（DKBA）的两名领导人。把商人和地方武装放进同一份名单，本身就是一个判断：华盛顿要打的不是几通诈骗电话，而是一套由园区、资本和枪杆子拼起来的生意。

## 一份名单，两条打击路线

据美国财政部通报，赵辉曾是太子集团旗下诈骗园区的承租方，并向陈志支付保护费；他还拥有一处独立园区，主要通过虚拟货币投资骗局针对美国和欧洲民众。与他一同被制裁的，是北方博林建筑装饰有限公司和另一家投资咨询公司，两家公司均由他担任董事长。这条路线指向经营者：谁出钱、谁租赁、谁把园区当作现金流机器。

另一条路线指向保护者。克伦民主仁爱军总司令苏瑞瓦（Saw Shwe Wah）与负责泰国边境三宝塔关口安全的苏阿万（Saw A One）被指为诈骗园区提供安保，并参与针对被贩运受害者的暴力。美国财政部说得很直白：没有地方武装放行，园区里的电脑和话术无法运转。把武装头目列为金融制裁对象，是把“保护伞”本身当成犯罪基础设施，而不只是道德批判。

点评：这份名单的针对性比大而化之的“反诈宣言”更强。它承认诈骗园区不是单一犯罪现场，而是一条包括地产、安保、资金和跨境招聘的产业链。问题在于，列出名字只是第一步；被制裁者能否被找到资产、能否被当地执法机构配合执行，才是成败所在。

## 182亿美元：数字背后的问责压力

美国国务院发言人汤米·皮戈特（Tommy Pigott）称，2025年东南亚诈骗园区从美国人手中骗走约182亿美元（约1310亿元人民币）。美国财政部此前估计，2024年美国民众因东南亚诈骗至少损失100亿美元（约720亿元人民币），比前一年增加66%。这些数字当然服务于国内的问责政治：选民被骗走毕生积蓄，国会要问为什么境外园区能长期运转。

但数字也揭示了几个事实。其一，诈骗对象已经从周边国家扩展到欧美普通家庭，远程通信让地理距离失去意义。其二，损失规模意味着园区不可能只靠少量街头骗子，它需要账户、通道和洗钱链条。其三，美国把受害者损失货币化，实际上是在为自己的域外执法寻找正当性：既然美国公民是主要受害者，华盛顿就有理由把制裁触角伸到金边和缅泰边境。

## 双重国籍与撤销国籍：身份成了工具

赵辉的个案尤其值得注意。美国财政部把他称为柬埔寨国民，而制裁名录同时列出中国为其替代国籍，并注明他出生于哈尔滨。这意味着国籍在这里不是情感归属，而是可以被不同政府用来主张管辖、识别风险或切割关系的法律工具。陈志的遭遇更典型：柬埔寨今年1月应北京要求将其逮捕并引渡至中国，同时撤销其柬埔寨国籍；美国则另行指控他共谋从事电信欺诈和洗钱。

两件事可以同时成立，却讲的是不同故事。北京强调跨境犯罪嫌疑人的移交与公民保护，华盛顿强调金融制裁与刑事起诉，金边则用撤销国籍表明“这个人不再属于我们”。当一个商人拥有多重身份时，哪个国家先行行动，往往取决于案件与谁的政治议程更贴近。对小国而言，这种选边并非单纯的法律问题，而是外交与投资之间的取舍。

## 美中罕见合流，叙事仍然不同

据《南华早报》报道，制裁宣布前一天，美国与澳大利亚、加拿大、日本、越南等12个国家共同发表打击网络诈骗的合作声明，并成立所谓“威胁干扰中心”。再往前看，美国国务院东亚和太平洋事务助理国务卿8月已表示，特朗普曾向习近平提及东南亚跨国犯罪组织问题。与此同时，中国也在推动柬埔寨等国打击电诈。表面看，这是大国少有的合作窗口。

但合作并不等于叙事一致。美方反复把园区与“中国有组织犯罪集团”联系起来，中方则更愿意把问题框定为跨境治理与受害者救助。前一种说法若被不加区分地引用，容易把海外华人与犯罪网络捆绑在一起；后一种说法若只强调公民保护，又可能回避地方保护和腐败链条。真正的合作应当落在可核验的标准上：共享受益所有人信息、统一受害者识别程序、冻结租金与保护费流向，而不是在发布会里比谁更重视反诈。

## 园区不是普通犯罪现场

把诈骗园区想象成“网吧里的骗子”会低估问题的严重性。受害者往往被高薪招聘诱骗至边境，抵达后被扣押护照、限制离开，在债务和暴力威胁下从事诈骗。克伦民主仁爱军被指控为园区提供安保并参与暴力，说明被贩运者面对的不只是老板，还有持枪的看守。园区因而具有三重身份：生产诈骗收益的车间、关押受害者的场所、地方武装的租金来源。

这也解释了为什么单点打击经常失灵。关掉一个园区，经营者可以在边境另一侧租用土地；抓走一批话务员，背后的招聘中介、账户供应商和保护费链条仍在。若土地登记、公司注册和银行尽职调查只看纸面文件，不查实际受益人和租赁关系，所谓整治就容易变成把问题从地图的一侧推到另一侧。

## 制裁能拆掉保护伞吗

金融制裁的优势是速度快、成本低，能直接抬高被指认者的交易成本；弱点则同样明显：它依赖美元结算体系和跨国银行的配合，且很少能直接解救被困在园区里的人。若没有同步的刑事调查、受害者遣返和地方反腐，被制裁的公司可以改名，武装头目可以换代理人，租金仍会找到新的收款人。

更实质的问题是，美国是否愿意把制裁工具用于整个生态，而不仅是几个显眼的名字。地产开发商、提供公司注册服务的中介、协助资金转移的地下钱庄，以及明知园区性质仍提供电力和网络的地方当局，都需要被纳入问责。同时，海外华人社群应主动与被指的犯罪网络划清界限，支持受害者救助，而不是让“华人涉诈”成为沉默的默认前提。反诈若要有效，必须既打击犯罪，也防止对整个族群的错误归罪。

## 延伸阅读

**金融制裁（Financial sanctions）**：通过冻结资产、禁止交易和限制金融通道，提高特定个人或企业的经营成本；其效力取决于结算网络、银行合规和国际协作。

**保护伞经济（Protection economy）**：企业向地方武装或腐败官员支付费用以换取安保与默许的灰色交易结构；在边境诈骗园区中，保护费是运营成本而非额外开销。

**侨民污名化（Diaspora stigmatisation）**：把少数成员的犯罪行为归因于整个族群的话语过程；它会使正当移民和侨社承受不信任，并削弱打击真正犯罪网络的公众支持。

# Washington Targets Southeast Asia’s Scam Networks — and the Protectors Behind Them

According to the South China Morning Post, the US Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control announced sanctions on 19 November against three individuals and two companies over alleged links to scam compounds in Southeast Asia. The list includes Zhao Hui, a Cambodian businessman associated with Chen Zhi, founder of the Prince Group, as well as two leaders of Myanmar’s Democratic Karen Benevolent Army. Putting a businessman and local armed commanders on the same sheet is itself a judgment: Washington is not after a few phone calls. It is after a business model assembled from compounds, capital and guns.

## A sanctions list with two routes

The Treasury says Zhao leased a scam compound owned by the Prince Group and paid protection fees to Chen. He also operated a separate compound that targeted Americans and Europeans through virtual-currency investment scams. Two companies chaired by Zhao — a Cambodian construction and decoration firm and an investment consultancy — were designated alongside him. This route targets operators: who pays, who leases, and who turns a fenced compound into a cash machine.

The second route targets protectors. DKBA commander-in-chief Saw Shwe Wah and Colonel Saw A One, who oversees security at Three Pagodas Pass near the Thai border, were accused of providing security for scam compounds and taking part in violence against trafficked victims. The Treasury’s language is unusually blunt: without armed permission, the computers and scripts inside a compound do not run. Designating armed leaders as financial targets treats the protection racket as criminal infrastructure, not merely as a moral failing.

The specificity matters more than another broad anti-scam declaration. It acknowledges that a scam compound is not a single crime scene but a chain linking real estate, security, finance and cross-border recruitment. The catch is that naming people is only the opening move. What counts is whether their assets can be located and whether local authorities will help enforce the designation.

## US$18.2 billion and the politics of accountability

State Department spokesman Tommy Pigott said scam centres in Southeast Asia stole an estimated US$18.2 billion from Americans in 2025. A previous Treasury estimate put losses suffered by Americans at at least US$10 billion in 2024, up 66 per cent year on year. Such figures serve domestic accountability politics: voters lose savings, and lawmakers ask how compounds overseas could operate for so long.

But the numbers also establish three facts. First, the target has expanded from neighbouring societies to ordinary households in Europe and North America; distance no longer protects anyone in an age of instant messaging. Second, losses on this scale cannot be produced by a handful of street-level operators. They require accounts, payment corridors and laundering networks. Third, by quantifying American losses, Washington is constructing the case for extraterritorial enforcement: if US citizens are the principal victims, officials argue, sanctions can legitimately reach Phnom Penh and the Thai-Myanmar border.

## Dual nationality and the weaponisation of identity

Zhao’s case is especially revealing. The Treasury describes him as a Cambodian national, while sanctions records list China as an alternative nationality and identify Harbin as his birthplace. Nationality here is not merely emotional belonging; it is a legal instrument that governments use to claim jurisdiction, flag risk or cut ties. Chen’s trajectory is even starker. Cambodia arrested him in January at Beijing’s request and extradited him to China while revoking his citizenship; US prosecutors have separately charged him with conspiracy to commit wire fraud and money laundering.

Both facts can be true while telling different stories. Beijing stresses the transfer of a criminal suspect and the protection of citizens. Washington stresses financial sanctions and criminal prosecution. Phnom Penh, by stripping citizenship, signals that the man no longer belongs. When one individual holds several identities, which government acts first often depends on whose political agenda the case serves. For smaller states, that choice is not only legal; it is a bargain between diplomacy and investment.

## Convergence with China, divergent narratives

The SCMP report says that a day before the sanctions, the United States joined 12 other countries, including Australia, Canada, Japan and Vietnam, in a statement on cooperation against online scams, and announced a Threat Disruption Centre. Earlier, a senior State Department official said President Donald Trump had raised transnational criminal organisations in Southeast Asia with President Xi Jinping. China, too, has pressed Cambodia and other neighbours to curb telecom fraud. On the surface, this is a rare window of great-power cooperation.

Cooperation, however, is not the same as a shared narrative. US officials repeatedly connect the compounds to Chinese organised crime groups, while Beijing prefers the language of cross-border governance and victim rescue. The first framing, quoted carelessly, risks binding ordinary overseas Chinese to criminal networks. The second, if it stops at citizen protection, can sidestep local protection and corruption. Serious cooperation should be measured by verifiable standards: sharing beneficial-ownership data, harmonising victim-identification procedures, and tracing rent and protection payments — not by competing press conferences.

## Why these compounds are not ordinary crime scenes

Imagining scam compounds as internet cafés full of small-time cheats underestimates the problem. Victims are often lured across borders by promises of high wages, then have their passports confiscated and are forced to work under debt and threats of violence. The allegation that DKBA fighters guarded compounds and assaulted victims shows that trafficked people face not only a boss but an armed keeper. The compound is simultaneously a workshop producing fraud proceeds, a site of confinement and a rental asset for local armed groups.

This is why single-point crackdowns often fail. Close one compound and operators lease land on the other side of a border. Arrest a batch of operators and the recruiters, account suppliers and protection-payment chain remain. If land registries, corporate filings and bank due diligence examine paper documents but not beneficial owners or lease relationships, enforcement merely pushes the problem from one side of the map to the other.

## Can sanctions dismantle the protection racket?

Financial sanctions are fast and relatively cheap: they raise the cost of doing business for named targets. Their weakness is equally clear. They depend on the dollar-clearing system and bank compliance, and they rarely rescue anyone trapped inside a compound. Without parallel criminal investigations, victim repatriation and local anti-corruption work, designated companies can be renamed, commanders can use proxies, and rent will find new payees.

The deeper question is whether Washington is willing to apply the tool to the ecosystem rather than a few visible names. Property developers, registration agents, informal money movers and local officials who knowingly supply power or connectivity all belong inside the accountability frame. At the same time, overseas Chinese communities should distance themselves explicitly from the accused networks and support victim assistance, rather than allowing “Chinese-linked scams” to become an unspoken premise. Effective enforcement must punish criminals without converting an entire diaspora into suspects.

## Further reading

**Financial sanctions**: Freezing assets, banning transactions and restricting payment channels to raise the operating costs of named individuals or firms; effectiveness depends on clearing networks, bank compliance and international coordination.

**Protection economy**: A grey-market arrangement in which businesses pay local armed groups or corrupt officials for security and tacit permission. In scam compounds, protection money is an operating cost, not an extra.

**Diaspora stigmatisation**: The process of attributing the crimes of a few to an entire ethnic community. It harms legitimate migrants and undermines public support for targeting the real criminal networks.
