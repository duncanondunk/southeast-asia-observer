# 十五年与一纸赦免：贝洛索重获自由，照见菲律宾海外劳工的保护盲区

2026年9月25日，玛丽·简·贝洛索走出马尼拉曼达卢永女子惩教所，重获自由。十五年前的2010年4月，她登上飞往印尼的航班，以为自己要去当一名家政服务员。在日惹机场，海关从她行李箱的夹层里搜出2.6公斤海洛因——她坚称，那是把她送出国的中介偷偷塞进去的。她因贩毒罪被判死刑，2015年靠印尼时任总统佐科·维多多的一纸暂缓执行令捡回性命，因为印尼方面需要她出庭指证据称拐卖她的人。2024年12月，她被遣返回国；今年9月，菲律宾总统费迪南德·马科斯最终签署绝对赦免令。

贝洛索的获释，理所应当地被她的家人、律师以及声援了十余年的移民权益团体视作一场胜利。但《南华早报》的提法——她的案子"暴露了菲律宾海外劳工保护的缺口"——才更刺耳。一个人自由了，她跌落的那道裂缝却还敞着。

## 回家的路走了十五年

这起案件的轨迹，像一则关于"谋生如何迅速变成无期徒刑"的警示寓言。贝洛索离乡既不是为冒险，也不是为观光，而是为活下去：和千千万万同胞一样，她要靠微薄的工资养活孩子。在去阿联酋打过工之后，一名中介许诺给她一份在马来西亚的家政工作——结果却把她绕道送到印尼，行李箱里塞满了毒品。

这个细节很关键。欺骗并非发生在边境，而是发生在招聘阶段、在菲律宾境内，那正是准移民工人最脆弱、也最缺乏保护的时候。等贝洛索抵达日惹，陷阱早已合拢。

## 数字：东南亚最大的"劳务输出国"

无论以什么标准衡量，菲律宾都是本地区首屈一指的劳务输出国。菲律宾移民工人部（英文：Department of Migrant Workers）的数据显示，每年被派往海外工作的菲律宾人超过230万，集中在家政、海员、护理与建筑等行业。2025年，汇回国内的现金汇款约达380亿美元（约2700亿元人民币）——这笔钱如此庞大，已经成为国民经济的隐形支柱，也成了这套移民模式的政治护盾。

然而，正是这种"常态化"的规模，让风险也被常态化了。当出国去给别人打扫屋子、到别国船只上做水手被当成一条司空见惯的职业道路，国家保护这些工人的职责，就会悄悄退化为"出事之后再捞人"的职责。

## 保护网在哪失守

贝洛索的遭遇暴露出三类反复出现的失灵。其一，针对非法招聘的出国前筛查仍然薄弱：人贩子仍通过非正式网络，专挑穷人和走投无路者下手。其二，工人一旦在海外陷入麻烦，领事协助虽真实存在却参差不齐——而且几乎总是被动的，靠丑闻而非制度来启动。其三，国内对招聘者的追诉缓慢且难得结论；针对贝洛索据称的拐卖者的法律程序，一拖就是十多年。

这正是《南华早报》点出的模式。保护在纸面上是齐全的——有移民工人部、有双边劳工协定、有海外福利服务站——但它是一张看得见窟窿的安全网：兜得住一部分人，却有太多人从网眼里漏下去。

## 外交能救急，制度才能兜底

值得追问的是：究竟是谁真正救出了贝洛索？不是法院，而是一位总统的赦免令；也不单是菲律宾一个部门，而是一项和印尼的双边安排，外加多年的草根施压。她的自由，是靠学者所谓"救援外交"赢得的——在公民已经陷入绝境之后，才调动政治资本。

这总比没有强，对贝洛索而言更是性命攸关。但"救援外交"从定义上说就是例外。一个要靠总统特赦和国际声援去拯救个体性命的体系，等于把照护之责外包给了运气与名气。那些背后没有全球性运动的工人——这样的人多得多——依旧暴露在风险里。

## "劳务输出"模式的结构性原罪

更深层的问题是：一位两个孩子的母亲，为何非得拿性命去国外赌一口饭？加布里埃拉（英文：Gabriela）与移民运动组织"米格雷特国际"（英文：Migrante International）把菲律宾的做法称作"劳务输出政策"——一种实质上把就业外包给外国经济体的发展模式，因为国内体面、稳定的工作太少。贫困、低工资与薄弱的社会保障在"推"，招聘产业在"拉"。

正如国会议员在2026年10月5日的全体会议上所指出的，答案不只是加强海外保护，更是"在菲律宾本土创造生计，而非在海外"——提高生活工资、保障就业安全、改善国内劳动条件。在此之前，每一次赦免都只是给经济不断撕开的伤口贴创可贴。

## 赦免之后，重建才开始

贝洛索的故事并不终结于监狱大门。移民工人部正在筹备一项重返社会计划——心理支持、生计启动包、技能培训——因为在十五年羁押之后重获自由，本身就是一次过渡。重新连接几乎陌生的孩子、重建稳定感、在案件阴影之外找到工作：这些安静而不起眼的事，才决定"自由"二字究竟有没有分量。

她的案子也应当促使政府下决心，在下一个"贝洛索"登机之前就保护她。更有力的出国前审查、更快追诉非法招聘者、认真推动国内体面就业，比任何一纸赦免都更有用。

## 延伸阅读

- **海外菲律劳工（英文：Overseas Filipino Workers）**：每年约230万菲律宾人被派往海外工作，汇款是菲律宾经济的核心支柱。
- **劳务输出政策（英文：Labour Export Policy）**：加布里埃拉与米格雷特国际提出的批评，指马尼拉把向外移民当成发展战略，而非解决国内就业短缺。
- **非法招聘（英文：Illegal Recruitment）**：通过欺诈或拐卖招募移民工人——多数剥削就此开端，也是当前保护中最薄弱的一环。

# Fifteen Years and a Pardon: What Mary Jane Veloso's Freedom Reveals About the Philippines' Migrant-Worker Blind Spot

On 25 September 2026, Mary Jane Veloso walked out of the Correctional Institution for Women in Mandaluyong, a free woman. Fifteen years earlier, in April 2010, she had boarded a plane to Indonesia believing she was bound for a job as a domestic helper. At Yogyakarta airport, customs officers found 2.6 kilograms of heroin sewn into the lining of her suitcase — a load she says was planted there by the recruiters who sent her abroad. Convicted of drug trafficking and sentenced to death, she survived a 2015 reprieve only because then Indonesian president Joko Widodo needed her testimony against her alleged traffickers. She was transferred home in December 2024; this September, Philippine president Ferdinand Marcos Jr. finally granted her an absolute pardon.

Veloso's release was rightly celebrated as a victory by her family, her lawyers and the migrant-rights groups who campaigned for over a decade. But the framing from the South China Morning Post — that her case "exposes gaps in Filipino migrant labour protection" — is the more uncomfortable truth. One woman is free. The hole she fell through is still open.

## Fifteen years to come home

The arc of the case reads like a cautionary tale about how quickly a search for decent work can become a life sentence. Veloso left not for adventure but for survival: like millions of her compatriots, she was supporting children on wages that did not stretch. After a stint working in the United Arab Emirates, a recruiter promised her a household job in Malaysia — and instead routed her through Indonesia with a drug-filled suitcase.

That detail matters. The deception did not happen at the border. It happened in the recruitment phase, inside the Philippines, where would-be migrant workers are most exposed and least protected. By the time Veloso reached Yogyakarta, the trap had already shut.

## The numbers: Southeast Asia's largest labour exporter

By any measure, the Philippines is the region's flagship labour exporter. Data from the Department of Migrant Workers show that more than 2.3 million Filipinos are deployed overseas for work each year, concentrated in domestic work, seafaring, nursing and construction. In 2025, cash remittances sent home reached roughly US$38 billion — a sum so large it functions as a hidden pillar of the national economy and a political shield for the migration model itself.

Yet the very scale that makes overseas work "normal" also normalises the risk. When leaving the country to clean another family's house or crew another nation's ships is treated as a routine career path, the state's duty to protect those workers can quietly erode into a duty to retrieve them after something goes wrong.

## Where the safety net broke

Veloso's ordeal exposes three failures that repeat across cases. First, pre-deployment screening against illegal recruitment remains weak: traffickers still operate through informal networks that prey on the poor and the desperate. Second, once a worker is abroad and in trouble, consular help is real but uneven — and almost always reactive, mobilised by scandal rather than by system. Third, the prosecution of recruiters at home is slow and rarely conclusive; the legal proceedings against Veloso's alleged recruiters dragged on for over a decade.

This is the pattern the South China Morning Post highlights. The protection exists on paper — a Department of Migrant Workers, bilateral labour agreements, overseas welfare desks — but it is a safety net with visible holes: it catches some, and far too many slip through.

## Diplomacy rescues, institutions must catch

It is worth asking who actually freed Veloso. Not a court, but a president's pardon; not a single Philippine agency, but a bilateral deal with Indonesia plus years of grassroots pressure. Her freedom was won through what scholars call "rescue diplomacy" — the mobilisation of political capital only after a citizen is already in extremis.

That is better than nothing, and for Veloso it was everything. But rescue diplomacy is, by definition, exceptional. A system that relies on presidential clemency and international campaigning to save individual lives has outsourced its duty of care to fortune and fame. The workers without a global movement behind them — and there are far more of them — remain exposed.

## The structural sin of the labour-export model

The deeper question is why a mother of two had to gamble her life abroad to feed her children in the first place. Migrant-rights groups such as Gabriela and Migrante International call the Philippine approach a "labour-export policy" — a development model that effectively outsources employment to foreign economies because decent, secure jobs at home are scarce. Poverty, low wages and weak social protection do the pushing; the recruitment industry does the pulling.

As lawmakers noted in the House plenary on 5 October 2026, the answer is not only better protection abroad but "livelihood in the Philippines, not abroad" — living wages, job security and safe working conditions at home. Until that changes, every pardon is a bandage on a wound the economy keeps reopening.

## After the pardon, rebuilding begins

Veloso's story does not end at the prison gate. The Department of Migrant Workers is preparing a reintegration programme — psychosocial support, livelihood kits, skills training — because freedom after fifteen years of detention is itself a transition. Reconnecting with children she barely knows, rebuilding a sense of stability, finding work without the shadow of the case: these quiet, unglamorous tasks determine whether "free" means anything.

Her case should also sharpen the government's resolve to protect the next Mary Jane before she boards a plane. Stronger pre-deployment vetting, faster prosecution of illegal recruiters, and a serious push for decent domestic employment would do more than any pardon.

## Further reading

- **Overseas Filipino Workers (OFW)**: The roughly 2.3 million Filipinos deployed abroad each year; remittances are a core pillar of the Philippine economy.
- **Labour-export policy (劳务输出政策)**: A critique, advanced by Gabriela and Migrante International, that Manila treats emigration as a development strategy rather than fixing domestic job scarcity.
- **Illegal recruitment (非法招聘)**: The recruitment of migrant workers through fraud or trafficking — the phase where most exploitation begins, and the weakest link in current protection.
