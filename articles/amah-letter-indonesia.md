# 当银幕响起潮州话：一部电影与印尼华人的"记忆解冻"

2026年8月5日晚，纪录片《给阿嬷的情书》在雅加达首映。82岁的陈美专看完没有立刻离场——银幕上"木生"下南洋的故事，让她想起父亲从广东揭阳漂洋过海、在加里曼丹成家的往事。她在家一直讲潮汕话，却因印尼华校一度关闭，许多同辈渐渐听不懂祖辈的乡音。

这部电影在马来西亚、新加坡上映后，才轮到印尼。对一个曾长期禁止华文、华校与公开庆祝华人文化近三十年的国家而言，银幕上重新响起的潮州话与童谣，不只是一段怀旧，更像是一次被压抑记忆的"解冻"。

本文认为：离散记忆从不是自然延续的，它需要载体。当制度性沉默夺走了一代人的语言，电影、家书与侨乡纽带就成为为数不多的"记忆技术"。印尼华人的故事提醒我们：所谓"根"，往往要靠一代代人主动去重新听见。

# When the Screen Spoke Teochew: A Film and the Slow Thaw of Indonesia's Chinese Memory

On the evening of 5 August 2026, a documentary titled *A Letter to Amah* (*Gei Ama di Qing Shu*) held its premiere in Jakarta. When the lights came up, an eighty-two-year-old woman named Tan Mei Zhuan did not leave. She sat in the darkened hall, watching the credits, and murmured that the film's protagonist — a young man named Musheng who crosses the sea to Nanyang — had reminded her of her own father, who had left Guangdong's Jieyang county for Kalimantan in the 1930s and never stopped writing home.

It is a small, almost private moment. Yet it captures something larger about the Chinese diaspora in Southeast Asia, and specifically in Indonesia: how memory survives, how it is lost, and what it takes to recover it.

## A family story, and a national one

Tan's father was born in 1917 in Jieyang, in China's Chaoshan region, and boarded a ship for the Dutch East Indies at twenty-two. He had already married in his hometown; in Kalimantan he wed a local ethnic-Chinese woman, and Tan was born there. The sea, as the China News Service report puts it, divided his life into two halves. He sent letters and remittances to Guangdong for decades — sometimes with private "code words" legible only to the family — until war severed the correspondence. In the elders' vocabulary, *Tangshan* (唐山) meant not a city but *home*, meant China itself.

What makes this more than a touching anecdote is the linguistic detail. Tan attended Chinese-medium schools from kindergarten through high school and spoke Teochew (潮汕话) at home. At the premiere, whenever the film's soundtrack carried familiar Teochew lines and nursery rhymes, she quietly mouthed them along. "The Teochew in the movie is spoken correctly, just like how we speak it," she told the reporter. Her son noted that the film had already screened in Malaysia and Singapore; his mother had been asking, *when will it be Indonesia's turn?*

That last line is the hinge of the whole story.

## The silence that was imposed, not chosen

To understand why an Indonesian-Chinese family would wait for a film to reach them, one has to recall that Indonesia's ethnic Chinese did not simply drift away from their heritage. For roughly three decades, from the mid-1960s until the late 1990s, the Suharto-era state banned the public use of Chinese languages, closed Chinese schools, and criminalized the open expression of Chinese cultural life. A generation grew up unable to read the characters their grandparents wrote, unable to follow the dialect broadcasts their parents remembered.

This is why Tan's biography is so telling. She belongs to the cohort that *received* a full Chinese education before the closures — kindergarten to high school in Chinese schools — and who therefore still carries the language as muscle memory. The "silence" fell on those who came after her: the children who, as the report gently notes, "gradually could not understand the ancestral accents." Memory was not abandoned; it was interrupted by policy.

Scholars of diaspora have long distinguished between *forgetting* and *silencing*. Forgetting is organic, the slow erosion of what no one rehearses. Silencing is structural — memory suppressed because the cost of voicing it is too high. Indonesia's Chinese community lived the second condition for a generation. What looks like assimilation from the outside was, for many families, a protective withdrawal.

## Film as a technology of memory

Here the documentary does something quietly political. It does not stage a protest; it stages a *reunion* — between a grandmother and a language, between a younger audience and a history they were never formally taught. In that sense the film functions as what we might call a technology of memory: a material artifact that re-attaches a community to its own past when the institutions that once did that work (schools, associations, newspapers) were dismantled.

The "letter to amah" of the title is doubly resonant. On one level it echoes the *qiaopi* (侨批) tradition — the transoceanic family correspondence and remittance letters that bound overseas Chinese to their natal villages for over a century, and which UNESCO inscribed on the Memory of the World register in 2013. Tan's father's coded letters are a direct descendant of that practice. On another level, the "letter" is the film itself: a message sent backward in time, addressed to the grandmothers who carried the memory and to the grandchildren who lost the key to it.

That the film reached Malaysia and Singapore first, and only later Indonesia, is not an accident of distribution. It reflects divergent post-colonial trajectories. In Singapore, Chinese language and culture are woven into state identity. In Malaysia, a robust vernacular-school system kept Chinese education alive despite pressures. Indonesia, having suppressed that infrastructure, is only now — a quarter-century after reformasi — beginning to reopen the cultural archive. The staggered release is, in miniature, the staggered recovery of memory across the region.

## The limits of a single screening

It would be sentimental to overstate what one documentary can do. A premiere in Jakarta does not reopen the Chinese schools, does not restore the dialects that dissolved under prohibition, does not undo the suspicion that attached to Chinese identity for so long. Tan's ability to recite Teochew along with the screen is the exception that proves the rule: she had the luck of a pre-ban education and a family that kept speaking at home.

Yet the scene also suggests a pathway. Memory, once silenced, does not return through policy alone. It returns through repetition — a song hummed, a letter read aloud, a film watched with a parent. The son who accompanied his mother, and who remembered her longing for the film, is part of that chain. So is the classroom in Jieyang that Tan's father donated and that his children later visited: a physical tie between diaspora and *Tangshan* that outlasted the silence.

## A restrained reading

For a blog that observes Southeast Asia rather than advocates, the lesson is modest but durable. The Chinese diaspora in Indonesia is not a fixed quantity — not "assimilated" nor "preserved" in any final sense. It is a memory under continuous negotiation, alternately suppressed and recovered, and the recovery now underway is being led not by the state but by culture: by a film, a dialect, a letter.

The eighty-two-year-old who stayed in her seat after the credits is not a relic of a lost past. She is evidence that the past was never fully lost — only waiting, in a language, for someone to press play.
