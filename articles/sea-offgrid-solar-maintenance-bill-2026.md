# 太阳能点亮村庄之后：谁为东南亚（Southeast Asia）绿色转型的「第二张账单」买单

## 一盏灯与一个村庄

对大多数时候的奥斯曼·乌基（Osman Uki）而言，一天的长度是由太阳决定的。据《南华早报》（South China Morning Post）报道，在距吉隆坡（Kuala Lumpur）约170公里的马来西亚彭亨州原住民村庄甘榜勿隆（Kampung Bertang），天黑意味着煤油灯和残油灯。今年4月，离网太阳能进了村，一切才改变。他告诉该报：「我们很高兴晚上有灯了，孩子们现在能在夜里读书。我们只用一盏灯，对他们来说就够了。」

这是一句很轻的话，却承载了很重的命题。能源转型通常被放在太瓦时和条约文本里讨论，但它真正的起点，是一个孩子天黑后还能不能看书。然而故事并未在开关合上的那一刻结束：光伏板是捐来的，灯却不是免费的。真正的问题在第二年、第五年——当捐赠者离场、电池老化，谁继续付账？

## 数字：470亿美元与1000亿美元之间

在2024年的第29届联合国气候变化大会上，发达国家同意带头行动，力争到2035年为发展中国家每年动员3000亿美元（约2.16万亿元人民币）气候资金。本周在土耳其安塔利亚开幕的第31届大会上，这个醒目的数字再次成为雄心的通用货币。

但国际能源署的数据是另一副面孔：2025年东南亚清洁能源投资仅470亿美元（约3384亿元人民币），而2026年至2030年要兑现区域气候承诺，每年需要约1000亿美元（约7200亿元人民币）。缺口不是四舍五入的误差，而是一种融资模式的必然结果：这种模式只计算第一张支票，却忘了随后十年的账单。

## 每户4930美元之后的分期付款

甘榜勿隆的离网太阳能，每户安装成本约4930美元（约3.55万元人民币）。项目由社会企业明点能源（Bright Spots Energy）与一家工程公司合作执行，资金来自哈萨纳基金会（Yayasan Hasanah）。摄像机拍完剪彩之后，真正的安排才开始：每户每月向社区基金缴纳7美元（约50元人民币），用于维修、维护，并向村能源委员会支付象征性报酬。电池更换预计在5年内到期，每套系统超过980美元（约7056元人民币），由各户分摊。

这里的7美元不是通常意义上的电价，而是一笔由最缺乏现金收入的人缴纳的保险费。村委员会成员哈西尔·金提亚（Hasil Gentiah）今年51岁，他说村民们把割胶、采摘果实和其他作物的收入凑在一起，才交得出这笔月费。换句话说，能源转型的成本被巧妙地拆散了：资本开支由捐助方出，运营开支由村民出。分工看起来很合理，账却未必公平。

## 「一两年就坏」的诅咒

「太阳能项目建好、装好，一两年后不工作、被弃置，因为没人懂维护和运营——这种情况非常、非常常见。」项目方联合创始人兼首席运营官叶美怡（Yeap Mei Yi）这样说。她自2020年从事农村电气化，估计西马半岛仍有近200个原住民村庄没有接入电网。

这句话是农村电气化领域公开的秘密，却很少出现在气候大会的成就清单里。捐助周期以项目为单位，电池寿命以年为单位，两者根本不同步。甘榜勿隆的应对办法是设立村能源委员会、汇集村民储蓄，让村民能独立操作和维护系统。这个设计很务实，值得肯定；但它也把制度记忆的负担转嫁给了最穷的人。捐助机构的五年规划会结束，电池不会因此不坏。

## 谁付钱，谁受益

把镜头拉远，这笔账在东南亚并不陌生。马尼拉（Manila）智库能源、生态与发展中心的数据显示，2025年菲律宾（Philippines）发电量中煤与天然气仍占74%。该中心执行主任杰里·阿兰塞斯（Gerry Arances）说，对普通菲律宾家庭而言，昂贵能源的负担不止于电费，它会传导为更高的交通费、食品价格和生活成本，对低收入家庭的打击最重。

这才是「谁付费」问题的真正含义：对贫困家庭，能源从来不是账单上的一行数字，而是整张生活成本表的底色。一场降低了全球排放、却抬高了本地经常性支出的绿色转型，本质上是一笔累退的交易。如果甘榜勿隆的孩子能在夜里读书，而五年后村庄必须在换电池和交学费之间做选择，那么这个项目只是把不公平推到了下游，并没有消除它。

## 从「补贴装机」到「补贴用电」

在本届气候大会上，气候政策倡议组织印尼（Indonesia）分部提出「公正能源转型基金」，设计了三个资金窗口：弥合可行性缺口、为运营维护提供技能提升培训、支持能源的生产性使用。印尼要实现2060年净零目标，每年需要约303亿美元（约2182亿元人民币）投资，而2019年至2023年清洁电力年均投资仅12亿美元（约86亿元人民币）。

三个窗口里，第三个最值得注意。太阳能板如果没有可以带动的生产活动，就只是半个项目：它让灯亮起来，却不能让村庄多挣一笔钱去支付那7美元。评价一个项目，标准不该是首年并网了多少兆瓦，而应是第十年它是否还在发电。把维护信托、本地技术员培养和生产性用途写进初始融资，才算把「公正」二字从口号变成条款。

## 大电网救不了最后的村庄

区域互联的宏观方案当然重要：东盟（ASEAN）电网与跨境输电线路能让电力在经济中心之间流动，也能消纳可再生能源的富余电量。但高压线不会自动修好距首都170公里处一块失效的逆变器，更多时候，重大项目反而吸走了本该用于本地维护的注意力和人才。

这里有一种政治经济学：剪彩上镜，维护不上镜。第31届气候大会真正该交出的，不是更大的认捐数字，而是另一种承诺——为运维设立专项基金，为社区培养技术员，为用电找到创收场景，让村民不必牺牲别的开支也能交出自己的那份钱。否则，灯会亮起，然后终有一天，再灭掉。东南亚的绿色转型不缺光伏板，缺的是让光伏板一直工作的制度。

## 延伸阅读

**公正转型（Just transition）**：能源转型中对就业、生计与分配公平的安排，强调不能让依赖化石能源的社区单独承担转型成本。

**能源可及性（Energy access）**：衡量人口能否获得可靠、可负担、清洁的电力供应，是联合国可持续发展目标第七项的核心。

**能源生产性使用（Productive use of energy）**：把电力用于加工、冷链、灌溉等创收活动，使社区有能力自行负担电费与维护费。

# After the lights come on: who pays Southeast Asia's second green bill

## A village and a single light bulb

For most of his life, Osman Uki measured the day by the sun. In Kampung Bertang, an Orang Asli village roughly 170 km from Kuala Lumpur in the state of Pahang, darkness once meant kerosene and leftover cooking oil. That changed in April, when off-grid solar finally reached the village, according to the South China Morning Post. "We are happy with the light we have at night. The kids can read at night now. We only use one light and that is enough for them," he told the paper.

It is a small sentence with a large claim. The energy transition is usually discussed in terawatts and treaty text, but its real starting point is whether a child can read after dark. The story, however, does not end when the switch is flipped. The panels were donated; the light is not free. The real question arrives in year two and year five, when the donors have left and the batteries begin to age: who keeps paying?

## The numbers: between US$47 billion and US$100 billion

At COP29 in 2024, developed countries agreed to lead efforts to mobilise US$300 billion a year in climate finance for developing countries by 2035. At COP31, opening this week in Antalya, Turkey, that headline figure is once again the currency of ambition.

The International Energy Agency tells a different story. Clean-energy investment in Southeast Asia reached just US$47 billion in 2025, against roughly US$100 billion a year needed between 2026 and 2030 to keep regional climate commitments on track. This gap is not a rounding error. It is the predictable result of a financing model that counts the first cheque and forgets the next ten years of bills.

## The instalments after US$4,930 per household

In Kampung Bertang, installing off-grid solar cost about US$4,930 per household, according to Yeap Mei Yi, co-founder and chief operating officer of Bright Spots Energy. The social enterprise carried out the project with SHER Engineering, with financial backing from Yayasan Hasanah. Once the ribbon was cut, the real arrangement began: each household contributes US$7 a month to a community fund covering repairs, maintenance and modest payments to the village energy committee. Battery replacement, expected within five years, is estimated at more than US$980 per system, shared across households.

That US$7 is not a tariff in the ordinary sense. It is an insurance premium paid by people whose cash income comes from tapping rubber and gathering fruit. Hasil Gentiah, 51, a member of the village committee, said residents pooled their earnings from rubber and produce to meet the monthly contribution. The division of labour looks neat — capital expenditure from donors, operating expenditure from villagers — but the fairness of that division is another matter.

## The curse of dying after a year

"It's very, very common to see a solar project that is built, installed and then within a year or two, it's not working and abandoned because nobody knows how to do maintenance and operations," Yeap said. She has worked on rural electrification since 2020 and estimates that nearly 200 Orang Asli villages across Peninsular Malaysia still lack grid access.

This is the open secret of rural electrification, rarely listed among the achievements at climate summits. Donor cycles are measured in project periods; battery life is measured in years. The two are not synchronised. Kampung Bertang's answer — a village energy committee, pooled savings and systems villagers can operate themselves — is sensible and worth crediting. Yet it also transfers the burden of institutional memory to the poorest participants. A donor's five-year plan ends; the battery does not therefore stop degrading.

## Who pays, who benefits

Widen the lens and this balance sheet looks familiar across Southeast Asia. The Centre for Energy, Ecology and Development, a Manila-based think tank, reports that coal and natural gas still accounted for 74 per cent of Philippine electricity generation in 2025. "For ordinary Filipino families, the burden of expensive energy goes beyond electricity bills. It translates into higher transportation fares, food prices and living expenses, hitting low-income households the hardest," said its executive director Gerry Arances.

That is what "who pays" really means. For poor households, energy is never a single line on a bill; it is the backdrop of the entire cost-of-living table. A green transition that lowers global emissions while raising local recurring costs is a regressive bargain. If the children of Kampung Bertang can read at night, but five years later the village must choose between new batteries and school fees, the project has merely pushed inequity downstream rather than removed it.

## From subsidising installation to subsidising use

At COP31, Climate Policy Initiative Indonesia proposed a Just Energy Transition Fund with three windows: viability gap funding, upskilling and reskilling for operation and maintenance, and productive use of energy. Indonesia needs roughly US$30.3 billion a year to reach net zero by 2060, yet clean-power investment averaged only US$1.2 billion a year between 2019 and 2023.

Of the three windows, the third deserves the most attention. A solar panel with nothing productive to run is only half a project: it lights the house but does not help the village earn the money to pay its own US$7. A project should be judged not by how many megawatts were commissioned in year one, but by whether the system is still generating in year ten. Writing maintenance endowments, local technician pipelines and productive-use venues into initial financing is how "justice" stops being a slogan and becomes a clause.

## The mega-grid cannot reach the last village

Macro-level interconnection matters, of course. The ASEAN Power Grid and cross-border transmission lines move electricity between economic centres and absorb surplus renewable output. But a high-voltage line will not automatically repair a failed inverter 170 km from the capital; indeed, megaprojects can siphon away the attention and technical talent that local maintenance badly needs.

There is a political economy here: ribbon-cuttings are photogenic, maintenance is not. What COP31 should deliver is not a larger pledge but a different kind of pledge — dedicated maintenance funds, technician training for communities, and income-generating uses of power that let villagers pay their share without sacrificing something else. Otherwise the lights will come on, and then one day go off again. Southeast Asia does not lack solar panels; it lacks the institutions that keep them working.

## Further reading

**Just transition**: arrangements in energy transition covering employment, livelihoods and distributive fairness, insisting that communities dependent on fossil fuels should not bear transition costs alone.

**Energy access**: whether populations can obtain reliable, affordable and clean electricity — the core of Sustainable Development Goal 7.

**Productive use of energy**: channelling power into processing, cold chains and irrigation so that communities can afford their own energy bills and maintenance costs.
