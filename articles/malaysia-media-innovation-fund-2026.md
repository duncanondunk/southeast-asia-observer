# 马来西亚3000万令吉媒体续命金：是救济，还是缓刑？

马来西亚（Malaysia）政府推出"媒体创新基金2.0"，承诺拨款3000万令吉（约940万新元，分别约合人民币5100万元与5070万元），救助陷入困境的本国新闻业加速数字化转型。通讯部长法米·法兹尔于10月31日宣布这项计划，11月1日起开放申请。表面看，这是一场受欢迎的救援。但若往深里看，一个更尖锐的问题浮现：在商业模式已被硅谷拆解的当下，一笔一次性补助，无论用意多好，真能救活这个行业吗？

## 背景：一纸基金的来龙去脉

马来西亚的传统媒体已失血十年。原因并不神秘。正如一家主流报业集团总编辑所指出的，社媒巨擘Meta、谷歌和TikTok如今攫取了广告市场的大头，把残羹剩饭留给传统媒体。在财务重压下，新闻机构优先保住工资支出，转而削减培训、设备和软件开支。后果是：报道变稀薄、采编队伍老化、员工时刻担心下一轮裁员。据称，首要媒体集团已将去年首轮拨款用于技术培训与移动采编工具包——有用，但只是杯水车薪。

## 条件：不裁员才给钱

这项基金最特别之处，在于它附带条件。法米明确表示，接受补助的媒体机构即便财务困难，也不得任意裁员。马新社总执行长努鲁阿菲达欢迎这一条件，称该基金是政府为保全国家新闻业未来所作的战略投资，而非单纯财务援助。逻辑站得住：一笔反倒补贴了裁员的拨款，会自毁初衷。可"不裁员"条款也暴露了政府的尴尬——它等于默认，若无国家出手，某些媒体早已在裁人。

## 数字：3000万令吉与940万新元

3000万令吉听着不少。但若摊到涵盖国家通讯社、私营传媒集团、华文报、淡米尔文报与马来文报的整个媒体版图，便显得单薄。基金涵盖五大援助范畴：人工智能技术应用、培训、设备采购等。首要媒体新闻与时事部门副执行总编辑法拉纳兹坦言，受集团年度预算所限，若无资金支持，转型所需时间会漫长得多。换言之，这笔钱并未填补结构性缺口，只是买到了时间。

## 华文媒体：转型中坚守语言与文化

这一部分，才是与东南亚（Southeast Asia）华侨华人最相关之处。星洲日报总编辑陈汉光表示，该基金让马来西亚媒体得以加快数码化转型、拓展新读者群，同时保留各自的语言文化特色与编辑风格。这句话，是整篇报道安静的内核。马来西亚的华文媒体从来不是商业奇观：它是大中华以外硕果仅存、仍能大众发行的中文报业生态之一，是离散族群文化传承的支柱。当一家淡米尔文报的总编辑说，这笔财政援助对推动传统报章转向数码平台、维持营运、保障少数族裔媒体从业者福祉"至关重要"时，基金便显露出更深的功能——在日益整合的市场里，保住少数族裔语言新闻的命脉。

## 隐忧：治标能否治本

观察者不应把救济误认为改革。一张政府支票，既无法唤回流向全球平台的广告收入，也无法修补多年来因所有权集中与政治压力而侵蚀的信任。更糟的是，对政府资金的依赖，可能把媒体变成它所应监督的权力的"客户"。马来西亚并非孤例——从雅加达（Jakarta）到马尼拉（Manila），整个地区的传统媒体都在乞求喘息空间，而科技垄断者却把收益尽收囊中。这笔基金是必要的，但远不充分。

## 延伸阅读

- **媒体创新基金（Media Innovation Fund）**：马来西亚政府先后推出的1.0与2.0补助计划，旨在补贴新闻编辑室数字化转型、培训与设备采购。
- **东南亚华文媒体（Chinese-language media in Southeast Asia）**：马来西亚的星洲日报、南洋商报、光明日报等，构成区域内最具韧性的中文公共舆论场之一。
- **平台广告主导（Platform ad dominance）**：数字广告收入向Meta、谷歌、TikTok集中，全球传统出版机构被动让利的趋势。

# Malaysia's RM30 Million Media Lifeline: Relief, or a Reprieve?

Malaysia's government has rolled out the Media Innovation Fund 2.0, committing RM30 million (about S$9.4 million, roughly US$6.9 million and US$7.0 million respectively) to help the country's besieged newsrooms go digital. Communications Minister Fahmi Fadzil announced the package on 31 October, with applications opening on 1 November. At first glance it looks like a welcome rescue. But scratch the surface and a harder question emerges: can a one-off grant, however well-meaning, save an industry whose business model was dismantled by Silicon Valley?

## The backdrop: where the fund came from

Traditional Malaysian media has been bleeding for a decade. The cause is no mystery. As one group editor-in-chief put it, social-media giants Meta, Google and TikTok now capture the lion's share of advertising revenue, leaving legacy outlets to fight over the crumbs. Under financial pressure, newsrooms protected payroll first and cut training, equipment and software. The result: thinner reporting, an ageing newsgathering workforce, and staff who live in dread of the next round of layoffs. Media Prima reportedly used last year's first tranche for technical training and mobile reporting kits — useful, but a drop in the ocean.

## The condition: no layoffs, or no money

What sets this fund apart is its strings. Fahmi made clear that recipients must not arbitrarily cut staff even when financially strained. Bernama chief executive Nurul Arfida welcomed the condition, calling the fund a strategic investment in the future of the nation's journalism, not mere financial aid. The logic holds: a grant that simply subsidises redundancy would defeat its own purpose. Yet the no-layoff clause also exposes the government's awkward position — it amounts to an admission that, without state help, some outlets would already be shedding jobs.

## The numbers: RM30 million and S$9.4 million

Thirty million ringgit sounds substantial. Spread across a media landscape that includes the national news agency, private conglomerates, Chinese-language dailies, Tamil and Malay presses, it is modest. The fund covers five categories: AI application, training, equipment procurement and related needs. Media Prima's news chief Fara Natasya noted that, given annual budget constraints, transformation without such support would take far longer. In other words, the money does not close the structural gap; it buys time.

## Chinese-language press: holding the line on language and culture

This is the part that matters most for Southeast Asia's overseas Chinese. Sin Chew Daily (星洲日报) editor-in-chief Tan Hong Kong said the fund lets Malaysian media accelerate digital transformation and reach new readers while preserving each outlet's language, cultural character and editorial style. That sentence is the quiet heart of the story. Malaysia's Chinese-language press is no commercial curiosity: it is one of the last mass-circulation Chinese newspaper ecosystems outside Greater China, a pillar of diaspora cultural continuity. When a Tamil paper's editor says the aid is "crucial" for moving traditional publications online, sustaining operations and safeguarding minority-media workers' welfare, the fund reveals its deeper function — keeping minority-language journalism alive in a consolidating market.

## The worry: can relief cure the disease?

Observers should not mistake relief for reform. A government cheque cannot restore the advertising revenue that migrated to global platforms, nor rebuild the trust eroded by years of ownership concentration and political pressure. Worse, dependence on state funds risks turning the press into a client of the very power it should scrutinise. Malaysia is not alone — from Jakarta to Manila, legacy media across the region begs for breathing room while tech monopolies pocket the proceeds. The fund is necessary. It is not sufficient.

- **Platform ad dominance**: The concentration of digital advertising revenue in Meta, Google and TikTok at the expense of legacy publishers worldwide.
