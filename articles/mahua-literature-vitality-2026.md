# 走过百年，马华文学为何仍有生命力？

## 一部小说的启示：地方经验如何跨越边界

最近几年，马来西亚华文作家黎紫书的小说《流俗地》在中文世界引发了一股不小的阅读热潮。这部以她故乡怡保为背景的作品，写锡矿历史的余温、华人社区的日常、粤语方言的肌理，以及跨族群之间微妙的关系。它于2020年先在中国文学期刊《山花》刊载，随后在马来西亚与海峡两岸暨香港陆续出版，并拿下多项文学奖。

值得玩味的是：恰恰因为它足够「在地」，它才打动了天南地北的华文读者。怡保的骑楼、咖啡店的喧嚣、一个普通家庭的生老病死，这些经验并不专属马来西亚，却因为被写得足够具体，反而成了所有人都能照见自己的镜子。马华文学最安静的胜利，正在于此——它的通行证不是「够不够中国」，而是「够不够诚实」。

这其实戳破了一个陈旧假设：海外华文文学不是母国文学的支流。北京与台北的市场之所以接纳马华写作，不是因为它「够中国」，而是因为它「不可还原地属于马来西亚」。

## 从「中国书写」到「马来西亚主体」：一场百年的转向

马华文学的源头，与十九世纪以来华侨华人从中国南来密不可分。移民带去的不仅是劳动力，还有汉字、方言、传统文化、教育与报刊。早期南来文人的作品，大多带着浓重的中国意识与故园情怀；他们身在南洋，精神上的文化中心却仍指向故土。五四与新文化运动之后，中国新文学进一步滋养了南洋文坛。

然而，随着移民逐渐落地生根，尤其是经历第二次世界大战与马来亚独立前后的社会剧变，写作者开始重新打量脚下这片土地。南洋不再只是暂居的驿站，而成了真正生活的家园。马华文学由此从故国的想象，转向本地社会的现实经验，开始追问国家认同、族群关系与自身文化身份。这一步至关重要：它意味着马华文学不再只是中国文学在海外的延伸，而是长出了属于自身的历史、社会经验与文化主体性。

从一个观察者的角度看，这不是「华人性」的流失，而是能动性的获得。离散创作的独特价值，恰恰在于它那双重的站位——既不完全在中国之内，也不完全在中国之外。

## 自足的文学生态：华教、报刊与文学馆

受访学者许通元点出了一个关键：马华文学的生命力，不来自个别的天才作家，而来自一套相对完整、长期经营的文学生态。这套生态从华文教育、华文报刊、文艺副刊起步，逐渐生长出文学社团、文学刊物、出版社、文学奖项，以及文学研究与文学史书写。作家有发表的平台，读者有接触的渠道，研究者则通过整理与评论，把作品送进更长远的文学记忆。

其中最具象征意义的，是南方大学学院马华文学馆。它于1998年4月26日正式成立，是马来西亚第一间文学馆，宗旨就是系统收藏、整理与保存马华文学作品和史料。如今馆内珍藏本室单行本一万三千多册、阅览室单行本一万二千多册、期刊一万多册，最早的珍贵藏品包括谭云山1930年出版的诗集《海畔》。文学馆还于2002年复刊了马华文学史上重要的纯文艺期刊《蕉风》，并持续出版至今。

这给所有离散社群提了个醒：文化存续的笨功夫，是建机构。哪里愿意投下档案馆、课程与刊物，哪里的文化就不再仰赖某个遥远中心的善意。

## 多语土壤里长出的「马来西亚味道」

马来西亚长期受到马来、中华、印度与西方文化的交叠影响，加上殖民历史、宗教传统与不同族群的生活方式，构成了一幅极为丰富的文化图景。对许多华人而言，通晓三四门语言并不稀奇——福建话、粤语、潮州话、客家话、海南话等方言，与日常生活里的马来语、英语交织共存。

这些语言并非彼此隔绝，而是共同活在家庭、学校、巴刹、咖啡店与街巷之中，慢慢沉淀出独特的表达习惯与生活记忆。马华文学的语言，正是在本地生活里不断吸收、不断变化的结果。热带的自然景观、植物、饮食、民间信仰，以及采矿与种植业的历史，也都化为创作的矿藏。它们不只是背景板，而是马来西亚人生活经验的一部分——这种「马来西亚味道」，别处无法轻易复制。

放在全球文化趋同的焦虑里看，这恰恰是马华文学给出的回答：一种扎进具体语言混杂与气候里的文学，正是最能抵抗扁平化的文学。

## 人工智能时代：离散文学如何续命

社交媒体与人工智能固然改变了阅读习惯，却也为文学打开了新的传播窗口。马华文学馆通过朗诵、讲座与跨领域活动，让文学走出书本，触达更年轻的读者。许通元给出的续命条件很朴素：只要持续回应现实、不断探索新的表达方式，马华文学就仍有旺盛的生命力。

回望其创作脉络，从早期现实主义，到后来的现代主义、后现代主义与后殖民书写，再到全球化时代的离散经验与「新南方写作」，马华文学一直在根据时代调整自己的方向。真正的威胁从来不是人工智能，而是停滞。只要马华写作仍在消化新的现实——迁徙、气候、数字生活、起伏的族群政治——它就不会死。离散者最大的本钱，从来就是被迫不断重新发明「归属」。

## 延伸阅读

**马华文学（Mahua Literature）**：指在马来西亚以华文创作、并形成自身主体性的文学系统，广义亦涵盖白话中文文学、古典中文文学、峇峇文学、华裔英文与马来文文学等多元脉络，而非中国文学在海外的简单延伸。

**南方大学学院马华文学馆**：1998年成立的马来西亚首间文学馆，系统典藏与整理马华文学作品及史料，并于2002年复刊重要纯文艺期刊《蕉风》，是马华文学传承的核心机构。

**《蕉风》**：马来西亚华文纯文艺期刊，在马华文学史上地位举足轻重；2002年由马华文学馆复刊后持续出版，成为连接老中青三代写作者的重要园地。

# A Century On, Mahua Literature Still Breathes

## A novel's lesson: how local experience crosses borders

In recent years, the Malaysian Chinese writer Li Zishu's novel *Li Su Di* (2020) sparked a quiet reading boom across the Chinese-speaking world. Set in her hometown of Ipoh, the book writes about the lingering warmth of tin-mining history, the everyday life of Chinese communities, the texture of Cantonese dialect, and the subtle relations between ethnic groups. It first appeared in 2020 in the Chinese literary journal *Mountain Flowers*, then was published in Malaysia, mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong, collecting several literary awards along the way.

What is worth pondering is this: precisely because it is so *local*, it moved readers far and wide. The shophouses of Ipoh, the clamour of coffee shops, the birth, illness, and death of an ordinary family — these experiences belong to no single country, yet because they are written with such specificity, they become a mirror in which anyone can recognise themselves. The quietest triumph of Mahua literature lies right here: its passport is not "Chinese enough," but "honest enough."

This punctures an old assumption. Overseas Chinese-language literature is not a tributary of the homeland canon. The markets in Beijing and Taipei embraced Mahua writing not because it was "Chinese enough," but because it was irreducibly Malaysian.

## From "China writing" to a Malaysian subject: a century's turn

The origins of Mahua literature are inseparable from the southward migration of overseas Chinese from China since the nineteenth century. What the migrants brought was not only labour, but also Chinese characters, dialects, traditional culture, education, and the press. The early "Nanyang" writers mostly carried a strong China consciousness and a longing for the homeland; though physically in Southeast Asia, their cultural centre of gravity still pointed northward. After the May Fourth and New Culture movements, China's new literature further nourished the Nanyang literary scene.

Yet as migrants put down roots — especially after the Second World War and the social upheavals around Malayan independence — writers began to reconsider the land beneath their feet. Nanyang ceased to be a temporary station and became a real home. Mahua literature thus turned from the imagination of the motherland toward the lived reality of local society, beginning to interrogate national identity, ethnic relations, and its own cultural selfhood. This step was decisive: it meant Mahua literature was no longer merely an extension of Chinese literature abroad, but had grown a historicity and subjectivity of its own.

From an observer's vantage point, this is not a loss of "Chineseness" but a gain of agency. The distinctive value of diaspora writing lies precisely in its double position — never fully inside China, never fully outside it.

## A self-sustaining literary ecology: schools, newspapers, museums

The scholar interviewed, Xu Tongyuan, points to a key truth: the vitality of Mahua literature does not come from individual genius writers, but from a relatively complete and long-cultivated literary ecosystem. This ecology began with Chinese-language education, Chinese newspapers, and literary supplements, and gradually grew literary societies, journals, publishers, awards, as well as literary scholarship and the writing of literary history. Writers have platforms to publish, readers have channels to encounter texts, and researchers, through archiving and criticism, send works into a longer literary memory.

The most symbolic institution is the Mahua Literature Museum at Southern University College. Founded on 26 April 1998, it was Malaysia's first literature museum, dedicated to systematically collecting, organising, and preserving Mahua literary works and historical materials. Today it holds more than 13,000 collected volumes in its rare-book room, over 12,000 reading-room volumes, and more than 10,000 periodicals; its earliest treasure is the poetry collection *Hai Pan* by Tan Yunshan, published in 1930. The museum also revived *Jiaofeng*, one of the most important pure-literature journals in Mahua history, in 2002, and it has continued publishing since.

This is a reminder to every diaspora community: the unglamorous secret of cultural survival is institution-building. Where communities invest in archives, curricula, and journals, their culture stops depending on the goodwill of a distant centre.

## A "Malaysian flavour" grown in a multilingual soil

Malaysia has long been shaped by the overlapping influences of Malay, Chinese, Indian, and Western cultures, compounded by colonial history, religious traditions, and the ways of life of different ethnic groups, producing an extraordinarily rich cultural landscape. For many ethnic Chinese, commanding three or four languages is unremarkable — Hokkien, Cantonese, Teochew, Hakka, and Hainanese dialects interweave with the Malay and English of daily life.

These languages are not sealed off from one another; they coexist in the family, the school, the market, the coffee shop, and the street, slowly sedimenting into distinctive habits of expression and memory. The language of Mahua literature is the result of constant absorption and change within local life. The tropical landscape, its plants, its food, its folk religions, and the history of mining and plantation have all become creative ore. They are not mere backdrop but part of lived Malaysian experience — a "Malaysian flavour" that cannot be easily replicated elsewhere.

Set against the global anxiety of cultural homogenisation, this is exactly the answer Mahua literature offers: a literature rooted in a specific creole of languages and climates is precisely the kind that resists flattening.

## In the age of AI: how diaspora literature stays alive

Social media and artificial intelligence have certainly changed reading habits, yet they also open new channels of distribution. The Mahua Literature Museum reaches younger readers through recitals, lectures, and cross-disciplinary events. The condition for staying alive that Xu Tongyuan offers is plain: as long as Mahua literature keeps responding to reality and exploring new forms of expression, it will retain its vitality.

Looking back at its creative trajectory — from early realism to later modernism, postmodernism, and postcolonial writing, and on to the diaspora experience and "new southern writing" of the globalised era — Mahua literature has never stopped adjusting its direction to the times. The real threat was never artificial intelligence, but inertia. So long as Mahua writing keeps metabolising new reality — migration, climate, digital life, the shifting ethnic politics — it will not die. The diaspora's greatest asset has always been its forced, repeated reinvention of "belonging."

## Further reading

**Mahua Literature (马华文学)**: The body of Chinese-language writing produced in Malaysia that has developed its own subjectivity; broadly it also encompasses vernacular and classical Chinese literature, Baba literature, and ethnic-Chinese writing in English and Malay — a distinct literary system rather than a simple extension of mainland Chinese literature.

**Southern University College Mahua Literature Museum**: Founded in 1998 as Malaysia's first literature museum, it systematically archives Mahua works and historical materials and revived the key pure-literature journal *Jiaofeng* in 2002; it is the core institution for transmitting Mahua literature.

**Jiaofeng (《蕉风》)**: A long-running Malaysian Chinese pure-literature journal of central importance in Mahua history; revived by the Mahua Literature Museum in 2002 and still published, it remains a vital ground linking three generations of writers.
