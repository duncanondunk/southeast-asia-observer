# 黄衫军元老林明达再上街：泰国街头政治会否重燃？

现年78岁的林明达（Sondhi Limthongkul）是泰国传媒大亨、保皇派"黄衫军"运动的发起人。二十年前，他领导的大规模示威曾导致曼谷机场瘫痪，成为时任他信政府倒台的重要推手。如今，他把矛头对准了现任首相阿努廷领导的政府。

## 林明达与"夺回泰国"运动

9月7日，林明达在曼谷拍那空路（Baan Phra Athit）召开记者会，联手维权律师尼提通（Nitithorn）与活动人士披猜（Phichai），正式发起名为"夺回泰国"（Take Back Thailand）的运动。9月10日，他率领数百名支持者聚集在以色列驻泰国大使馆外示威，要求政府严查部分以色列游客不尊重泰国法律与习俗的行为。

林明达受访时强调，他的目标不是推翻阿努廷政府，而是要确保：只要民众的诉求得不到回应，政府就"不得安宁"。他把自己这番行动称为"最后使命"，希望无论谁执政，都能为泰国建立起一种永久的制衡力量。"我想动员泰国人民，把对政府不满的人团结起来，向政府施压，促使当局采取行动。我想赋予人们示威游行和质疑政府的工具。"

## 不满从何而来

"夺回泰国"并非凭空而起。林明达刻意把诉求铺得很宽：生活费高涨、腐败、政治干预的指控，以及外国犯罪网络与"灰色资本"的渗透，都被他纳入动员的旗帜之下。据他一方声称，地方公务员考试舞弊涉及约40亿至50亿泰铢（约8亿至10亿元人民币）的利益，调查却只追到高级公务员，未能触及更高层；他还质疑政府迟迟未能消除考克拉东土地争议，并批评曼谷数据中心快速扩张之下泰国是否得到与投入相匹配的收益。

这些指控目前仍属林明达一方之说，并非司法机关的定论。但把它们摆在一起，指向的是一个更古老的叙事：泰国底层民众对"被资本与权贵裹挟"的持续不安。素攀杜斯特民调对1149人的调查显示，69.54%的受访者认为经济与生活成本问题可能推动民众走上街头，68.76%的人则把矛头指向参议院选举勾结案。

## 阿努廷的回应与9月28日的宪政关口

面对林明达的攻势，阿努廷的回应显得克制而防御。他受询时称，这种街头行动对泰国人民的伤害，可能大于对政府的伤害；并强调政府"早已决心每时每刻为民服务"，相信人民仍愿意给他机会、信任他的政府。

真正的火药桶，藏在9月28日。泰国宪法法院将于当天就2026年2月大选选票上印有条形码及二维码是否违宪作出裁决。这一裁决可能影响2月大选本身的法律效力，乃至现任议员的资格，因而备受政坛关注。林明达已计划在未来几周奔赴泰国各地，围绕这一裁决动员支持者向当局施压。换句话说，真正可能点燃大规模集会的，不是林明达一个人的口号，而是法院的一纸判决。

## 街头政治能否重燃

客观地说，林明达此番动作，尚不足以对阿努廷政府构成直接威胁，更不意味着二十年前那种反他信的百万人大示威会卷土重来。素可泰开放大学讲师尤塔蓬就直言，这种"什么都要管"的多议题策略，恐怕不如林明达当年聚焦他信来得有效——涵盖所有议题的风险在于缺乏重点，而他现在抛出的许多议题并不直接影响人们的生计，动员会因此变得困难。

但尤塔蓬也留下一句关键的注脚：如果泰国经济持续恶化、民众生活水平继续下滑，林明达眼下的行动，就可能成为引爆更广泛街头政治运动的催化剂。这并非空穴来风。过去两年里，泰国经历了"两年换三总理"的怪圈：2023年8月赛塔上任，2024年8月被宪法法院解职，佩通坦接任后又于2025年7月被暂停、8月底遭解职，阿努廷才在国会投票中胜出。政治的高频更迭，本身就是不满的温床。

## 华裔身影与泰国政治的深层结构

一个耐人寻味的细节是，林明达本人是海南裔华人，其父曾就读于黄埔军校。在泰国，像他这样身兼传媒大亨与政治操盘手的华裔并不少见——华人在泰国经济与舆论场中的分量，远超过其人口占比。这也提醒我们，泰国的街头政治从来不只是"红黄"颜色的更迭，其背后是资本、宗族、军方与王室之间反复重组的隐性联盟。

林明达能否真的"夺回"什么，仍是未知数。但可以确定的是：在经历数年相对平静之后，泰国街头的那股暗流，正重新涌动。

## 延伸阅读

**黄衫军（Yellow Shirts）**：泰国保皇派反他信群众运动（人民民主联盟，PAD），2006年与2008年两度大规模走上街头，曾占领曼谷两座机场，重创旅游与经济。

**宪法法院（Constitutional Court of Thailand）**：泰国政治中屡次通过裁决解散政党、罢免总理的关键机构，被视为"裁判政治"的核心枢纽。

**他信（Thaksin Shinawatra）**：泰国前总理，林明达昔日盟友、后来主要政敌；其家族势力深刻塑造了近二十年的泰国政治周期。

# Yellow Shirts veteran Sondhi returns to the streets: will Thai street politics reignite?

Sondhi Limthongkul, 78, is a Thai media tycoon and the founder of the royalist "Yellow Shirts" movement (the People's Alliance for Democracy). Two decades ago, the mass demonstrations he led paralysed Bangkok's airports and helped bring down the then-Thaksin government. Today he has trained his sights on the government of Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul.

## Sondhi and the "Take Back Thailand" movement

On 7 September, Sondhi held a press conference at Baan Phra Athit in Bangkok alongside activist-lawyer Nitithorn and campaigner Phichai, formally launching a movement called "Take Back Thailand." On 10 September he led several hundred supporters in a demonstration outside the Israeli embassy in Bangkok, demanding that the government crack down on Israeli tourists he accused of disrespecting Thai law and customs.

In interviews Sondhi stresses that his aim is not to topple the Anutin government but to make sure that, as long as public grievances go unaddressed, "the government will have no peace." He calls this his "final mission": to build, regardless of who holds power, a permanent counterweight in Thai politics. "I want to mobilise the Thai people, unite those dissatisfied with the government, and pressure the authorities to act," he said. "I want to give people the tools to demonstrate and to question the government."

## Where the grievances come from

"Take Back Thailand" did not appear from nowhere. Sondhi has deliberately cast his net wide, folding the high cost of living, corruption, allegations of political interference, and the infiltration of foreign criminal networks and "grey capital" into a single mobilising banner. By his own account, fraud in local civil-service examinations involves some 40–50 billion baht (about US$1.2–1.5 billion) in vested interests, yet the investigation has stopped at senior officials without reaching higher levels; he also questions the government's failure to resolve the Khok Kradon land dispute and criticises whether Thailand is getting returns commensurate with the rapid expansion of data-centre projects around Bangkok.

These remain Sondhi's accusations, not judicial findings. Yet bundled together they tap an older narrative: a persistent unease among ordinary Thais that the country is being captured by capital and entrenched elites. A Suphan Dusit poll of 1,149 people found that 69.54 per cent believed economic and cost-of-living pressures could drive people onto the streets, while 68.76 per cent pointed to the Senate-election collusion case.

## Anutin's response and the Sept 28 constitutional flashpoint

Facing Sondhi's offensive, Anutin has responded with restrained defensiveness. He argued that street action of this kind may harm the Thai people more than the government, insisted that his administration is "determined to serve the people every moment," and expressed confidence that the public still trusts his government.

The real powder keg sits on 28 September. Thailand's Constitutional Court is due to rule on whether the barcodes and QR codes printed on ballots in the February 2026 general election are unconstitutional. The verdict could call into question the legal validity of that election and even the eligibility of sitting lawmakers, which is why it has the political class on edge. Sondhi has already announced plans to tour the country in the coming weeks and mobilise supporters around the ruling. In other words, what could truly ignite mass demonstrations is not Sondhi's slogan alone, but a single court judgment.

## Can street politics reignite

To be fair, Sondhi's current move does not yet pose a direct threat to the Anutin government, and it is far from signalling a return of the million-strong anti-Thaksin rallies of two decades ago. Yutthapong, a lecturer at Sukhothai Open University, notes bluntly that this scattershot, multi-issue strategy is likely less effective than Sondhi's earlier laser focus on Thaksin: the risk of covering every topic is a lack of focus, and many of the issues he now raises do not directly touch people's livelihoods, making mobilisation difficult.

But Yutthapong adds a crucial caveat: if Thailand's economy deteriorates and living standards keep sliding, Sondhi's present campaign could become the catalyst for a far broader street movement. This is not far-fetched. Over the past two years Thailand has lived through the strange loop of "three prime ministers in two years": Srettha took office in August 2023, was removed by the Constitutional Court in August 2024, was succeeded by Paetongtarn who was suspended in July 2025 and dismissed at the end of August, only then for Anutin to win the parliamentary vote. Such frenetic churn at the top is itself a breeding ground for discontent.

## An ethnic-Chinese figure and Thailand's deeper structure

A telling detail: Sondhi is himself of Hainanese-Chinese descent, his father having studied at the Whampoa Military Academy. In Thailand, ethnic-Chinese figures who double as media moguls and political kingmakers are not unusual—the Chinese community's weight in the economy and the public square far exceeds its share of the population. This is a reminder that Thai street politics has never been merely a rotation of "red" and "yellow"; behind the colours lie the repeatedly reconfigured, hidden alliances of capital, clan, military and monarchy.

Whether Sondhi can actually "take back" anything remains an open question. What is certain is that, after several years of relative calm, the undercurrent on Thailand's streets is moving once again.

## Further reading

**Yellow Shirts (People's Alliance for Democracy)**: Thailand's royalist anti-Thaksin mass movement, which took to the streets in 2006 and 2008 and once occupied Bangkok's two airports, dealing a heavy blow to tourism and the economy.

**Constitutional Court of Thailand**: The institution that has repeatedly dissolved parties and removed prime ministers through its rulings, widely seen as the pivot of Thailand's "courts-driven politics."

**Thaksin Shinawatra**: Former prime minister, once Sondhi's ally and later his principal rival; his family's influence has shaped two decades of Thailand's political cycle.
