# 坦克坐船出国：云壤港跑通的“最后一公里”

据《南华早报》报道，解放军首次使用071型两栖船坞登陆舰，把96A型主战坦克跨海运抵柬埔寨，在云壤港直接卸上码头。这件事被放进“金龙-2026”联合演习的框架里，官方课目写的是反恐、反海盗与人道救援。课目表和码头画面之间有一道值得追问的落差：一场反恐演习，为什么要把主战坦克运过海？点评一句——演习从来不只是演习，它也是一次流程验收。装船、系固、航渡、靠泊、卸载、公路机动，这条链条第一次在境外完整跑通，才是真正的新信息。

## 码头上的那一刻：坦克从舰艏驶出

据《南华早报》报道，央视画面显示，96A型主战坦克和军用卡车从昆仑山舰的舱内驶出，随后被运上转运车辆。这艘满载排水量约2万吨的071型两栖船坞登陆舰，与054A型导弹护卫舰许昌舰、903A型综合补给舰卧龙湖舰一同抵达云壤港。南部战区发布的照片则显示，中方参演部队来自陆军、海军、空军和联勤保障力量，抵柬后经公路机动前往指定区域。

中柬联合保障和训练中心于2025年4月投入运行，演习就在这个中心开训。这里的关键不是又办了一次联演，而是投送方式的变化。此前解放军出国演习的重装主要靠跨境铁路；071型舰在2015年赴俄参加联合演习时运的是两栖装甲车，那类车辆本就能泛水上岸。这一次运的是不具备泛水能力的主战坦克，靠的是码头跳板和港口承重。从“能运两栖车”到“能把坦克开上外国码头”，差的不是吨位，而是最后那一段对接能力。

## 数字：十五天、近三千人和第八届

据《南华早报》援引《金边邮报》的报道，此次演习投入1626名柬方人员与1314名中方人员，总数比上一年增加700余人。需要说明的是，另一些公开报道把总兵力写成约2000人，口径并不一致；但无论按哪一组数字，规模上升、军种齐全都是共同事实。演习从11月25日持续至12月9日，共15天，是自2016年以来的第八届，地点覆盖西哈努克国际机场、云壤港和王家军联合军官学院。

军事分析师王云飞对《环球时报》表示，这似乎是96A型坦克首次参加“金龙”演习；上一次该型坦克出国，还是俄罗斯“中部-2019”演习，当时靠铁路抵达。把人数和装备清单放在一起看，重点不在“多”，而在“全”：陆海空与联勤同时出场，海上方向有封控、反海盗、临检拿捕，陆上方向有重装协同与实兵演练。这类组合不是为拍照准备的，它对应的是一整套协同流程。

## 为什么走海路：铁路的终点与海运的起点

过去解放军把主战坦克送往境外演习，惯例是铁路跨境。这条路有它自己的地理逻辑：需要连续的陆地通道、兼容的轨距、能承受四十多吨履带车辆的桥梁与限界。它可靠，但终点受制于陆上邻国和沿线基础设施。海运换了一套条件：不再要求沿途国家逐一配合，转而要求出发港能装、目的港能卸、航路上有保障。

据《南华早报》报道，云壤基地预计将在中国寻求绕开海上咽喉、改善进入安达曼海条件的过程中发挥作用。这句话值得仔细读：它说的是“改善进入条件”，不是“替代马六甲海峡”。从泰国湾的云壤出发，要到安达曼海仍需穿越或绕行马来半岛，陆路、运河方案都各有政治与工程成本。把一次卸载说成战略通道已经改道，是过度解读；但把它说成毫无意义，同样忽略了预案的价值。海运坦克的意义在于多了一个可选项，而不是眼下就有了一个替代品。

## 云壤的账：谁出钱升级，谁说了算

据《南华早报》报道，中国被认为资助了云壤海军基地的扩建，这引发外界对解放军使用该设施的疑问；柬方否认相关说法，西方国家尤其是美国则持续表达关切。柬埔寨随后表示向更多国家开放该基地，升级完工后日本和越南的舰艇相继到访，美国海军“辛辛那提”号也在今年1月靠港；在五角大楼政策副部长科尔比8月访问金边之后，美方还释放了希望再次靠泊的信号。与此同时，卫星影像被指显示北京仍保留对部分设施的专属使用权。

这段记载把云壤的真实处境摆得很清楚：它既不是纯粹的“中国基地”，也不是完全中立的公共港口。柬方的策略是把同一处设施的使用权分散给多方，用多次靠港来对冲单一依赖。点评：判断一个港口是否真正开放，看靠泊记录比看外交辞令更有用；但同样要看到，多次靠泊并不自动等于同等准入。对小国而言，把大国竞争转化为基础设施投资是本事，把基础设施投资转化为可持续的自主权，则是另一道更难的题。

## 反恐为什么要主战坦克

演习公布的课目包括反恐、反海盗、人道救援，以及海空与陆空联合行动、伤员救治后送、海上搜救和临检拿捕。问题来了：对付零散武装人员，通常一辆轮式装甲车加一挺机枪就够，为什么要动用主战坦克和120毫米自行迫榴炮？

合理的解释是，演习设定的对手并非只有散兵游勇，而是可能有组织、有装甲装备的力量；或者，它根本就是面向最坏情况的预案演练。反恐是各方都能接受的公开标签，重型装备则服务于另一种情景。这里需要克制：一次联演不等于常驻部署，更不等于作战计划已经落地。它能证明的是协同与保障能力的存在，而不是政治意图已经确定。把“坦克上岸”直接翻译成“中国已在柬埔寨建立重装桥头堡”，是评论里最常见也最省事的错误。

## 区域反应：开放港口与信任赤字

柬埔寨的做法并不难理解：一边接受中国坦克开进云壤，一边接待日本、越南和美国的舰艇。这不该被简单读成选边，也不该被读成没有成本。对周边国家来说，反复的港口访问本身是低成本的信任措施；但当一处深水港同时容纳多个大国的军事存在，透明度和通知机制就不再是技术细节，而是区域安全的必需品。

真正的问题不是云壤“归谁”，而是准入规则能否可预测。如果每次靠港都要靠事后解读卫星图来判断性质，信任赤字就会不断累积。东盟处理此类议题惯用的办法是把敏感议题拆成可操作的小项——提前通报、演训观察、设施用途说明。这些办法慢，也不解决权力对比，但至少能把误判的空间压小。区域国家应当关心的不是把某一方赶出港口，而是让所有使用者都在同一套可读的规则里行动。

## 结论：跑通链条比摆出坦克更重要

把这次演习放回它该在的位置：它验证了一条从本土港口到外国码头、能承载主战坦克的海上投送链条，而不是宣布了一个新基地的诞生。重装备上岸之后还要面对油料、备件、道路承载和维修体系，这些条件既决定了投送的可持续性，也给了东道国实实在在的议价筹码。对北京来说，这是一次战略选项的扩充；对金边来说，是投资与自主之间的再次权衡；对本区域来说，则是一次关于规则的需求提示。

接下来值得盯住的，不是这次卸了几辆坦克，而是这条航线会不会重复出现、演训会不会常态化、第三方靠港会不会同步增加。如果三者同时发生，云壤就不只是一次演习的地点，而会成为观察区域安全秩序的一处窗口。点评收尾：把一次流程验收炒成战争叙事，会看错风险；把它当成普通训练一笔带过，同样会漏掉真正的变化。

## 延伸阅读

**两栖船坞登陆舰（amphibious transport dock）**：设有坞舱与车辆舱的大型水面舰艇，可把坦克、装甲车与登陆艇成建制运往海外，并在条件具备的港口直接卸载重装。

**战略咽喉（strategic chokepoint）**：航道狭窄、难以绕行的海上节点；对其依赖度决定了远洋力量在危机中是否容易被截断。

**港口准入（port access）**：一国军舰使用外国港口的条件与范围，通常涉及通知、靠泊频次、设施专用权等安排，是判断军事存在性质的重要指标。

# Tanks by sea: the last mile China tested at Ream port

According to the *South China Morning Post*, the People's Liberation Army used a Type 071 amphibious transport dock for the first time to carry main battle tanks across the sea to Cambodia and unload them directly onto the pier at Ream port. The deployment was folded into the "Golden Dragon 2026" joint exercise, whose public agenda lists counter-terrorism, anti-piracy and humanitarian rescue. There is a gap worth probing between that agenda and the imagery from the quay: why ship main battle tanks to a counter-terrorism drill? The point to make is blunt: an exercise is never just an exercise. It is also a test of procedure. Loading, lashing, sea transit, berthing, unloading and road movement were run end to end outside China for the first time, and that is the genuinely new information.

## The moment at the pier: tanks roll ashore

According to the SCMP, footage from Chinese state television showed Type 96A main battle tanks and military trucks emerging from the belly of the *Kunlunshan*, a roughly 20,000-tonne Type 071 amphibious transport dock, before being transferred onto transport vehicles. The ship arrived at Ream alongside the Type 054A guided-missile frigate *Xuchang* and the Type 903A replenishment ship *Wulunguhu*. Photos released by the PLA Southern Theatre Command showed Chinese participants drawn from the army, navy, air force and joint logistics support force, moving by road to designated areas after arrival.

The Cambodia-China Joint Support and Training Centre began operating in April 2025, and the drill opened there. The significance lies less in another exercise than in a change of route. Chinese tanks sent abroad for drills have historically travelled by cross-border rail. In 2015, a Type 071 carried Type 05 amphibious vehicles to an exercise in Russia — vehicles designed to swim ashore. This time the cargo was main battle tanks, which cannot, and the transfer depended on a pier ramp and the port's load-bearing capacity. Moving amphibious vehicles is one thing; driving tanks off a ship onto a foreign quay is another. The difference is not tonnage but that final interface.

## The numbers: fifteen days, nearly 3,000 personnel, eighth edition

The SCMP, citing Cambodia's *Phnom Penh Post*, reported 1,626 Cambodian and 1,314 Chinese personnel, more than 700 more in total than last year. Other public accounts put the combined force at about 2,000, so the counts are not consistent; either way, a larger and more complete force is the shared fact. The exercise ran from 25 November to 9 December, lasting fifteen days, the eighth since the series began in 2016. Sites included Sihanouk International Airport, Ream port and the Royal Cambodian Armed Forces' Joint Officers Academy.

Military analyst Wang Yunfei told the *Global Times* it appeared to be the first time the Type 96A had joined a Golden Dragon exercise; the last time the type went abroad was Russia's Centre-2019 drill, reached by rail. Read the headcount alongside the equipment list and the emphasis becomes clear: not "more" but "complete". All four services were present, with maritime blockade, anti-piracy and visit-board-search-seizure at sea and combined arms drills on land. Such a mix is not arranged for photographs. It corresponds to a whole sequence of coordination.

## Why by sea: where rail ends and sealift begins

Sending main battle tanks abroad for exercises has usually meant cross-border rail. That route has its own geography: contiguous friendly territory, compatible gauges, bridges and clearances able to carry a forty-tonne tracked vehicle. It is reliable, but its endpoint is hostage to neighbouring states and their infrastructure. Sealift swaps those conditions for others: no need for every country along a land corridor to cooperate, but a need for a loading port, a discharge port and security along the sea lane.

According to the SCMP, the facility at Ream is expected to matter as Beijing seeks to bypass maritime chokepoints and improve access to the Andaman Sea. That formulation deserves care. It says "improve access", not "replace Malacca". From Ream on the Gulf of Thailand, reaching the Andaman Sea still requires transit around or through the Malay peninsula, and both overland and canal options carry political and engineering costs. To describe one unloading as a strategic route already redirected is overreach; to call it meaningless is equally to miss the value of contingency planning. What sealift provides is an option, not yet a substitute.

## The Ream ledger: who paid, and who decides

The SCMP reports that China is said to have helped finance the expansion of Ream naval base, raising questions about PLA access. Cambodia has denied the claim, while Western governments, especially the United States, have voiced concern. Cambodia subsequently moved to widen access to other countries: Japanese and Vietnamese vessels visited after the upgrade was completed, the USS *Cincinnati* made a port call in January, and Washington signalled interest in further visits after Under Secretary of Defence for Policy Elbridge Colby went to Phnom Penh in August. Satellite imagery, meanwhile, has been read as suggesting Beijing retains exclusive use of certain facilities.

That record puts Ream's actual position plainly. It is neither a purely "Chinese base" nor a fully neutral public port. Phnom Penh's strategy is to distribute usage rights among several parties and hedge dependence with repeated port calls. Here is the comment: to judge whether a port is truly open, look at the berthing record rather than the diplomatic language — but also recognise that repeated visits do not automatically mean equal access. Turning great-power rivalry into infrastructure investment is a skill small states can practise. Turning that investment into durable autonomy is a harder examination.

## Why counter-terrorism needs main battle tanks

The announced agenda covers counter-terrorism, anti-piracy, humanitarian rescue, joint sea-air and land-air operations, casualty treatment and evacuation, maritime search and rescue, and visit-board-search-seizure. So the question insists: scattered armed groups can usually be handled by a wheeled armoured vehicle and a machine gun. Why bring main battle tanks and 120mm self-propelled howitzers?

The reasonable inference is that the scenario is not limited to small irregular groups, but may involve an organised opponent with armoured equipment — or that the drill is simply rehearsing for the worst case. Counter-terrorism is a label everyone can accept; heavy armour serves a different scenario. Restraint is in order here: one joint exercise is not a permanent deployment, still less proof that an operational plan has been activated. What it demonstrates is that coordination and support exist, not that political intent is settled. Translating "tanks ashore" directly into "China has established a heavy armoured bridgehead in Cambodia" is the laziest mistake in commentary on this story.

## Regional reactions: an open port and a credibility deficit

Cambodia's approach is legible: accept Chinese tanks at Ream while receiving Japanese, Vietnamese and American ships. That should neither be read as taking sides nor as cost-free. For neighbouring states, repeated port calls are cheap confidence-building measures. But when one deep-water port accommodates several major powers, notification and transparency stop being technical details and become requirements of regional security.

The real question is not who owns Ream but whether access rules can be predictable. If each visit has to be interpreted after the fact from satellite images, the credibility deficit will keep accumulating. ASEAN's habitual method is to break a sensitive issue into operational items — advance notification, exercise observers, clarification of facility use. The method is slow and cannot alter the balance of power, but it can narrow the space for miscalculation. Regional states should not be asking how to expel one user from the port. They should be asking how to make every user act inside the same legible rules.

## Conclusion: the tested chain matters more than the tanks

Put the exercise where it belongs. It validated a sea-based chain capable of delivering main battle tanks from a home port to a foreign quay; it did not announce a new base. Once heavy armour is ashore it still needs fuel, spares, road capacity and maintenance, conditions that determine sustainability and give the host state real bargaining leverage. For Beijing this is an expansion of strategic options; for Phnom Penh, another round of trading investment against autonomy; for the region, a reminder that rules are in demand.

What to watch is not how many tanks came off the ship, but whether the route is repeated, whether such drills become routine, and whether third-party port calls rise at the same time. If all three happen, Ream becomes more than the site of one exercise — it becomes a window on regional security order. One final observation: inflating a procedure test into a war narrative misreads the risk, but dismissing it as routine training misses the change as well.

## Further reading

**Amphibious transport dock**: a large surface ship with a well deck and vehicle decks that can carry tanks, armoured vehicles and landing craft overseas and discharge heavy equipment directly at a suitable pier.

**Strategic chokepoint**: a narrow, hard-to-bypass maritime node; dependence on it determines how easily distant forces can be cut off in a crisis.

**Port access**: the terms and scope under which a country's warships use a foreign port, usually covering notification, visit frequency and exclusive use of facilities — a key indicator of the nature of a military presence.
