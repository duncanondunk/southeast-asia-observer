# China–ASEAN Bilateral Relations Quantitative Index (Jan–Aug 2026)
**Scoring Standard, Chart Commentary, and Forecast to December 2026**

---

## 1. Scoring Standard (Rating Criteria)

### 1.1 Theoretical basis
This index adapts the **Quantitative Measurement of China's Foreign Relations** framework developed by Professor Yan Xuetong's team at Tsinghua University. Bilateral relations are treated as a continuous variable that can be observed through verifiable public diplomatic behavior and expressed on a single −9 to +9 scale. The approach converts qualitative diplomacy into comparable monthly numbers while remaining transparent and reproducible.

### 1.2 The six-tier scale
The full range is divided into three macro-categories and six graded tiers:

| Macro-category | Tier | Range | Character |
|---------------|------|-------|-----------|
| Hostile (敌对) | Confrontation (对抗) | −9 to −6 | Open rivalry, coercion, severed cooperation |
| Hostile | Tension (紧张) | −6 to −3 | Frequent friction, mutual recrimination |
| Non-aligned (非敌非友) | Discord (不和) | −3 to 0 | Net-negative but below open-rivalry threshold |
| Non-aligned | Ordinary (普通) | 0 to +3 | Neutral, transactional engagement |
| Amicable (友善) | Good (良好) | +3 to +6 | Cooperative, expanding ties |
| Amicable | Friendly (友好) | +6 to +9 | Close partnership, institutionalized alignment |

Boundary convention: a score at exactly −6 reads as "Tension," +3 as "Good," and +6 as "Friendly."

### 1.3 Monthly indexing method
Each month's score is **baseline-anchored**: it begins from the prior month's value and is adjusted by that month's publicly verifiable diplomatic events.

- **Nature weighting** — positive events add, negative events subtract.
- **Tier-of-actor weighting** — head-of-state / government-head engagement (±0.5 to ±0.9) > ministerial & institutionalized dialogue (±0.2 to ±0.5) > director-general / working level (±0.1 to ±0.3) > societal / track-II (±0.1 to ±0.2).
- **Domain weighting** — political and security pacts, landmark economic agreements, and military cooperation carry the largest positive loadings (±0.3 to ±0.6 for signing a flagship deal); maritime confrontation or legal provocation carries the steepest negative loadings (−0.5 to −1.2).
- **Accumulation rule** — multiple events in the same month are summed, with a **hard cap of ±2.0 on the net monthly change** so a single busy month cannot overstate a structural shift.
- **Decay toward baseline** — in months with no significant event, the score drifts back toward the country's structural baseline at roughly 0.1–0.3 points per month.

### 1.4 Structural baselines
Baselines are set by formal relationship status. A Community with Shared Future / Comprehensive Strategic Partnership anchors the upper-amicable band (≈6.0–6.5); a strategic partnership without that framing sits slightly lower (≈5.8–6.2). The Philippines' baseline is set in negative territory by the structural weight of unresolved maritime disputes and its mutual-defense treaty with the United States.

### 1.5 Sources and disclaimer
Scores draw on the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs, China's Mission to ASEAN, Xinhua and People's Daily, national broadcasters, and each country's official releases and major media (Lianhe Zaobao, Reuters, Manila Times, etc.). **The index is an academic, event-based assessment, not official data.** Months lacking verifiable events inherit the structural baseline with minor decay.

## 2. Commentary on the Chart

The visualization is built as a three-panel figure. Panel ① plots all eleven countries on one axis with the six-tier color bands as background, immediately exposing the single outlier that sits alone in the red negative zone. Panel ② zooms into the amicable band (roughly +4.6 to +9.0) where ten countries cluster, annotated with the year's decisive summit moments. Panel ③ isolates the Philippines on its own scale (−7.2 to −3.0) to make its downward trajectory legible without compressing the friendly bloc. Together the three panels tell one story with unusual clarity: **a region almost uniformly amicable with one severe exception.**

**The dominant pattern is bifurcation.** Across the eight months from January to August 2026, ten of the eleven China–ASEAN dyads sit comfortably inside the amicable macro-category, while the Philippines alone declines from "Tension" into "Confrontation." No other country crosses even the +6 friendly threshold's lower edge in the wrong direction; the entire amicable group trades in a band between +5.5 (Timor-Leste) and +8.6 (Cambodia at its June peak). The visual gap between the Philippines line and the nearest friendly country — Singapore at +6.3 — is more than twelve points, a chasm wider than the entire friendly band itself.

**Within the friendly bloc, the mainland Southeast Asia cluster is the engine.** Cambodia, Laos, Vietnam, Myanmar, and Thailand together form the upper tier of the index, each reaching the "Friendly" grade (≥+6). Three peak nodes stand out on the chart. The first is **April**, when Vietnam's Communist Party General Secretary and State President Tô Lâm paid a state visit to China; the Vietnam line jumps to +8.3, its year high, and the joint statement's phrase that bilateral relations were "at their best in history" is reflected quantitatively. The second is **June**, a remarkable month in which the leaders of Cambodia (Hun Sen), Laos (Thongloun), and Myanmar (Min Aung Hlaing) all visited China within weeks; Cambodia's line spikes to +8.6 and Myanmar to +7.6 as Xi Jinping and Senior General Min Aung Hlaing issued a joint declaration on building a China–Myanmar community with a shared future. The third is **July**, when the China–ASEAN Foreign Ministers' Meeting in Manila coincided with Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul's official visit to China; the Thailand line leaps from +6.1 to +7.3 and the multilateral meeting adds a small collective lift across the board.

**The maritime ASEAN economies are friendly but structurally capped.** Malaysia (+7.2), Indonesia (+6.8), Brunei (+6.5), and Singapore (+6.3) all qualify as friendly, yet none reaches the +8 heights of the mainland cluster. Their ceilings are visible on the chart as gentler slopes: Indonesia's line rises only slowly because recurring frictions over Chinese-invested nickel policy and the business environment act as a steady brake; Singapore's May bump from +6.0 to +6.8 (Emeritus Senior Minister Lee Hsien Loong's visit) fades by July as the event's effect decays. These countries balance deep economic interdependence with hedging — maintaining close trade and investment ties with China while preserving security relationships with the United States and Japan — and the index captures that equilibrium as a stable, mid-to-high friendly score rather than a soaring one.

**Timor-Leste is the quiet newcomer.** Having joined ASEAN as the eleventh member in October 2025, its line sits lowest among the amicable group at +5.5, flat and undramatic. This is not weakness but immaturity: the partnership is young, low-volume, and free of major disputes, producing a steady, slowly rising baseline rather than event-driven spikes.

**The Philippines is the chart's defining anomaly.** Its line descends almost monotonically from −3.8 in January to −6.4 in August, crossing from Tension into Confrontation. Three events drive the drop: the April "Balikatan 2026" exercises (over 17,000 troops, with Japanese forces deploying to the Philippines for the first time and a US Tomahawk cruise-missile test on Luzon); the late-July sequence in which Manila lodged repeated reef incursions and, on July 31, submitted a so-called "Scarborough Shoal" baseline chart to the UN while also seeking a 350-nautical-mile extended continental shelf; and the August 1 four-track Chinese response (diplomatic rejection, Southern Theater Command exercises, coast-guard interdiction drills, and a new national-nature-reserve regulation for the shoal). As ASEAN chair for 2026, Manila's confrontational posture toward Beijing simultaneously weakened its own chairmanship's centrality — visible in the chart as the only line moving opposite to the regional current.

**The methodology leaves visible fingerprints.** The decay effect is clearest in Singapore and Thailand: each post-visit peak erodes within one to two months when no follow-up event arrives, exactly as the baseline-anchored rule predicts. The accumulation cap is respected everywhere — Thailand's +1.2 July jump is the largest single-month move and stays under the ±2.0 ceiling. The chart is therefore not only a record of events but a demonstration of the scoring logic working as designed.

**Caveats the reader should hold.** The index measures *public, verifiable* diplomacy; it cannot capture quiet back-channel repair, nor does it weight domestic political volatility (a coup, an election, a scandal) except as it surfaces in bilateral behavior. The August data is cut off on August 6, so late-summer developments are necessarily provisional. And because baselines are set by relationship status, a country cannot fall into "Hostile" (below −9) through ordinary friction alone — the scale is built to flag confrontation, not rupture.

In sum, the chart documents a China–ASEAN relationship that is, with one conspicuous exception, the most uniformly amicable in its modern history: ten partners in the friendly or good band, three summit-driven peaks, and a single dyad — the Philippines — sliding into open confrontation under the weight of maritime disputes and alliance politics.

## 3. Forecast to December 2026

**Method.** Projections extend the baseline-anchored logic: absent major new events, scores drift toward each country's structural baseline; anticipated recurring diplomacy (the annual China–ASEAN Summit, typically convened with the Q4 ASEAN Summit; expected 2+2 and leader-level follow-ups) is assumed to add modest positive loadings; the Philippines is assumed to remain in structural confrontation with only marginal managed de-escalation. Figures are a **central scenario**, not a prediction of specific events.

### 3.2 Reasoning by group

- **Mainland cluster (Cambodia, Laos, Vietnam, Myanmar, Thailand).** All five stay firmly in "Friendly." Cambodia's party-to-party ties and corridor projects keep it the regional ceiling; a Q4 China–ASEAN Summit under any chairmanship typically yields a small collective lift, modeled here as +0.1–0.2 in November–December. Myanmar carries mild downside risk if domestic instability disrupts border cooperation, but the June community declaration anchors a high floor. Thailand's July spike decays toward +6.9, then steadies as the new 2+2 mechanism produces working-level follow-ups.

- **Maritime ASEAN (Malaysia, Indonesia, Brunei, Singapore).** These four hold their mid-friendly band. Malaysia, as 2026 coordinating country, gains a slight chairmanship dividend. Indonesia's nickel-and-investment frictions keep it just under +7.0. Brunei and Singapore remain low-volatility; Singapore edges up as it prepares to assume the 2027 chair.

- **Timor-Leste.** Slow, steady climb continues as post-accession cooperation matures; crosses from "Good" toward the lower edge of "Friendly" by year-end without event-driven spikes.

- **Philippines — central scenario and risks.** The central path assumes a gradual, managed de-escalation: back-channel talks and the Q4 summit context trim the most acute friction, lifting the score from −6.4 toward −5.6 by December while it **remains in Confrontation**. Two risks flank this: (a) **downside** — a fresh maritime standoff or a new legal filing could push the score toward −7.0; (b) **upside** — a surprise leader-level meeting could arrest the decline nearer −6.0. Under any scenario, the Philippines is the only dyad that will not re-enter the amicable band in 2026.

**Bottom line.** The regional picture is set to remain historically amicable through year-end: ten partners holding "Friendly" or "Good," Timor-Leste rising, and the Philippines the lone — and likely persistent — confrontation case, with its trajectory hinging on whether Q4 diplomacy can convert managed tension into at least stabilized rivalry.
